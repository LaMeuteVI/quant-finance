# Chapter 1: Overview of Equity Portfolio Management
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2023, Level III, Reading 15 · Portfolio Management | James Clunie, PhD, CFA, and James Alan Finnegan, CAIA, RMA, CFA

## Core Idea
Equities play four roles in a portfolio (capital appreciation, dividend income, diversification, partial inflation hedge), and a manager's job starts with segmenting the universe, understanding every income source and cost line, and deciding where on the passive-active spectrum to sit. Net-of-cost return is what the client gets, so income enhancement (securities lending, covered calls) and cost control (fees, trading costs, taxes) matter as much as stock selection.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- describe the roles of equities in the overall portfolio
- describe how an equity manager's investment universe can be segmented (size/style, geography, economic activity)
- describe the types of income and costs of owning and managing an equity portfolio and their effect on performance
- describe the benefits of shareholder engagement and the equity manager's role in it
- describe rationales for equity investment across the passive-active spectrum

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four roles of equities**: capital appreciation, dividend income, diversification, inflation hedge.
  - When to use: justifying (or sizing) the equity allocation against an IPS.
  - How: map each role to client objectives. Income-seeker maps to large, established dividend payers; growth/long-horizon maps to earnings-growth names; inflation-worried client maps to firms able to pass through input costs plus commodity producers.
  - Why it works / failure mode: diversification benefit fades in crises (correlations rise); equities as an inflation hedge is mixed and negative in severe inflation, because inflation lags the cycle while equity prices lead it.
- **Three equity-universe segmentations**: (1) size/style, (2) geography, (3) economic activity; real benchmarks combine them (e.g., MSCI World Small Cap Value).
  - When to use: defining a mandate, building a benchmark, organising an analyst team.
  - How: pick the dimension that matches the mandate's return driver (growth theme: size/style; domestic investor going global: geography; sector fund: economic activity).
  - Failure mode: categories drift over time and are defined differently by different providers; large companies span several industries and several countries.
- **Passive-active spectrum (six rationales)**: confidence to outperform, client preference, suitable benchmark, client-specific mandates, risks/costs of active, taxes.
  - When to use: deciding how active a product or mandate should be.
  - How: score each rationale. Efficient, heavily covered segments (large-cap developed) lean passive; narrow benchmarks with few liquid names lean passive; ESG/exclusion mandates lean active; high-turnover taxable accounts lean passive.
- **Income-and-cost ledger**: income = dividends + securities lending + dividend capture + option writing; costs = management + performance + administration + marketing/distribution + trading (explicit + implicit).
- **Shareholder engagement ladder**: dialogue and voting, then proxy voting, then activism (resolutions, letters, media campaigns, proxy contests for board seats).

## Key Concepts
- **Size/style matrix**: 3x3 grid of large/mid/small cap by value/core/growth; style derived from a composite score of P/B, P/E, earnings growth, dividend yield, book value growth.
- **Core vs blend**: Morningstar uses "core" for stocks where neither value nor growth dominates and "blend" for funds holding both styles or mostly core.
- **Production-oriented classification**: groups companies by products made or inputs used (ICB, TRBC, RGS); coal sits in basic materials/mining.
- **Market-oriented classification**: groups by markets served and revenue source (GICS); coal sits in energy; airline in travel and leisure.
- **Securities (stock) lending**: collateralised lending of shares, open-ended, callable on demand; lender earns a fee plus collateral reinvestment income, and receives manufactured dividends.
- **Specials**: stocks in unusually high borrowing demand that carry elevated lending fees.
- **Dividend capture**: buy just before ex-date, hold through it, sell; in theory price falls by the dividend, so gain is income offset by capital loss.
- **Covered call / cash-covered put**: writing a call against held shares / writing a put with the exercise price held in cash; both earn premium, both alter risk (covered call caps upside).
- **Performance (incentive) fee**: share of gains above a threshold, typically 10-20% over a hurdle, "upwards only", usually in hedge funds and long/short portfolios.
- **High-water mark**: highest net-of-fee value reached; no performance fee until value exceeds it, so clients do not pay twice for the same gain.
- **Explicit vs implicit trading costs**: explicit = commissions, taxes, stamp duties, exchange fees; implicit = bid-offer spread, market (price) impact, delay cost (slippage).
- **Predatory trading**: trading ahead of known index additions/deletions; a hidden cost borne by index funds.
- **Free rider problem**: non-engaging holders benefit from price gains produced by another investor's engagement.
- **Empty voting**: borrowing shares only to vote them, with no capital at risk in the voted shares.
- **Closet index**: a fund marketed as active that essentially replicates its benchmark.
- **Key person risk**: dependence of the firm's success on a few "star" managers.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
No new formulas; the working relationships are:

`Lending income ≈ AUM on loan × loan fee + collateral reinvestment spread − programme admin cost`
`Performance fee = p × max(0, ending NAV − max(HWM, beginning NAV × (1 + hurdle)))` (p = fee share; one common structure, check the prospectus)
`Net return ≈ gross return − management fee − performance fee − admin/custody − trading costs (+ lending income)`

| Item | Typical level (per source) |
|---|---|
| Stock-loan fee, developed markets | ~0.2-0.5% annualised |
| Stock-loan fee, emerging large caps | ~1-2% annualised |
| Specials | ~5-15%; extreme cases 25-100%, rarely persistent |
| Performance fee | 10-20% of gains above a threshold (e.g., 8%) |
| S&P 500 dividend yield since 1990 | ~1-3% |

| Segmentation | Dimension | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size/style | Market cap; value/core/growth | Easy risk/return/income control, built-in diversification, benchmarks | Categories drift; definitions differ |
| Geography | Developed / emerging / frontier | Diversifies a home-biased investor | Domicile does not equal revenue exposure; currency risk |
| Economic activity | GICS, ICB, TRBC, RGS | Sector benchmarks, industry diversification | Conglomerates straddle industries |

## Mental Models
- **Net-of-everything view**: active alpha must clear management fee + trading costs + taxes before the client sees it; for efficient, well-covered segments that hurdle is often too high.
- **Liquidity demanders vs providers**: momentum and other trend-following styles demand liquidity (higher trading costs); deep value provides liquidity (lower costs).
- **Lend what you do not need to vote**: index funds lend freely (no view on short pressure); active holders must weigh short-selling pressure and recall shares before key votes, giving up fee income.
- **Segmentation follows the thesis**: choose the segmentation axis that isolates the return driver you are betting on.
- **Engagement is a public good**: expect free-riding; engagement pays for large holders and active managers, much less for small passive funds (costs spread over big AUM is the exception).

## Worked Example
**Securities lending income.** A passive fund holds EUR 2,000m; 15% of assets are on loan at an average 0.30% fee.
Gross fee = 2,000m × 15% × 0.30% = EUR 0.90m = 4.5 bp of fund AUM. Collateral reinvestment and programme costs shift this up or down; dividends on loaned shares are manufactured by the borrower, so no income is lost, but voting rights transfer (recall before votes loses fee income).

**Performance fee with high-water mark.** 15% over a 7% hurdle, starting NAV 100, HWM 100 (fee on gains above max(HWM, start × 1.07)):
- Y1 +14%: end 114.0; threshold 107.0; fee 0.15 × 7.0 = 1.05; NAV 112.95; HWM 112.95.
- Y2 -5%: NAV 107.30; no fee; HWM stays 112.95.
- Y3 +10%: end 118.03; threshold max(112.95, 107.30 × 1.07 = 114.82) = 114.82; fee 0.15 × 3.21 = 0.48; NAV 117.55.
- Y4 +6%: end 124.60 is below 117.55 × 1.07 = 125.78; no fee.
Interpretation: the HWM stops the manager charging again on the Y2 recovery, and the hurdle suppresses fees when the return is below 7%.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating active vs passive as binary**: the real choice is a position on a spectrum, including closet indexers and unconstrained mandates.
- **Assuming equity diversifies in a crisis**: correlations among equities and with other risky assets rise in stress.
- **Using headline domicile as exposure**: a US/European large cap often earns most revenue abroad.
- **Ignoring invisible trading costs**: they are not itemised to the client but are inside reported returns.
- **Chasing specials fees**: very high stock-loan fees rarely persist; do not budget them.
- **Treating dividend capture as free income**: the ex-date price drop (and taxes) can offset it.
- **Engaging with selective disclosure**: private meetings risk receiving material non-public information.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- Classifying funds: oil producers and transporters across many countries = **economic activity** (production-oriented); young fast-growing firms = **small-cap growth**; exclusion of defence = **negative screening**; ESG-best-in-class = **positive screening**; focus on a theme (climate) = **thematic**.
- Performance fee is "upwards only"; the **high-water mark** (not the hurdle) prevents paying twice for the same performance.
- GICS is the market-oriented system; ICB, TRBC, RGS are production-oriented.
- A fee that is a share of appreciation above a threshold with an HWM = performance fee, not management or administrative.
- Securities lending: voting rights go to the borrower; dividends are manufactured; collateral reinvestment carries market/credit/liquidity/operational risk. A statement that lending "misses dividend income" is wrong.
- Covered call: income now, upside capped. Cash-covered put: premium, obligation to buy at strike.
- Conflict of interest in engagement arises when the investee also invests in the manager's fund; the free-rider problem benefits non-engaging holders.
- Adding bonds to an equity fund does not guarantee lower short-term risk (correlations are unstable).
- Passive wins on cost, turnover, taxes; client-specific (ESG/exclusion) mandates and wide benchmarks lean active; narrow country/sector benchmarks lean passive.

## Key Takeaways
1. Justify equities by role (growth, income, diversification, partial inflation hedge) and tie weight to the IPS (risk, return, liquidity, horizon, tax, legal, unique circumstances).
2. Segment the universe along size/style, geography and economic activity, or a combination, according to the mandate.
3. Income sources: dividends, securities lending, dividend capture, covered calls, cash-covered puts; each changes risk or voting rights.
4. Costs: management, performance (with HWM), administration, marketing/distribution, trading (explicit + implicit, undisclosed).
5. Passive funds have lower fees, less turnover, fewer taxes, but face predatory trading around index changes.
6. Shareholder engagement helps information gathering and governance but is costly, risks selective disclosure and conflicts, and invites free-riders.
7. Position on the passive-active spectrum via confidence to outperform, client preference, benchmark suitability, mandate, cost/risk, and taxes.

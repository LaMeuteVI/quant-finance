# Chapter 9: Application of the Code and Standards (Level II)
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2022, Level II, Reading 47 · Ethical and Professional Standards | Case authors: Blasky, McMillan, Mainzer, Lamba, Ingram, Stevens, Harrington (CFA Institute Ethics Cases, 11th-edition Standards)

## Core Idea
Reading 47 is a casebook: eight vignettes where you must spot which Standard is implicated, decide whether conduct is a violation, and, in the last two cases, prescribe corrective action plus a firm policy statement. The skill is pattern recognition: a small set of fact patterns (selective dissemination, undisclosed referral benefits, stricter-law-wins, MNPI, supervisor failure, undisclosed outside compensation) recur with predictable answers. Each case below is a playbook: facts, standards implicated, analysis, correct conduct.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- Evaluate practices, policies and conduct against the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct
- Explain how practices, policies and conduct do or do not violate the Code and Standards

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Framework for Ethical Decision Making (Exhibit 1)**: Identify, Consider, Decide and act, Reflect.
  - *Identify*: relevant facts, stakeholders and duties owed, ethical principles, conflicts of interest.
  - *Consider*: situational influences (pressure, incentives), additional guidance (firm policy, law), alternative actions.
  - *Decide and act*, then *Reflect*: was the outcome as anticipated, and why or why not? Steps need not run in order, but review comes last.
  - Use when a case is ambiguous; it forces you to list stakeholders and conflicts before picking a Standard.
- **Violation-spotting routine for case questions**: (1) list each actor and each act; (2) for each act name the single best-fit Standard; (3) check the stricter-of-law-or-Code rule; (4) check whether disclosure/consent was timely (before the agreement or the act); (5) distinguish the person who acted from the supervisor who failed to prevent; (6) check whether a distractor Standard is a near-miss (see table in Exam Focus).
- **Dissociation ladder (Standard I(A))**: (1) confront the wrongdoer and try to fix the problem; (2) escalate to supervisor, senior management or compliance; (3) if unsuccessful, step away from the activity; (4) in extreme cases leave the firm. Contacting clients directly or leaving first are not the "next step". Failure mode: skipping to resignation leaves clients harmed.
- **Case-answer format for the open-ended cases (Stark, Agarway)**: Violation, Standard, Actions Required, Policy Statement. Policy statements are short, rule-like sentences a firm can apply to all employees.

## Key Concepts
- **Stricter-law rule (I(A))**: where applicable law and the Code differ, follow the stricter. Applicable law is the jurisdiction of the clients (as stipulated in the Banco Libertad case), so a manager with clients in several countries must satisfy the strictest regime among them.
- **Knowing participation (I(A))**: a member who knowingly participates in or assists a violation is responsible for it, and must dissociate.
- **Independence and Objectivity (I(B))**: do not offer, solicit or accept anything that could reasonably be expected to compromise one's own or another's independence. Best practice: pay for commercial travel yourself or through your firm; modest meals and entertainment at an event are generally acceptable.
- **Misconduct (I(D))**: dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or any act reflecting adversely on integrity; using confidential material one knows was not meant for you is a form of dishonesty.
- **Material nonpublic information (II(A))**: information that would probably move price or that reasonable investors would want before deciding; materiality depends on substance, specificity and source reliability, not on whether the deal is certain.
- **Market manipulation (II(B))**: transaction-based (e.g., wash trading, simultaneous buying and selling to create artificial activity) or information-based distortion of prices or volume.
- **Fair dealing (III(B))**: fairness is about dissemination of recommendations and taking investment action; clients may receive different service levels if disclosed and open to all, and no client is disadvantaged.
- **Suitability (III(C))**: recommendations must follow each client's objectives, constraints and risk tolerance, not the adviser's confidence or risk of being wrong.
- **Performance presentation (III(D))**: simulated and actual results may both be shown if clearly labelled; prior-firm track records need firm name and the person's specific role, plus the prior employer's permission.
- **Preservation of confidentiality (III(E))**: covers client information; sharing between firms needs the client's prior approval, and need-to-know controls must work in practice (physical layout, screens).
- **Additional compensation (IV(B))**: written consent from the employer is needed for any outside benefit that competes with or could conflict with the employer's interest.
- **Responsibilities of supervisors (IV(C))**: adequate, effective procedures, education and monitoring; a policy that exists on paper but fails in practice is still a violation.
- **Diligence and reasonable basis (V(A))**: includes understanding model results, testing all model components with adequate frequency, and having records and an adequate basis for actions.
- **Communication with clients (V(B))**: disclose investment process and promptly disclose changes that might materially affect it (mandate changes, process changes) to current and prospective clients.
- **Disclosure of conflicts (VI(A)), Priority of transactions (VI(B)), Referral fees (VI(C))**: disclose before the formal agreement; clients' trades come before personal trades.
- **CFA program conduct (VII(A), VII(B))**: do not disclose exam content details or topical coverage; "CFA charterholder" requires program completion plus the required work experience.

## Mental Models
- **"Who decides, who benefits"**: Use when a referral, gift or fee appears. If a benefit flows for a recommendation, it must be disclosed before any agreement, whatever the legal minimum or whether execution quality changes.
- **"Public first, personal service second"**: Use when a favored client gets extra attention. Extra service (calls, invitations, premium products) is fine once information is public or the service is open to all and no one is disadvantaged.
- **"Strictest rule wins"**: Use in multi-jurisdiction cases. List each client country, take the strictest rule on each topic (social media, IM, disclosure to police), and compare it to the Code.
- **"Next rung of the ladder"**: Use when a superior ignores a problem. Escalate (supervisor, compliance, senior management) before resigning; never go straight to clients.
- **"Paper policy vs working control"**: Use for supervisor questions. A confidentiality policy is violated if layout, screens or access let information leak.
- **"Source, substance, specificity"**: Use for MNPI. Insider-sourced facts about a pending transaction are material even if not final.

## Worked Example
**Playbook: the Magadi MNPI chain (Kirabo, analyst, Omondi).**
Facts: PAFF owns 9% of Mtume's. Mtume's CFO privately says the board has received a BWP500 million all-cash offer for a subsidiary and will vote next week. Kirabo tells the analyst, she revises her model, Kirabo orders traders to buy "any and all" shares, then tells Omondi, who buys for personal and family accounts.
- Standards implicated: II(A) Material Nonpublic Information; VI(B) Priority of Transactions (Omondi trading for himself after clients' orders were set in motion); IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors (no information barrier).
- Violation analysis: the tip is nonpublic, specific, from an insider, and about a significant corporate transaction, so it is material even though the vote is pending. The revised model does not cleanse the information: it is derived from MNPI. Both Kirabo (acting and causing others to act) and Omondi (personal trade) violate II(A).
- Correct conduct: stop selling, do not trade or pass the information internally, escalate to compliance for a restricted list, and wait for public announcement; encourage the CFO's company to disclose; document.
Interpretation: pending status is no defense; the tests are materiality and nonpublic status.

**Playbook: BL referral and roadshow.** Referral gifts (tickets and dinners) to law firms are a benefit given for client recommendations: VI(C) requires disclosure to employer, clients and prospects even though no local law requires it. Roadshow invitations for the two largest clients do not disadvantage others because IPO allocation is still offered to all suitable clients. Rule of thumb: ask what the other clients lose.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating "all three countries allow it" as a defense when the Code is stricter (referral fee disclosure).
- Disclosing referral compensation at signing instead of before entering any formal agreement.
- Policy documents without physical or access controls (co-located desks).
- Fixing a known model error "at quarter-end" while masking symptoms (disabling risk factors) and explaining losses with unrelated causes.
- Testing different model components at different frequencies and calling it compliance with the firm's annual guideline.
- Treating a favored client's written consent as a substitute for employer consent.
- Presenting prior-firm performance without naming the firm and the individual's role.
- Applying a flat allocation (3% for everybody) because the recommendation is exciting.
- Believing the confidentiality agreement signed with two firms permits sharing between them.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- Standard-label traps: choose the standard that fits the act, not the one that fits the harm. Telling a favored person about an unreleased recommendation is III(B), not III(E) or V(B). Trading around it is VI(B) (the source text mislabels the Priority of Transactions standard as "V(B)" in Serengeti Q11; correct label is **⚠ Correction**: VI(B)).
- Wash trading is II(B); Priority of Transactions requires the member to be the beneficial owner, so it is the "except" answer in Serengeti Q10.
- Option-style questions: expenses paid by the sponsor. Air travel and lodging should be paid by the firm; meals and entertainment at the event are the tolerated part.
- III(B) is about "fair", not "equal": premium service is permitted if offered to all.
- Magadi Q4: Change 2 needs no disclosure because it stays within the mandate (minimum eight countries). The source says "still within the 80% mandate"; the 80% limit concerns exchange-listed companies, while the number of countries is governed by the eight-country minimum. The conclusion is unchanged. Changes to cash maximum (10% to 15%) and decision process must be disclosed to current and prospective clients.
- Pre-resignation steps: IV(A) permits personal-platform contact after leaving only if allowed by firm policy and no client lists from the employer are used.
- $500 per client paid by the new employer is employer compensation, not a referral fee (VI(C)) and not additional compensation (IV(B)).
- CFA program: even a reference to "ethics questions were hard" or "graders lenient" is fine; specific question content and topical coverage are not.
- Open-ended questions expect: violation, Standard, action, policy statement.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name the single best-fit Standard for each act, then check the supervisor and the participant separately.
2. Law versus Code: follow the stricter; applicable law follows client residence in the case facts.
3. Referral benefits must be disclosed to clients and prospects before any formal agreement; paid-for gifts to referrers also raise I(B).
4. Information distributed unevenly breaks III(B); extra service after public release does not.
5. A supervisor is responsible when controls fail in practice, not just when no policy exists.
6. MNPI materiality turns on source, substance and specificity; a pending board vote does not cleanse it.
7. When your superior will not fix harm, escalate within the firm before dissociating; clients are not the next step.
8. Cases Stark and Agarway show how new asset classes and young firms still trigger old standards: III(B), III(C), IV(B), V(A), VI(A), I(A), I(B).

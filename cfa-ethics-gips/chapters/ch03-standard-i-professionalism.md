# Chapter 3: Standard I, Professionalism (Knowledge of the Law, Independence and Objectivity, Misrepresentation, Misconduct)
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2023, Level III, Reading 32 (Guidance for Standards I-VII; Standards of Practice Handbook, 11th ed.) | CFA Institute

## Core Idea
Standard I makes the individual the guardian of four things: **compliance** (follow the law and the stricter of law or Code), **independence** (judgment unbent by gifts, pressure or compensation), **truthfulness** (no knowing misrepresentation, including omission, plagiarism and misleading performance), and **character** (no dishonesty, fraud or conduct that damages professional standing). The unifying test: would a client relying on this person's statements or recommendations be misled or have their trust abused?

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- Interpret Standards I(A) to I(D) in fact patterns involving professional integrity.
- Distinguish conforming from violating conduct.
- Recommend procedures (firm and individual) that prevent violations.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stricter-rule principle (I(A))**
  - *When to use*: law, regulation, firm policy and the Code and Standards differ, or no law exists.
  - *How*: (1) identify the **applicable law** (the one governing your conduct: where you work, where clients are, what the law says about locality or client home country); (2) compare with the Code and Standards; (3) follow whichever imposes greater restrictions or demands more investor-protective action. Law is the minimal threshold; no law means follow the Code.
  - *Failure mode*: assuming home-country law or CFA disclosure alone suffices when the host country is stricter.

| Residence / business | Applicable law vs. Code | You follow |
|---|---|---|
| Any, law less strict than Code or absent | Code stricter | Code and Standards |
| Any, law stricter than Code | Law stricter | The law |
| Law says "locality of business governs" | Test the local law against the Code | Whichever is stricter |
| Law says "client's home country governs" | Test client-country law against the Code | Whichever is stricter |

- **Dissociation ladder (I(A))**: when you have reasonable grounds to believe client or employer activity is illegal or unethical: (1) raise with the person; (2) escalate to supervisor or compliance; (3) if unresolved, **dissociate** (remove your name from reports, request reassignment, decline or drop a client, in the extreme resign); document the violation. Continued association after knowledge can be treated as participation. Reporting to a regulator is not compelled unless the law requires it, but may be prudent (consult legal and compliance). Relying on counsel's view that regulators could not prove wrongdoing does not excuse compliance.

- **Independence defence checklist (I(B))**: protect the integrity of opinions (unbiased research on corporate clients); restricted list when adverse opinions cannot be published; pay own commercial travel and hotel; token gifts only (consider value limits, per gift or annual); prior approval and prompt disclosure for IPO and private placement investing; supervisory review of personal investing; written independence policy with research reporting lines separated from investment banking and sales; senior compliance officer and reporting channels.

- **Client gifts vs. third-party gifts (I(B))**: gifts from a client already in a compensation relationship are less likely to compromise but **must be disclosed to the employer** (before, or after if not possible) so a supervisor can test for favoritism (compare transaction costs and returns across similar accounts). Gifts from parties seeking influence (brokers, issuers, managers, consultants) that could reasonably compromise judgment are prohibited.

- **Misrepresentation anatomy (I(C))**: an untrue statement or omission, or any misleading statement or impression, in oral, written or electronic form. "Knowingly" means knew or should have known. Covers: credentials, firm services, performance records, investment characteristics, guarantees on volatile investments, benchmarks, valuation shopping, social media, omitted model inputs, cherry-picked composites, plagiarism, misuse of third-party research or ratings.

- **Plagiarism rules**: copying or lightly rewording others' work without attribution; unnamed "leading analysts"; quoting forecasts without their caveats; undocumented charts; copying proprietary spreadsheets or algorithms; also oral communication. Allowed: distributing third-party research while not claiming authorship and while disclosing the source; firm may reuse work product of departed analysts (work belongs to employer), but a member cannot reissue a prior report solely under own name. Cite the **original source**; a media intermediary need not be cited if it adds nothing.

## Key Concepts
- **Applicable law**: the law that governs the member's conduct on the facts. The "more strict" law restricts more or demands more investor protection.
- **Dissociation**: separating from known illegal or unethical activity, documenting it, possibly resigning.
- **Independence and objectivity**: capacity to give opinions unaffected by conflicts, pressure or inducements. Perceived loss counts.
- **Firewall**: separation between research and investment banking (enhanced form: separate reporting, no banker authority over reports, compensation not tied to deals).
- **Restricted list**: security on which the firm issues only factual information because it will not publish adverse opinions.
- **Issuer-paid research**: independent research funded by the subject. Needs flat fee set before writing, not tied to conclusions, full conflict and compensation disclosure; minimum content is financial statement analysis, peer benchmarking, industry analysis.
- **Pay-to-play**: gifts or political contributions to those deciding manager hiring. Soliciting or offering gifts, donations or charity gifts on either side of manager selection breaches I(B).
- **Misrepresentation by omission**: leaving out facts, model inputs or accounts that could change the decision.
- **Cherry picking**: selecting or moving accounts into or out of composites to flatter results.
- **Valuation shopping**: switching pricing vendors for illiquid securities to get higher values (misleads clients and inflates fees).
- **Guarantee rule**: never guarantee a return or capital preservation on volatile investments. Products with structural or institutional guarantees (e.g., insured deposits within limits) may be described accurately.
- **Misconduct**: dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or acts harming professional reputation, integrity or competence (even if legal, e.g., working intoxicated). Lack of due diligence can qualify.
- **Abuse of the PCP**: using Standard I(D) complaints to settle personal or political disputes is addressed by CFA Institute policy.

## Mental Models
- Use **"strictest wins"** whenever jurisdictions or policies overlap: map law of residence, business location and client, then add the Code.
- Use **escalate, document, dissociate** when you spot someone else's violation: silence plus association equals participation.
- Use **"would a reasonable client discount this opinion if they knew?"** to test I(B): inducements, bonuses and boss pressure all fail it.
- Use **"knew or should have known"** to decide I(C) liability: errors found late must be corrected at once, and repeated use of a known-bad document becomes knowing.
- Use **source-chain check** for third-party material: verify, attribute, and never present as own.

## Worked Example
*Gift disclosure triage (reconstructed).* A PM receives (a) from a long-standing client: two sports tickets worth about 700 as a thank-you for outperforming the agreed benchmark; (b) from the broker who executes 40% of her commissions: a weekend trip worth 3,000.
- Inputs: relationships (client already pays fees; broker seeks flow); values; firm gift policy (token limit 100).
- Steps: (a) client gift allowed under I(B) if disclosed to the employer (before or promptly after) so the supervisor can compare her treatment of this account with similar accounts (transaction costs, IPO allocations, returns). (b) The broker gift could reasonably be expected to compromise independence in broker selection and best execution, and it exceeds the policy: decline or return it, document, inform compliance.
- Result: accept and disclose (a); refuse (b). Interpretation: the test is influence over duty to other clients; disclosure cures client gifts, but it does not cure third-party inducements.

## Anti-patterns
- Telling clients an investment is "guaranteed" without structural or institutional backing.
- Reusing a competitor's or colleague's text, charts or models without credit.
- Presenting model output as fact or knowingly omitting inputs.
- Letting banking, sales, a boss or an issuer edit a rating or recommendation.
- Accepting travel on corporate jets or free hotels from the issuer you cover, or letting the same issuer always host you.
- Keeping stale web pages, brochures or fact sheets in circulation.
- Using one favorable fund or account to represent the firm.
- Staying silent while continuing to work on a deal you know is tainted.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- When law is stricter, follow the law; when the Code is stricter or law is silent, follow the Code. CFA disclosure alone does not satisfy a stricter local ban.
- Reliance on legal advice does not excuse a violation; reporting to regulators is generally voluntary unless the law requires it, but dissociation is mandatory when you know of violations.
- Gifts from **clients**: allowed with employer disclosure. Gifts from **brokers, issuers, managers, consultants**: prohibited if they could reasonably compromise independence. Modest, token items acceptable.
- Compensation for issuer-paid research: **flat fee set up front**, not performance-linked.
- "Knowingly" includes "should have known"; an unintentional error corrected promptly is not a violation, an uncorrected one becomes one.
- Plagiarism covers oral statements and unattributed ideas; credit the original source, not a media conduit; reusing departed analysts' work inside the firm is fine.
- Guarantees: prohibited on volatile investments; not prohibited for products with built-in guarantees.
- Benchmark not always required, but comparability is; explain reference indices that do not reflect the opportunity set.
- I(D) covers conduct that harms professional reputation or competence, mainly professional-life dishonesty; civil disobedience and most personal acts do not trigger it. Work-impairing behavior and fraud do.
- Research reports on a corporate client must reflect the analyst's unbiased opinion; commit to coverage, never to a favorable rating.

## Key Takeaways
1. Know applicable law, follow the stricter of law and the Code, and stay current as rules and technology change.
2. Do not knowingly assist violations; escalate, document and dissociate, with resignation as the last resort.
3. Independence must be real and perceived; control gifts, travel, personal IPO investing and compensation design.
4. Client gifts need disclosure; inducements from parties seeking influence are off-limits.
5. Misrepresentation includes omission, stale information, misleading benchmarks, cherry-picked composites and plagiarism.
6. Never guarantee returns on volatile investments; avoid claiming expertise or authorship you lack.
7. Misconduct reaches fraud, deceit and professional-capacity impairment, not personal lifestyle in general.
8. Firms support compliance with codes, written procedures, firewalls, reference checks and clear reporting channels.

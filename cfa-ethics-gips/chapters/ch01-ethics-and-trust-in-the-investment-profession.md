# Chapter 1: Ethics and Trust in the Investment Profession
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2020, Level I, Ethical and Professional Standards, Reading 1 (CFA Institute authors)

## Core Idea
Investment management runs on trust: clients cannot easily verify advice, fees or conflicts, so the industry depends on professionals who put client interests and market integrity ahead of their own. **Ethics** is a set of moral principles that guide conduct by balancing self-interest against the direct and indirect effects on others. The law is a floor, not a ceiling. Good people still fail because of **overconfidence**, **situational influences** and **short-term focus**, so a structured **ethical decision-making framework** plus a code of ethics is the working tool for avoiding lapses.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- Explain ethics; describe the role of a code of ethics in defining a profession.
- Describe professions and how they establish trust.
- Describe the need for high ethical standards in investment management and explain professionalism in it.
- Identify challenges to ethical behavior.
- Distinguish ethical from legal standards.
- Describe a framework for ethical decision making.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ethical Decision-Making Framework (Exhibit 2: Identify, Consider, Decide and act, Reflect)**
  - *When to use*: any decision that affects several stakeholders or in which duties conflict (e.g., duty to a client vs. employer, research vs. confidentiality).
  - *How*:
    1. **Identify**: relevant facts (separate facts from opinions, judgements and biases), stakeholders and the duties owed to each, applicable ethical principles and legal requirements, conflicts of interest and conflicting duties.
    2. **Consider**: situational influences and personal behavioral biases (bonus, boss, prestige, loyalty), additional guidance (firm code and policies, compliance, mentor, outside counsel, CFA Code and Standards), alternative actions.
    3. **Decide and act**.
    4. **Reflect**: was the outcome as expected, and if not, which fact, stakeholder, conflict or bias was missed? Reflect repeatedly as consequences emerge.
  - *Why it works*: it forces a wider, longer-term view than the self-focused short-term one, and it lets you explain and justify the action to stakeholders afterwards.
  - *Failure mode*: it is iterative and gives no guarantee. Cases can end with no fully acceptable option. Take advice from someone outside your own incentive structure.

- **Challenges to ethical conduct (three traps)**
  1. **Overconfidence bias**: people believe their ethics are above average and over-weight internal traits ("I am honest") over circumstances.
  2. **Situational influences**: external factors (money, prestige, loyalty to boss or colleagues, peer behaviour, culture) that shape behaviour. Even good people conform. Bystander effect: less intervention when others are present. Situations can also improve behaviour (being watched).
  3. **Short-term focus**: immediate rewards are easier to see than long-term integrity or market-trust costs, so the brain discounts the latter.
  - Counter-measure: deliberately consider long-term consequences for all stakeholders.

- **How professions build trust (ten common characteristics)**: normalise practitioner behavior (codes and standards complement regulation); provide service to society (advocate for higher standards); client focus (fiduciary duty); high entry standards; body of expert knowledge; continuing education; monitoring of conduct and sanctions; collegiality; recognised oversight bodies; engagement of members (volunteering, mentoring).

- **Compliance approach vs. ethical approach**: a rules-only culture can become one more situational influence (box-ticking). The compliance question is "What can I do?"; the ethical question is "What should I do?".

## Key Concepts
- **Ethics**: moral principles and rules of conduct that guide behaviour (from Greek *ethos*, character). Widely shared principles: honesty, transparency, fairness, diligence, respect for others' rights.
- **Ethical conduct**: behaviour that follows moral principles and balances self-interest with the direct and indirect consequences for others.
- **Stakeholders**: individuals or groups directly or indirectly affected by a decision (clients, colleagues, employer, profession, regulators, market participants, communities).
- **Trust**: a strong belief in the reliability of a person or institution. Investors rank transparent and ethical business practices as the top attributes for earning it.
- **Code of ethics**: a public statement of a community's shared principles and expected behaviour. It does not guarantee ethical conduct.
- **Standards of conduct**: specific required behaviours that serve as the minimally acceptable benchmark and clarify the code. Members may exceed them.
- **Profession**: an occupational community with specific education, expert knowledge, and a practice and behaviour framework that supports public trust. What separates it from craft guilds and trade bodies is the ethical standard and the mission to serve society.
- **Fiduciary duty**: the obligation to deliver a high standard of care (best interest, reasonable care, skill and diligence) when acting for another party.
- **Suitability vs. best-interest standard**: some jurisdictions only require a suitable recommendation (a cheaper, similar product need not be chosen); fiduciary jurisdictions require the best option, ongoing monitoring and client-first conduct.
- **Whistleblowing**: disclosure of dishonest, corrupt or illegal organisational activity. It may breach policy or local law yet be viewed as ethical.
- **Behavioral bias**: a tendency to behave in a not strictly rational way because beliefs and emotions interfere with reasoning.
- **Continuing professional development**: annual learning requirement that keeps competence and ethical awareness current.
- **CFA Institute mission**: lead the profession globally by promoting the highest standards of ethics, education and professional excellence for the benefit of society. Candidates and members follow the strictest of CFA Institute, regulator and employer rules.

## Mental Models
- Use the **legal-vs-ethical test** when a practice is "allowed": ask whether it would still look right to clients and market participants, since legality is a minimum.
- Use **situational audit** whenever a bonus, boss or team loyalty is nudging you: name the influence, then get advice from someone outside it.
- Use **long-horizon check** before acting: list the consequences in 1, 5 and 10 years for you, the client, the employer and market trust.
- Use **"should" not "can"** when a rule seems to permit something questionable.
- Use **stakeholder map plus duty ranking** when duties conflict: client interests generally come first, except that market integrity overrides.

## Worked Example
*Junior analyst and IPO research (Example 5, reconstructed).*
- Inputs: your bank is one of several underwriters of a hot technology IPO; you draft IPO analysis for your supervisor and plan to email the draft, marked confidential, to two industry contacts for feedback.
- Identify: employer, supervisor, issuer client, asset-management clients, market participants are all owed duties. Conflicts: issuer wants a high IPO price while investing clients want a low one; sell-side marketing vs. objective analysis; your bonus tied to deal success. Principles: confidentiality, objectivity, client first, fairness to the market.
- Consider: situational influences are the fees, wanting to impress, prestige. Guidance: firm policy bans distributing written material during an offering and quiet period; the wall-crossing policy limits information to those with a valid need to know.
- Decide: delete the email, ask the senior analyst, then discuss only the general sector by phone without revealing the deal.
- Interpretation: the real analyst skipped the process and sent the email; he was fired, his supervisor was fired for failure to supervise, and the firm was fined. The framework would have caught the policy breach in minutes.

## Anti-patterns
- Believing "I'm a good person, so I'm safe" (overconfidence).
- Treating legal compliance as the end of the analysis.
- Relying on a code of ethics alone, without practising decision skills.
- Consulting only people who share your incentives.
- Reflecting only when something goes wrong.
- Letting loyalty to colleagues or employer override duties to clients.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- Overconfidence is an **internal** bias; money, prestige and loyalty are **situational**. The exam tests this split directly.
- A strong compliance programme does **not** minimise situational influences; considering long-term consequences does.
- Standards of conduct = minimum benchmarks; code of ethics = general guide (do not swap them).
- Trading on MNPI is legal in some places yet unethical per CFA Institute.
- The framework is iterative, not a fixed uniform sequence, and considers more than confirmable facts.
- Where client and market interests conflict, market integrity is the overriding duty; otherwise the client comes first.
- Unethical behaviour in a firm lowers revenues and raises costs (regulatory scrutiny).
- When a dilemma arises, first raise it with a senior person in the firm for independent review.
- Distinguishing professions from trade bodies: ethical standards and a service-to-society mission; fiduciary duty is held by professions.

## Key Takeaways
1. Trust is the asset of the industry; one person's breach harms colleagues, the firm and the whole market.
2. Ethical conduct goes beyond law and must work where rules are silent, vague or conflicting.
3. Character alone does not predict behaviour; situation does. Recognise the situation.
4. Overconfidence, situational pressure and short-termism are the three challenges to name and counter.
5. Use Identify, Consider, Decide and act, Reflect, and seek independent guidance.
6. A code plus standards communicates expectations; only practised decision skills turn them into behaviour.
7. Professions earn trust through entry standards, knowledge, continuing education, discipline and fiduciary client focus.
8. Compliance culture alone can narrow thinking; ask what you should do.

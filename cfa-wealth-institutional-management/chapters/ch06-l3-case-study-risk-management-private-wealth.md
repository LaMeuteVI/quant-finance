# Chapter 6: Case Study in Risk Management: Private Wealth
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2023, Level III, Reading 29 · Portfolio Management (Risk Management) | Giuseppe Ballocchi, PhD, CFA (the Schmitt family, hypothetical Eurozone country "Eurolandia")

## Core Idea
One couple (Paul, a civil-servant teacher with bond-like human capital; Jessica, a startup IT employee with equity-like human capital) is followed from age 28 to 65. At each stage the adviser re-runs the same loop: update the economic balance sheet, list the risks that threaten the goals, size insurance by two methods, fix portfolio problems that correlate with human capital, then redesign the plan around what the household now owns. The exposures change: early career is dominated by human capital and liquidity, mid-career by insurance gaps and concentration, peak accumulation by goals funding (a special-needs son, a bequest), retirement by longevity, inflation and decumulation. Use it as a decision playbook: situation → analysis → decision → why.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- identify and analyze a family's risk exposures in the early career, career development, peak accumulation and early retirement stages
- recommend and justify methods to manage those exposures (insurance, self-insurance, portfolio changes)
- recommend and justify a plan to manage risks to retirement lifestyle goals

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stage loop (four-step process applied repeatedly)**: specify objectives, identify risks, evaluate and select methods, monitor. Re-run after any trigger: house purchase, first child, large pay rise, job change, five to ten years before retirement, relocation abroad.
- **Human capital valuation**: PV of mortality-weighted after-tax earnings at a risk-adjusted rate. Use Paul-type (low volatility, low growth, y = 0%) and Jessica-type (high growth, risk adjustment y = 3%, falling to 1% as income stabilizes) inputs. Marriage diversifies human capital because the two incomes are bond-like and equity-like.
- **Playbook 28 (early career, net wealth only €49,300; human capital €1.87m)**
  - Facts: gross pay €45,600 (Paul) and €24,000 (Jessica), savings €15,000, no debt, expenses €34,800 a year, wish to buy a €270,000 condo.
  - Analysis: risks are unemployment (higher for Jessica; benefit €800 a month is under half her net pay), disability (state €1,500 a month), premature death (funeral, emergency fund, survivor carries full rent), car repair, liability.
  - Decisions: self-insure unemployment with a buffer of at least six months of spending (€17,400); buy supplemental disability cover of €80,000 (Jessica) and €490,000 (Paul), with the option to buy more without underwriting because her pay will rise; life cover on Paul by needs analysis (€414,000 need, buy €400,000); no extra car or health cover (low car use, adequate basic health insurance); advise against the purchase now (it would absorb almost all liquid assets, the house is a mixed personal/investment asset, mortgage rate resets after year 5, running costs about 1% a year, €225 a month).
  - Why: insurance is priced with a load, so buy it for risks that cannot be absorbed (disability, death), retain the rest.
- **Playbook 28b (after they buy anyway: €285,000 cost, €200,000 mortgage, €80,000 family loan, €5,000 own funds)**: recompute life cover with the mortgage and the family loan repaid at death (local custom), emergency fund doubled to €30,000, survivor costs €19,000 a year. Result: Paul's cover needs €443,000 (existing €400,000, so raise it); insure the property for its full value (€280,000) with contents and liability, not just the €200,000 mortgage; build an instant-access cushion; plan a contingency borrowing route (house equity); fixed-rate mortgage removes near-term rate risk; reassess when a child is expected.
- **Playbook 45 (career development: two children, one with special needs; human capital €1.9m; net wealth €391,000)**
  - Risks: Jessica's job loss, disability and death (children now dependent), concentrated portfolio of ten domestic IT shares (€130,000) correlated with Jessica's human capital and with local property, retirement shortfall (projected retirement income €76,000 gross, about half of current pay; DC pot near €150,000 at 65 buys only €7,500 at a 5% annuity yield), state pension cut risk.
  - Decisions: reset disability cover to €520,000 (Jessica) and €300,000 (Paul, whose state benefit and tenure now cover more); raise life cover to €900,000 (Paul) and €1,000,000 (Jessica), as temporary cover to age 65 bought while healthy; direct new money to low-cost diversified pooled funds rather than IT shares; reject the speculative rental property near Jessica's employer (more correlation, leverage, illiquidity, rate risk); stop adding to the €80,000 instant-access buffer and raise pension contributions (25% state top-up, tax-free growth); consider supplemental health cover while insurable.
  - Why: hold the investment portfolio with the lowest correlation to human capital; once the buffer exceeds need, extra cash is a cost.
- **Playbook 55 (peak accumulation: portfolio €611,400, retirement in 10 years, human capital now €1.15m, net wealth €848,000)**
  - Insurance: required cover is now below existing cover (PV of lost income 10 years out is small); recommend letting cover fall to cut premiums, but note that death would stop saving for the son's future care, so weigh that before cutting.
  - Goals-based sub-portfolios (mean-variance rejected as a single-period tool against multi-period goals): (1) Peter's care: about €500,000 in real terms, probability of success near 100%, horizon about 20 years, invest in long inflation-linked government bonds, switch gradually using the €25,000 a year tax-free gains allowance; (2) Roxane's inheritance: the remaining about €110,000, probability about 60%, horizon over 30 years, global equity funds; (3) retirement lifestyle already funded by pensions.
  - Pension actions: keep contributing to the legal maximum; move Jessica's aggressively managed private plan (assumed 8% return) to a balanced option; do not draw the 25% tax-free lump sum now (no need, it would forfeit tax-free growth, and the option stays open).
  - Risks to retirement income: job loss stops contributions; state pension reform (benefit cut, less likely a higher age); DC investment and inflation risk.
- **Playbook 65 (retirement decision, portfolio €1.51m, expenses €70,000 a year)**
  - Three choices for DC pots: annuity, lump sum, leave invested. An annuity transfers longevity risk and is the only option that answers "do not outlive the assets"; a lump sum keeps flexibility and control but the retiree bears longevity risk; an inflation-indexed annuity pays about 4.5% initial yield instead of 5%, roughly 10% less starting income, in exchange for purchasing-power protection. Annuities add provider counterparty risk and unforecastable tax on payments; a lump sum fixes the tax once. Pre-tax, the annuity wins only if they live beyond roughly age 83 (source figure).
  - Decision: state pensions (€77,150) plus indexed annuities from 75% of Jessica's company plan (€11,800) and 75% of Paul's private plan (€4,600) give €93,600 pre-tax, €72,000 after tax, above the €70,000 need. Take 25% of those two pots tax free (€121,250) and 25% of Jessica's private plan (€43,750); the rest of her private plan (€131,250) stays invested. Cash lump (€165,000) is earmarked for Roxane's deposit (under one year).
  - Portfolio (stated limits: no more than 20% annual loss, liquid instruments, inflation worry, no real estate funds): 35% international equities, 10% domestic equities, 45% inflation-linked bond funds (about €739,000, aimed at Peter's care, PV about €800,000, horizon 10 years), 10% corporate bond funds; phase the switch to limit capital gains tax. A looser drawdown limit would permit more risk assets and a higher expected return.
  - Relocation to a low-tax country: verify tax claims with a local expert, estate and inheritance law, ability to return, currency risk and transfer costs, and who supports Peter.
- **Referral rule**: no single adviser masters risk, insurance, tax, estate and retirement law; know when to hand off (here, inheritance tax and foreign tax specialists).

## Key Concepts
- **Economic balance sheet**: financial assets, accrued pension PVs and human capital against debts and PV of lifetime consumption.
- **Bond-like vs equity-like human capital**: stable civil-servant pay vs volatile, bonus- or equity-linked pay; sets the risk adjustment and the optimal financial portfolio.
- **Self-insurance**: retaining risk with a savings buffer (six months of spending in the case) when insurance is uneconomic.
- **Human life value method**: PV of the insured's after-tax income net of own expenses plus lost employer benefits, grossed up to pre-tax, less existing cover.
- **Needs analysis method**: cash needs plus PV of survivor spending minus survivor income minus capital available.
- **Annuity due with growth**: `PV = PMT × [1 − (1+i')^−n] / i' × (1+i')`, `i' = (1+r)/(1+g) − 1`.
- **Mixed asset**: a home combines consumption and investment features.
- **Goals-based sub-portfolio**: capital assigned to one goal, with its own horizon and required probability of success.
- **Inflation-linked bond sub-portfolio**: matches a real liability; duration mismatch is unavoidable for very long goals.
- **Deferred-start annuity**: models a liability that begins in the future (Peter's external care).
- **Longevity risk**: outliving assets, transferred by annuities.
- **Tax-free lump sum**: 25% of private pension assets available from 55 in the case; one third of the company plan at retirement.
- **Counterparty risk (annuity)**: provider cannot honor payments.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- Human capital: `HC_0 = Σ_{t=1}^{N} p(s_t)·w_1·(1+g)^{t−1} / (1 + r_f + y)^t`, `p(s_t)` = survival probability, `w_1` = first-year after-tax income, `g` = wage growth, `r_f` = nominal risk-free rate, `y` = occupational risk adjustment, `N` = working years. (Case values reconcile to about 97% survival weighting at age 28.)
- Disability cover needed = PV, annuity due over the years to retirement, of (net salary − state benefit), benefit growing 2%, discount 3%.
- Life cover (needs) = cash needs + PV(survivor spending) − PV(survivor income) − capital available. Life cover (HLV) = PV(pre-tax income to replace) − existing cover.
- Pre-tax replacement = after-tax shortfall / (1 − 30%).

## Mental Models
- Use total wealth, not the brokerage statement, to size risk: young households are bond- or equity-heavy through human capital.
- Use insurance for what you cannot retain and retention for what you can; a growing buffer lowers the insurance needed.
- Pick the investment portfolio that hedges the human-capital factor, not one that doubles down on it.
- Use two methods for life cover and treat the gap as the range to choose within.
- Use a separate sub-portfolio per goal when success probabilities differ (about 100% vs about 60%).
- Use annuities for the floor that must not fail and keep the rest liquid for flexibility and bequest.

## Worked Example
**Jessica's disability cover at 28.** Net pay €20,490 − state benefit €18,000 = shortfall €2,490. Benefit grows 2%, discount 3%, so `i' = 1.03/1.02 − 1 = 0.98%`. PV of an annuity due, n = 37: about €77,700, so buy about €80,000. Paul: shortfall €15,670, same method, about €489,000, buy €490,000. Interpretation: the policy tracks earnings, not the benefit.

**Paul's life cover after the house.** Cash needs: funeral and taxes 15,000 + mortgage 200,000 + family loan 80,000 + emergency fund 30,000 = 325,000. Survivor costs €19,000 a year for 62 years, 2% growth, 3% discount: about 888,000; less survivor's income PV 758,000 gives capital needs 130,000. Total 455,000 less vested pension 12,000 = 443,000; existing cover 400,000, so raise by about 43,000. Interpretation: the debt, not income replacement, drives the rise.

**Human life value at 45 (Paul).** After-tax pay 46,510 − 10,000 own expenses + 10,000 lost pension contribution = 46,510; grossed up at 30% = 66,440; 2% growth, 3% discount, 20 years, annuity due: 1,213,000; minus existing 638,000 (private 440,000 + employer 198,000) = 575,000 more cover. Needs analysis gives 366,000. The adviser recommends 900,000 total private cover, between the two methods, weighted to the needs analysis because the family's concern is expenses.

## Anti-patterns
- Buying a home with almost all liquid assets and little insurance in force.
- Paying for coverage the household can self-insure (collision on an old rarely used car).
- Letting the portfolio load up on the employer's sector (stocks, local property) when pay depends on it.
- Holding a large instant-access buffer while pension tax subsidies go unused.
- Insuring the property only for the mortgage amount, not replacement value.
- Using a single-period mean-variance model for multi-horizon family goals.
- Keeping insurance unchanged for decades (the Schmitts' cover was stale at 45 and over-sized at 55).
- Buying a fixed annuity with no inflation protection when inflation is a stated worry.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- Growth in payments is handled by adjusting the rate: `(1 + r)/(1 + g) − 1`, with the calculator in BGN (annuity due) mode. Practice: Josephs at 2.5% discount, 2% growth: i' = 0.4902%; n = 59 gives $1,798,197 and n = 57 gives $1,745,354; needs net of survivor income give $1,034,360 for Ron and $425,692 for Jennifer after $50,000 capital.
- Human life value: Susan $1,700,000 − $200,000 − $270,000 (two times salary at work) = $1,230,000; Robert $394,000 − $300,000 = $94,000.
- Higher pension contributions in the Hunters case: 5% of $190,000 = $9,500, only $6,650 after tax at 70%; benefit is tax-free growth and employer match; still about $30,000 a year to save.
- Chang case: inflation, job loss and poor returns all hit projected retirement income; after-tax income $145,875 beats nominal spending $130,477 ($100,000 × 1.03^9), but a non-indexed annuity should be reconsidered.
- Correlated holdings: a petroleum engineer's microcap energy stocks, a technology worker's IT shares; the answer is diversification away from the human-capital industry.
- Premature death risk rises with young dependents, not with wealth; unemployment risk is not insurable and is self-insured.
- Answer pattern: identify each risk, size it with a number, pick insure / retain / reduce, justify with a reason about the family's own facts.

## Key Takeaways
- Start every analysis from the economic balance sheet; at 28 the Schmitts had €1.87m of human capital and €15,000 of savings.
- Match insurance to risks the household cannot self-insure, and recompute at every life event.
- Size life cover by both methods and read the spread as a range.
- Correlation with human capital is a first-order portfolio risk.
- Use goals-based sub-portfolios when goals have different horizons and required probabilities; fund the near-certain goal with inflation-linked bonds.
- Annuities solve longevity risk; indexed annuities cost about 10% of starting income here.
- Bring in tax, estate and foreign-residency experts before irreversible moves.

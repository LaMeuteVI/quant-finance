# Chapter 19: Alternative Investments - Product and Distribution Innovation
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private (AP Advisor Private n. 12, Jul-Aug 2018), *Alternative Investments: L'importanza nel Wealth Management*, Chapter 5 "Innovazione di prodotto e distributiva" | Massimiliano Saccone, CFA

## Core Idea
Structural illiquidity and complexity are the natural counterpart of the premium, not a "market failure". Still, the closed-end self-liquidating model has limits: illiquidity can bind too hard in down markets, and it distracts the GP, who must also manage fundraising. The industry has answered with two opposite families of solutions - **listed vehicles** (liquidity via the stock exchange) and **disintermediation** (direct investing, deal-by-deal clubs, co-investment) - and now with **packaging for wealth management and defined-contribution channels** (feeder funds, wrappers, auctions, digital platforms). None yet delivers true liquidity at low cost and transparency; the direction is standardization of valuation and distribution.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Model 1 - listed investment vehicles (manager-led)**
  - Idea: invest in funds but trade as listed shares: liquidity plus tax, regulatory and operational simplification, more transparency.
  - Why it disappointed: seen as permanent capital for the manager; value transmission from underlying to price is broken by costs and listed-market dynamics; transparent valuation discourages arbitrage that would close the gap between value and price; weaker alignment of interests, leverage needed to optimize exposure and aggressive diversification worsen it. Result: wide bid-ask spreads and, for almost all listed vehicles, **sizable discounts to NAV**, worse under large trades and market stress.
  - Failure mode to test: liquidity of the share is not liquidity of the underlying; check discount history and volume before using.
- **Listed alternative asset managers (distinct from listed vehicles)**: since the early 2000s firms such as Blackstone, Apollo, Carlyle and KKR listed their own shares to obtain balance-sheet capital to invest as **permanent capital** in their funds. The listed security is a hybrid: its price depends on NAV of fund stakes but is valued as an asset-manager business (fee margins net of costs). Indices and ETFs for listed private equity often mix vehicles and managers under one label - **do not treat them as equivalent exposures**.
- **Model 2 - disintermediation (direct investing)**: large sophisticated investors redirect fees into in-house investment teams. Motives beyond cost: control of investment timing and finer diversification. Limits: organizational complexity, governance and the difficulty of retaining teams make it unscalable.
- **Investment club (deal-by-deal)**: an intermediate step between closed fund and direct investing. The investor can opt in or out of each deal, controlling liquidity and diversification and possibly saving fees. Costs: the investor needs rare skills to analyse unlisted deals, so idiosyncratic selection risk (alpha, which can be negative) rises. For the sponsor, the challenge is to build a pool large enough to avoid abandoning a selected deal, while keeping deal confidentiality.
- **Co-investment**: a fund syndicates a deal between the fund and one or more of its investors. Unlike direct investing, the GP runs the whole process (sourcing, pricing, exit). Benefits: lower average cost and the option to choose. Risks: selection risk (sometimes investors accept GP proposals passively, sometimes they underwrite like direct investors) and **moral hazard** - the GP may push deals much larger than the fund's focus and track record. Conclusion: co-investment is sophisticated and mostly institutional.
- **Feeders, wrappers and auctions (wealth management / DC channels)**: packaging closed funds for less sophisticated investors (private banking, US 401(k) defined-contribution plans) with ancillary liquidity services.
  - **Feeder fund**: a vehicle pooling many small investors into a single commitment to a master fund.
  - **Wrapper**: structure that adds liquidity and administration services around the fund.
  - **Auctions / Nasdaq Private Markets**: standardized mechanisms to build feeder funds and run auctions for stakes so channels needing frequent liquidity and pricing can distribute alternatives.
  - **Digital distribution consolidation**: KKR bought Artivest (digital platform), which had acquired Altegris (advisor and distributor via feeder funds); Blackstone, Morgan Stanley and UBS invested in iCapital Network.
  - Assessment: still too costly and not transparent enough on liquidity, especially for US pension schemes. Other actors are working to standardize and make fund valuation transparent.
- **Choosing a structure (decision map)**:
  1. Need daily liquidity and regulatory simplicity: liquid alternatives under UCITS (ch15, ch16).
  2. Want exposure with intraday liquidity to private-equity economics: listed vehicle, accept NAV discount and volatility; separate listed managers from listed funds.
  3. Sophisticated, institutional, want lower fees: co-investment or club; require capability to underwrite.
  4. Small ticket, wealth channel: feeder funds/wrappers via digital platforms; verify fees, liquidity terms and valuation process.
  5. Need to exit a closed fund: secondary market (ch18).

## Key Concepts
- **Self-liquidating closed fund**: fund that returns capital through exits with no redemptions.
- **Permanent capital**: capital not subject to redemption, here the manager's balance-sheet or a listed vehicle's equity base.
- **Listed investment vehicle**: listed company or trust holding fund interests; trades at premium or discount to NAV.
- **Listed alternative manager**: publicly traded asset manager (Blackstone, Apollo, Carlyle, KKR).
- **Disintermediation**: investor removes the fund manager layer by building internal teams.
- **Investment club**: deal-by-deal syndication with opt-in or opt-out.
- **Co-investment**: GP-syndicated deal alongside a fund.
- **Moral hazard (co-investment)**: GP incentive to push deals beyond the fund's scope.
- **Feeder fund**: pooled vehicle investing in a master fund.
- **Wrapper**: packaging adding liquidity or administration to an illiquid fund.
- **DC plans**: defined-contribution retirement schemes (401(k)).
- **Idiosyncratic selection risk**: risk specific to chosen deals, which diversification across a fund otherwise reduces.

## Mental Models
- Use "liquidity of the wrapper is not liquidity of the asset" when assessing any listed or feeder vehicle.
- Use the discount to NAV as the price of daily liquidity and as a signal of stress.
- Use "can I underwrite this deal alone?" as the entry test for clubs and co-investment.
- Use a two-line distinction: listed funds trade on NAV, listed managers trade on fee margin.
- Use platform consolidation (distributor acquisitions, bank-backed funding) as a signal that wealth-channel access is expanding but pricing is not yet efficient.

## Worked Example
No numerical example in the source. Decision illustration:
- Inputs: a private bank client with EUR 300,000 to allocate, wants private-equity exposure, needs the option to sell within 12 months.
- Steps: (1) closed fund: ticket below the EUR 500,000 non-professional minimum and no redemption (ch16). (2) Listed private-equity vehicle: liquid but may trade at a discount that widens under stress; (3) feeder via digital platform: accessible but extra fee layer, liquidity partial.
- Result: propose a diversified listed vehicle sized so a 20-30% discount would not breach the client's tolerance, and disclose that the secondary-market route (ch18) would be the only exit from a closed fund.
- Interpretation: the structure choice substitutes one cost (illiquidity) for another (discount, fees); make the trade-off explicit.

## Anti-patterns
- Buying a listed private-equity ETF without checking whether it holds vehicles or managers.
- Expecting a listed vehicle to track NAV closely in stress.
- Joining co-investments passively because the GP is trusted.
- Building in-house direct teams without governance and retention capacity.
- Treating digital platforms as liquidity providers.

## Practitioner Notes
- The chapter's own conclusion: the market is still evolving; feeders, wrappers and auctions remain too costly and opaque for US pension plans.
- Gap between price and NAV is the real return driver for listed vehicles; track it over a full cycle.
- Moral-hazard check for co-investments: compare deal size and strategy with the fund's mandate and track record.
- Cross-reference the Italian survey (ch15): closed-end funds, investment clubs and listed vehicles are the vehicles advisors expect to grow; evergreen was seen as marginal.

## Key Takeaways
1. Illiquidity is the price of the premium, but investors and managers are looking for ways to soften it.
2. Listed vehicles give liquidity but trade at wide discounts and spreads, especially in stress.
3. Listed alternative managers are a different exposure from listed funds; indices often blur them.
4. Direct investing is cost-efficient but not scalable.
5. Deal-by-deal clubs and co-investment trade lower fees and control for higher selection risk and need real skill.
6. Co-investment carries GP moral hazard.
7. Feeder funds, wrappers, auctions and digital platforms extend access to wealth and DC channels but remain costly and opaque.
8. Convergence on standard valuation and transparency is the real enabler.

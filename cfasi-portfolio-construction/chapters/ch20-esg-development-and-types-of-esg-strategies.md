# Chapter 20: ESG Investing - Development Over Time and Types of ESG Funds and Strategies
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private (AP Advisor Private n. 13, Sep-Oct 2018), *Investimenti ESG*, Introduction and Chapter 1 "Lo sviluppo nel tempo e le tipologie di Fondi e strategie ESG" | Anna Gamba, CFA (Olympia Wealth Management; CFA Society Italy board)

## Core Idea
ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) investing grew out of **ethical exclusion** (1928) and has progressively added best-in-class selection, engagement, thematic and impact investing, and finally **full integration** of ESG factors into financial analysis. The Global Sustainable Investment Alliance (GSIA) taxonomy defines seven overlapping strategies; exclusion is still the most widespread in open-end funds because it is easy to implement, while integration and factor-based approaches are growing fastest. Demand is driven by risk management, client interest, fiduciary duty and a shifting risk landscape in which environmental risks now top global risk rankings. Climate change is the anchor theme.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Timeline of the ESG toolbox (supply-side evolution)**
  1. 1928: Pioneer Fund (US), religious investors excluding alcohol, tobacco, gambling: **exclusion**.
  2. Vietnam era and late 1970s: student pressure on university endowments; **Sullivan Principles** for US firms operating in apartheid South Africa.
  3. 1980s: **best-in-class** funds, then **engagement**.
  4. Late 1980s Europe: conferences and forums on responsible finance; 1990s: first sustainability indices; **thematic** and **impact** funds.
  5. Late 1990s: **integration** of ESG factors into financial analysis.
  6. Italy: first "ethical" sub-fund in 1997 (Sanpaolo group asset manager). Reference concept: 1987 Brundtland report (sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations).
- **GSIA seven strategies (use as the classification checklist for any ESG fund)**
  1. **Negative screening / exclusion**: remove sectors, companies or practices on ESG criteria (e.g. MSCI SRI indices exclude weapons, alcohol, tobacco, gambling, adult entertainment, nuclear power, GMOs).
  2. **Positive screening / best-in-class**: pick the best ESG performers within each sector; sector weights stay close to the benchmark.
  3. **Norms-based screening**: screen against international norms (OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, UN agencies, ILO conventions).
  4. **Thematic investing**: concentrate on sustainability themes (clean energy, water, pollution control, sustainable agriculture).
  5. **Impact / community investing**: targeted, often private-market investments seeking measurable positive impact together with financial return (renewables, microfinance, social housing, marginalized communities).
  6. **ESG integration**: systematic and explicit inclusion of ESG factors in the financial analysis.
  7. **Corporate engagement and shareholder action**: use shareholder rights (dialogue with management and boards, resolutions, list voting under ESG guidelines) to change corporate behaviour.
  - Strategies overlap; "multi-strategy" funds combine several. Factor investing treats ESG characteristics as return determinants.
- **Why investors adopt ESG (demand-side drivers)**: CFA Institute 2017 survey: about 73% of respondents consider at least one ESG factor in their process; the top reasons are managing investment risk and client demand; others include ESG as a proxy for management quality, fiduciary duty, identifying opportunities, reputational benefit and regulation. Equities lead integration, then bonds. Retail surveys: Morgan Stanley 2015 found 71% of individual investors interested in sustainable investing (higher among Millennials and women); Nielsen 2014 listed improved risk/return, positive societal and environmental impact, and aligning the portfolio with personal values.
- **Global risk shift (World Economic Forum, Global Risks Report 2018)**: before 2011 economic risks dominated the top-five rankings; since then environmental risks lead. In 2018 extreme weather events ranked first by likelihood and second by impact; natural disasters second and third; failure of climate-change mitigation and adaptation fifth by likelihood and fourth by impact. Social risks (forced migration, water crises) and geopolitical risks (weapons of mass destruction) also rank high, which maps to exclusion of weapons and sometimes nuclear.
- **Climate-change investment case (post COP 21, Paris 2015)**: climate is changing (2017 among the three warmest years; Italy's first half 2018 among the hottest since 1800); main cause is greenhouse-gas concentration (CO2 from fossil fuels, nitrous oxide from fertilizers, methane from landfills and livestock); CO2 persists (about one-third remains after 100 years, one-fifth after 1,000). "Business as usual" implies +4 to 7 degrees C by 2100; Paris targets are below 2 degrees (about 80% emission cut by 2050 versus 1990) with efforts toward 1.5 degrees (net zero).
  - Consequences for investors: up to about 250 million environmental refugees by 2050; higher health costs and pension-system strain; insurers face harder risk estimation, higher premiums or uninsurable risks (so they pay close attention to ESG in their portfolios); an estimated 60-80% of listed companies' coal, oil and gas reserves cannot be burned (stranded-asset risk).
- **Circular economy**: contrast the linear model (take, make, own/dispose) with a regenerative model (Ellen MacArthur Foundation): design for longer life, reuse, sharing (car sharing), recycling and renewable energy; reduces negative externalities and fits mitigation goals.

## Key Concepts
- **ESG**: environmental, social and governance factors.
- **SRI**: socially responsible investment, the Italian-era term for ethical/screened investing.
- **Negative screening / exclusion**: omit sectors or companies by criterion.
- **Best-in-class**: select the highest ESG scorers per sector.
- **Norms-based screening**: exclusion based on breaches of international standards.
- **Thematic investing**: portfolios built around sustainability themes.
- **Impact investing**: intentional, measurable social or environmental impact plus return.
- **ESG integration**: ESG factors enter forecasts and valuation alongside traditional ones.
- **Engagement**: dialogue and voting by shareholders to change behaviour.
- **GSIA**: Global Sustainable Investment Alliance, source of the seven-strategy taxonomy.
- **Sullivan Principles**: late-1970s corporate conduct code for US firms in apartheid South Africa.
- **Stranded assets**: reserves or assets that lose value because they cannot be used under climate constraints.
- **Circular economy**: regenerative resource model replacing take-make-dispose.

## Mental Models
- Use the GSIA seven-strategy list to label any ESG product before reading its marketing.
- Use "exclusion = values/risk filter, integration = valuation input, engagement = change agent" to match method to goal.
- Use the WEF risk ranking to argue that environmental risk is now a mainstream portfolio risk.
- Use stranded-asset thinking when valuing fossil reserves and carbon-intensive sectors.
- Use the circular economy lens to spot business models that benefit from the transition.

## Worked Example
No numerical example. Classification exercise: a fund "excludes tobacco and weapons, then holds the top-quartile ESG scorers in each remaining sector and votes shares per ESG guidelines". It uses exclusion + best-in-class + engagement; it is a multi-strategy fund, not "impact" (no intentionality or impact measurement).

## Anti-patterns
- Equating ESG with exclusion only.
- Assuming a "sustainable" label means impact: impact requires intent and measurement.
- Ignoring that best-in-class keeps polluting sectors in the portfolio by design.
- Treating climate as only a moral theme and not a risk (insurance, stranded assets).
- Reading survey percentages as precise: chart values here were reconstructed from a garbled extraction.

## Practitioner Notes
- Exclusion is the most common strategy in open-end funds partly because institutions like it and it is simple to implement; integration interest is growing.
- Small caps can show a strong innovation tilt in ESG screens (business models, production, products).
- Insurance and pension investors are natural early adopters because climate and health risks hit them directly.

## Key Takeaways
1. ESG has evolved from exclusion (1928) to integration (late 1990s).
2. GSIA lists seven strategies; they overlap and many funds combine them.
3. Exclusion dominates open-end funds, integration is growing fastest.
4. Drivers: risk management, client demand, fiduciary duty, regulation.
5. Environmental risks dominate the 2018 global-risk rankings.
6. Climate risk transmits to investors via insurance, stranded fossil reserves, migration and health costs.
7. Circular economy is the business-model counterpart of mitigation.

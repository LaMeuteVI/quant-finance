# Chapter 23: ESG Investing - Fund Selection and Benchmark Choice
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private (AP Advisor Private n. 13, Sep-Oct 2018), *Investimenti ESG*, Chapter 4 "Selezione e scelta del benchmark per i fondi ESG" | Antonella Puca, CFA, CPA and Andrea Dal Santo, CFA, ARPM (interview with Andrew Parry, Hermes Investment Management)

## Core Idea
Choosing an ESG fund is a five-step process: (1) define the investor's ESG and risk-return objectives, (2) pick the ESG strategy (GSIA list), (3) run due diligence on individual funds, (4) test the **benchmark** (ESG index) and what it reveals, (5) understand the **rating agencies** behind the index. The key trade-off: the more ESG content an index carries, the larger its **tracking error** versus the parent index. ESG indices come in three construction types (exclusion, inclusion, over/underweight) and several families (market, thematic). Because ESG ratings differ across providers, the index choice is also a methodology choice.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Five-step ESG fund selection**
  1. **Objectives and constraints**: ESG integration is often a primary objective next to risk-return; origins are mission/values (e.g. a foundation excluding arms or rights-violating countries) or the belief that high-ESG companies improve absolute and risk-adjusted returns over medium-long horizons. Parry's warning: do not assume ESG automatically adds return; implementation must be effective, and the manager's first job remains to be a good asset manager.
  2. **Strategy choice**: map to the seven GSIA strategies (ch20). Exclusion is most common among open-end funds; integration and factor approaches are growing; many funds are multi-strategy. Check compatibility with the investor's ESG goals (an investor prioritizing E should avoid a fund holding low-E firms because they score well on S and G).
  3. **Fund due diligence** (needs exhaustive ESG documentation and manager track record), key questions: How are exclusions identified? How are engagement and proxy voting done, and how is voting intention decided? How are ESG factors applied - exclusively, or integrated with momentum, volatility, size, value? What is the ESG impact on historical (risk-adjusted) returns? Who runs the fund and with what experience? Is manager compensation consistent with the ESG goals?
  4. **Benchmark analysis**: the index defines the geographic, sector and company universe, measures adherence to ESG goals and the manager's room to manoeuvre (including cash allocations).
  5. **Rating-agency analysis**: understand data sources, KPIs and coverage.
- **Benchmark practice**: most ESG funds sold in Italy reference general market indices; others reference ESG indices (e.g. MSCI World ESG Leaders). For ESG-index benchmarks examine selection rules, how each company's ESG rating is set, and the KPIs that drive construction. Some managers build custom or blended benchmarks (e.g. UBI Pramerica Azionario Etico: 95% ECPI Ethical Index Euro for the equity sleeve, 5% BofA Merrill Lynch Euro Treasury Bill for cash). Active managers (Hermes) may use ESG indices only as reference, since being "paid to deviate".
- **ESG momentum (Hermes Quantitative ESG Dashboard)**: about 30 E, S, G factors from MSCI, Trucost, FactSet, Bloomberg; emphasis on the change in ESG level over time as well as its absolute value - a stock with high but deteriorating scores can be a strong warning.
- **Ex-post versus ex-ante ESG rating of a fund**: agencies can retroactively rate any fund from its holdings (ex-post), including those with no ESG mandate. For a prospective purchase, also assess the **systematic nature** of the fund's ESG process (ex-ante), not just the rating. In Italy ECPI and Standard Ethics offer fund-rating tools.
- **Functions of a benchmark in ESG**: (1) performance measurement via tracking error; (2) peer grouping by asset class, sector, region; (3) product selection signal - lower tracking error against, say, MSCI All Country ESG Leaders implies similar holdings (top ESG rated, controversial sectors removed), and sector weights close to MSCI ACWI. (4) The index describes the sustainable subset of the traditional universe. Limit: strongly active products with proprietary universes deliver less signal. The informational value of the benchmark rises as tracking error falls (and funds can hold off-benchmark securities).
  - Importance highest for index and passive funds (ETFs), a small but fast-growing segment.
- **Types of ESG indices**
  - **Market ESG indices** (e.g. STOXX ESG Impact): broad base, lower tracking error.
  - **Thematic indices** (e.g. NASDAQ OMX US Water, S&P Global Water, about 50 stocks): concentrated on one theme (gender diversity, renewables, water).
  - **Newer optimized indices**: unconstrained or constrained optimization neutralizes region/sector/style differences versus the parent index; but optimization is ex-ante and drifts without rebalancing.
  - Bond and emerging-market sustainability indices are recent; construction needs deeper data and quality may be lower than for equity ESG indices.
  - **Trade-off rule**: more ESG content means more tracking error. A client wanting strongly ESG investment must accept results that deviate more from the starting index.
  - Some indices emphasize one pillar only (e.g. MSCI World Women Leadership targets the social dimension).
- **Three index-construction methods (use singly or combined)**
  1. **Exclusion**: remove sectors, activities or products incompatible with chosen values (weapons, human-rights breaches); composition depends on the thresholds. Drawbacks: if criteria are too tight the index differs widely from the parent in composition, return and tracking error; it does not encourage laggards to improve (coal miners face exit barriers; selling the business to a private firm leaves the harm in the system but improves the exposed fund's rating).
  2. **Inclusion**: include the most sustainable firms regardless of sector, so a sector laggard could enter if best in its peer group. Needs deeper analysis (higher cost, falling with agency competition, standardized sustainability reports and AI that extracts fundamentals). Index tends to be more representative of the whole economy with smaller risk-return gap versus the parent. Rewards best practice but unsuitable where strict exclusions are required.
  3. **Over/underweight**: same constituents as parent index, weights tilted toward higher ESG ratings; sector, region, duration can be neutralized. No exclusions, so unsuitable for mandates with strict exclusion constraints.
- **Client-specific overlays**: when a standard index does not fit, the advisor (through the manager) can add an **active overlay** to remove structurally incompatible instruments or, tactically, issuers whose ratings seem outdated or gamed. Rating gaming example: many indices reward more disclosure, so low-rated firms disclose more and raise scores without becoming more sustainable. For large mandates a bespoke index may be cheaper than constant overlays.
- **Role of rating agencies**: choosing an index means choosing its methodology; each agency has hundreds of quantitative and qualitative variables, different aggregation, different coverage (from Corporate Knights Global 100 to thousands of companies at MSCI ESG Research), so investable universes derived from different agencies may not coincide. Ratings are generally paid, can be aggregated by sector or region and applied to check any portfolio's consistency with its stated style. Index composition drifts with the parent universe and ESG ratings, so ESG-index passive mandates are **semi-passive**.
  - Best practice: use multiple agencies and complement with direct evidence from engagement; keep in-house ESG analysis for private companies and active mandates; the advisor must satisfy independence and objectivity duties and show the checks done.
- **Limits of ESG indices**: usage remains limited, owing to heterogeneity and coverage gaps or cost minimization; it is harder to take large active bets against the index in ESG turnarounds (Volkswagen cited). But no ESG index makes the product harder to understand and its results harder to analyse, and without strong internal analysis it creates discretion and mandate-breach risk.

## Key Concepts
- **ESG momentum**: direction of change of ESG scores, treated as a signal.
- **Tracking error**: volatility of the return difference between fund and benchmark.
- **ESG Leaders index**: parent index filtered to top ESG-rated firms and controversy exclusions.
- **Controversial sectors** (as MSCI definition): alcohol, tobacco, civil and military weapons, nuclear power, adult entertainment, GMOs, gambling.
- **Semi-passive**: index mandate whose holdings still change with ratings and universe.
- **Overlay**: customization layer removing issuers from an existing ESG index.
- **Blended benchmark**: weighted combination of equity and cash indices.
- **Ex-post rating**: retroactive ESG rating of a fund based on holdings.
- **Rating agency**: provider of ESG scores and analytics (MSCI ESG, Sustainalytics, Vigeo, ECPI, RobecoSAM and others).

## Mental Models
- Use the tracking-error-versus-ESG-content trade-off to set client expectations before selecting an index.
- Use exclusion when mandate or values require absolute prohibitions; use inclusion to reward best practice; use over/underweight to stay close to the benchmark.
- Use more than one rating source; check overlap of holdings across agencies.
- Use ESG momentum as an early-warning screen.
- Use overlay rather than a new index when only a few names conflict with client values.

## Worked Example
No numerical example in the source. Selection walk-through: a foundation wants "no weapons, strong environmental credentials, equity Europe, low tracking error".
- Step 1-2: values constraint (exclusion) plus environmental preference (inclusion with E focus).
- Step 3-4: favour a fund benchmarked to an ESG Leaders-type market index (low tracking error, sector close to parent) over a focused thematic index; check that exclusion list covers weapons.
- Step 5: test that the fund's holdings are also rated well by a second agency; verify E scores are high, not only S and G.
- Result: shortlisted funds meet constraint and tracking-error appetite; reject any fund where the E score is low but S/G offsets it.

## Anti-patterns
- Assuming ESG automatically adds return.
- Picking an ESG index without reading its methodology and ratings source.
- Relying on one rating agency and ignoring engagement evidence.
- Using over/underweight indices for mandates with strict exclusion constraints.
- Treating ESG index mandates as fully passive.
- Over-tight exclusion that creates unintended sector or style bets.

## Practitioner Notes
- Document the independence checks on agency ratings; the advisor's duty of objectivity requires it.
- Exclusion alone leaves engagement potential unused (laggards cannot easily exit sectors).
- When ratings reward disclosure volume, review whether score rises reflect real change.
- Hedge: for private-equity ESG funds, see AIFI and Forum per la Finanza Sostenibile guide (2015), not covered here.

## Key Takeaways
1. Selection runs from objectives to strategy, due diligence, benchmark and rating agency.
2. Benchmark choice defines universe, risk and measurement of ESG adherence.
3. More ESG content means more tracking error.
4. Exclusion, inclusion and over/underweight serve different mandates.
5. Ratings differ by agency, so index choice is a methodology choice.
6. ESG momentum adds information beyond ESG levels.
7. ESG-index passive mandates are semi-passive and need monitoring.
8. Internal analysis is indispensable for private companies and active mandates.

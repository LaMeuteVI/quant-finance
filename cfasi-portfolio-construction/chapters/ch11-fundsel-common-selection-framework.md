# Chapter 11: Selecting an Investment Manager: Four-Phase Framework Common to Active and Passive (La selezione di un investment manager)
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy x Advisor Private, "ETF & Fund Selection" guide (Advisor Private no. 16, 2019), Chapter 3 | Francesco Paganelli, CFA

## Core Idea
With over 35,000 funds sold in Europe and more than 3,000 European-domiciled ETFs (Morningstar Direct, Feb 2019), choosing the instrument that delivers a given market exposure is a decision that deserves a rigorous process even for niche segments, since differences in cost, structure and process lead to very different risk and return. The chapter proposes a four-phase framework that works for both active and passive: (I) planning, (II) screening, (III) due diligence, (IV) managing the outcome. Costs and investment process are the two common pillars; the active-passive line is a continuum (smart beta, systematic vs discretionary).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Phase I: Planning** (derive from the IPS: "circumstances, objectives, constraints and policies", CFA Level III curriculum): 
  - Objectives: role in the portfolio (long-term strategic, tactical, diversifier), risk measure (volatility, tracking error, failure to meet financial goals).
  - Constraints: cost budget, product availability, regulation (e.g. UCITS), resources and expertise (circle of competence), granularity of the asset allocation.
  - Preferences: ESG, ethical, sustainability criteria (can shrink the candidate list drastically; a tracking-error risk definition may exclude high-conviction managers from the start).
  - Check the odds of picking a winner in each asset class using the **Morningstar Active/Passive Barometer**: compares all active funds with a basket of passive funds in the same market (not an abstract benchmark: includes fees and costs, and fund mortality from mergers and liquidations). Findings (year-end 2018): success rate of active managers over 10 years is below 25% in over two thirds of European categories (US similar); in the biggest equity categories (global large-cap blend, US large-cap blend, Europe large-cap blend) below one in four over 5 and 10 years. Better odds in small cap and niches where benchmarks are mega-cap dominated (India, Nordic equity). Bond funds: costs weigh heavily, particularly in government bonds; better odds in corporate and global bonds. Success rate table (%, year-end 2018, 1/3/5/10 years): Global EM equity 24.8/20.2/31.7/31.6; Europe large-cap blend 22.1/12.6/17.7/16.6; Europe small-cap 39.7/54.1/63.2/28.6; US large-cap blend 33.3/17.8/21.2/9.5; Global large-cap blend 22.7/15.8/14.1/9.3; EUR diversified bond 36.9/17.4/19.6/18.5; EUR government bond 11.8/19.7/23.3/16.7; EUR corporate bond 20.3/56.8/45.6/59.5. Lessons: finding a good fund is difficult and varies by category; limiting costs raises the odds. Passive selection is lighter (cap-weighted differences marginal); active selection needs resources, so many investors run a hybrid, concentrating manager research where value-add potential is highest.
- **Phase II: Screening** (reduce the universe to a short list with quantitative data), three families of data:
  - Operational: costs, AUM, flows, management company, inception, manager name, benchmark.
  - Performance: absolute and relative returns, risk metrics, risk-adjusted returns, correlation, advanced methods such as return-based style analysis (RBSA).
  - Portfolio: sector/geography, style box/factor exposure, turnover, active share, concentration.
  - Adapt the filter by asset class: alternatives need the ability to produce returns uncorrelated with main asset classes; active share is relevant only for long-only equity funds. Morningstar's European classification has over 300 categories. The number of risk factors a manager can use (unconstrained multi-asset vs euro government bonds) shapes alpha potential; where alpha potential is low, passive does better and cost differences matter more. Global bond funds illustrate active use: many risk drivers (credit, currency, rates, inflation), few passive options, and concentrated benchmarks (US, Japan governments).
  - Screening can end by excluding an asset class (too few products) and sending you back to phase I.
  - **Limits of screening**: many data are merely descriptive and weakly predictive. The main exception is cost: Russel Kinnel (2016, "Predictive Power of Fees") found fee-based screening improved results in all periods and asset classes; fees are known ex ante and controllable.
- **Phase III: Due diligence**: costs and process are shared pillars. For passive: analyze the replicated index (inclusion rules, diversification, rebalancing) and cost as the main competitive edge; absence of key-person turnover and scalability/repeatability are strengths. For active: qualitative analysis of competitive advantages that should allow outperformance (see ch14). Active-passive is a continuum (Ben Johnson, Morningstar: "true" indices broadly cover a market segment, cap-weighted; anything else is closer to active); the difference is also systematic vs discretionary, driven by technology and data. **Morningstar Analyst Rating** framework, five pillars for all asset classes: **People, Process, Performance, Parent, Price**; active vs passive differ in the weighting of each.
- **Phase IV: Managing the outcome**: expectations, discipline, behavior. Time-weighted return (fund return) vs money-weighted return (investor return) differ: Morningstar "Mind the Gap" (Europe, 2016) shows investor timing destroys value: chasing past performance. Success requires tolerating relatively long underperformance; consistency of performance is a "unicorn" in both passive and active. Practical tools: write down the selection process and investment thesis so you can check later whether an event invalidated it; plan success on avoiding errors and managing emotions. ## Key Concepts
- **IPS-derived criteria**: objectives, constraints, preferences that drive screening.
- **Morningstar Active/Passive Barometer**: success rate of active funds vs passive peers, net of fees, with survivorship correction.
- **Success rate**: percentage of active funds that survived and beat the passive-peer average.
- **Fund mortality**: mergers and closures removing laggards from records.
- **Active share**: proportion of holdings differing from the benchmark.
- **Return-based style analysis (RBSA)**: infers style from return correlation with indices.
- **Circle of competence**: scope within which you can judge managers.
- **Five Ps**: People, Process, Performance, Parent, Price.
- **Time-weighted vs money-weighted return**: fund vs investor outcome.
- **Systematic vs discretionary**: rule-based vs judgment-based management.
- **Noise / value / speculative**: data classification by importance and knowability.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `Investor gap = TWR (fund) - MWR (investor)` measures the cost of flow timing.
- Framework summary:

| Phase | Output | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| I Planning | criteria and odds by asset class | what role and what odds of winning here? |
| II Screening | short list | which candidates pass cost and quantitative filters? |
| III Due diligence | selected manager | do people, process, parent and price support the thesis? |
| IV Outcome | monitoring, discipline | is the thesis intact; are we behaving? |

- Barometer reading: success rate below 25% means passive is the better default for that category.

## Mental Models
- Use the barometer before searching: if the success rate in the category is under 25% over 10 years, justify why your manager is in the top quartile.
- Use cost as the first screen: the only reliably predictive variable.
- Use asset-class-specific filters, not a single scoring grid.
- Use five-pillar scoring with different weights for active vs passive.
- Use a written thesis and review it on events, not on short-term performance.
- Use "value" data (knowable and important) and ignore noise.

## Worked Example
Odds screen. Inputs: Europe large-cap blend equity with 10-year success rate 16.6%; EUR corporate bonds 59.5%; US large-cap blend 9.5%. Steps: rank by odds: EUR corporate (59.5%) > Europe large-cap (16.6%) > US large-cap (9.5%). Decision: for US large-cap, passive exposure (odds under 10%); for EUR corporate bonds, an active shortlist is justified if fees are low; for Europe large-cap, active only for a manager with a clear edge and fees in the bottom quartile. Interpretation: the framework allocates research effort where active has a real chance and otherwise defaults to cheap index exposure.

## Anti-patterns
- Skipping phase I and starting with fund rankings.
- Using one-size screening for alternatives and equity funds.
- Selecting on trailing returns (low predictive power).
- Ignoring survivorship (mortality) when estimating the odds of beating the market.
- Selling after underperformance without checking whether the thesis broke.
- Treating passive selection as trivial: index rules, domicile and costs still differ.

## Practitioner Notes
- Keep a selection memo per fund: role, thesis, kill criteria, expected tracking error and cost.
- Use cost percentile within category as a hard filter, then look at process quality.
- Re-run the barometer annually to update the category odds.
- For ESG constraints, state them before screening to avoid shortlists that violate the policy.
- Money-weighted vs time-weighted gaps flag clients' behavior risk: educate in advance.

## Key Takeaways
- Selection is a four-phase process: plan, screen, due diligence, manage the outcome.
- Planning derives from the IPS and uses category odds to decide where active deserves effort.
- Fewer than one in four active funds beat passive peers over 10 years in most European categories.
- Costs are the one screening variable with consistent predictive power.
- Active vs passive is a continuum; five pillars (people, process, performance, parent, price) apply to both.
- Investor behavior (timing) often destroys more value than manager choice: write down the thesis and keep discipline.

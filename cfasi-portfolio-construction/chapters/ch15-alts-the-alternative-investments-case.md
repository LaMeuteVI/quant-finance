# Chapter 15: Alternative Investments - Why They Matter in Wealth Management
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private (AP Advisor Private n. 12, Jul-Aug 2018), *Alternative Investments: L'importanza nel Wealth Management*, Chapter 1 "Gli investimenti alternativi" + insert on absolute-return funds | Massimiliano Saccone, CFA (XTAL Strategies); round table of Italian wealth-management heads (Mediolanum, Cordusio, Mediobanca, Azimut, Anima, Fideuram)

## Core Idea
After a 30-year bull run in both equities and bonds driven by falling inflation and rates, consensus expected **low returns from traditional asset classes**, and a classic 60/40 diversifier works less well when bond yields are near zero. Advisors therefore look at alternatives for two functions: **decorrelated return** and **a premium for illiquidity** unavailable in public markets. "Alternative" is best defined **by exclusion** (everything outside the traditional long-only fund), and splits in practice into **liquid alternatives (UCITS)**, **hedge funds (AIFMD)** and **illiquid private-market strategies (AIFMD)**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Why traditional portfolios face lower returns (macro drivers of the past 30 years that will not repeat)**
  - When to use: justifying an alternatives allocation to a client or committee.
  - Drivers: (1) developed-market inflation falling from >10% to <2%; (2) the resulting collapse in interest rates; (3) productivity and employment growth; (4) record corporate profit margins; (5) expanding financial leverage. Globalization, technology and financial innovation amplified them.
  - Evidence quoted (McKinsey Global Institute 2016, real returns): US equities 6.5% p.a. and government bonds 1.7% over 1915-2014, versus 7.9% and 5.0% over 1985-2014; Europe 4.9% and 1.6% (1915-2014) versus 7.9% and 5.9% (1985-2014).
  - Failure mode: assuming the last 30 years are the base case. Normalizing rates, slower labor-force growth, weaker productivity and a globalization pull-back all point to lower expected returns.
- **Two-bucket map of the alternatives universe** (Fideuram taxonomy used in the round table)
  1. **UCITS universe**: traditional liquid funds plus **liquid alternatives** (hedge-fund-derived strategies repackaged inside retail-regulated, daily-liquidity vehicles).
  2. **Hedge funds**: structurally outside UCITS (leverage, shorting, concentration), governed by **AIFMD** (Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive).
  3. **Illiquid private-market strategies** (private equity, private debt, real estate): also AIFMD.
  - Practical split used by Mediobanca: *liquid alternatives* move traditional strategies to total-return/hedge-style management inside UCITS; *illiquid alternatives* target returns above what traditional classes now offer.
- **Five-question survey grid (the guide's structured market scan, scored 1-6 now vs five years ahead)**
  1. Role of alternatives: total return, diversification, income, absolute return, impact (ESG/local).
  2. Which investor types: family offices, UHNW/HNW, institutions, retail, pension products.
  3. Which strategies: private equity, real estate, infrastructure, natural resources, hedge funds, liquid alternatives.
  4. Which vehicles: closed-end funds, listed funds, open-end funds, investment clubs, evergreen.
  5. Which growth barriers: education, due diligence, transparency, liquidity, compliance/regulation, operations.
  - Use as a checklist when designing an alternatives offer. The sample is small, so treat results as directional.
- **Low-vol / high-vol alternatives as bond and equity proxies** (Cordusio): use low-volatility alternatives as a proxy for part of the bond sleeve, high-volatility ones as a proxy for part of the equity sleeve. Directional alternatives matter more in sideways or falling markets.
- **Absolute-return fund logic**: objective is low correlation to equities across the full cycle and positive 12-month return, achieved with derivatives; minimum horizon about **three years**. Selection rule: favor products combining a good return/risk ratio with strategies of low correlation to traditional classes.

## Key Concepts
- **Alternative investment (by exclusion)**: any strategy or vehicle that is not a traditional long-only fund; boundaries with traditional products are blurred and keep moving.
- **Liquid alternatives**: hedge-fund-style strategies packaged under UCITS rules, trading daily, more regulated and cheaper than hedge funds.
- **UCITS vs AIFMD**: UCITS is the retail fund framework with leverage and liquidity limits; AIFMD regulates managers of everything else (hedge funds, private equity, private debt, real estate).
- **Private markets**: private equity (buyout, venture capital, growth), private debt, real estate, infrastructure, natural resources; Preqin counted more than $5,000bn of AUM (Sep 2017, +12% y/y), over half in private equity and mostly North America.
- **Hedge-fund industry size**: about $3,547bn of AUM (Preqin, Nov 2017, +9% y/y), more than 5,600 managers and 14,800 active funds; 63% of managers and 74% of assets in the US, 18% and 20% in Europe.
- **Illiquidity premium**: extra return earned by investors able to lock up capital for years in less efficient private markets. Endowments (Yale model) hold most of their assets there.
- **Dry powder / unrealized value**: committed-but-uncalled capital versus value of holdings not yet exited.
- **Side pockets**: segregated accounts in which hard-to-sell hedge-fund positions were parked in 2007-08; their use created forced, unexpected illiquidity for investors.
- **Club deal**: co-investment by a small group of investors in a single deal; seen as a successor to traditional closed-end funds for HNW clients and family offices.
- **Evergreen fund**: closed-end structure with perpetual life; judged a marginal phenomenon by the panel.
- **EuVECA / ELTIF**: European venture-capital and European long-term investment fund regimes meant to open private assets to a wider public.
- **Adoption gap**: alternatives are about 0.2% of Italian households' financial wealth (about $4,500bn of assets, BCG-AIPB 2017) versus 0.9% in Europe, 2.4% in APAC and 3.4% in North America. Reaching the European level would require more than tripling the Italian allocation.
- **Family-office benchmark**: illiquid assets are 30-35% of US family-office portfolios versus under 10% in Italy and the EU (Azimut).
- **Behavioral distortion from QE**: years of bull markets created the impression of "return as a cash machine" (an expected steady ~4% net with no volatility at any horizon).

## Mental Models
- Use **alternatives as a return and diversification substitute** when bond yields cannot fund liabilities and equity valuations are not at a discount.
- Use **liquid alternatives (UCITS)** when the client or mandate needs daily liquidity; use **illiquid private markets** only when the investor has a multi-year horizon and can absorb lock-ups.
- Match **vehicle to underlying**: illiquid assets in closed-end or club structures, liquid strategies in open-end funds. A liquid wrapper on illiquid assets is a run risk.
- Treat **education and transparency as preconditions**, not features: no alternatives sale without the client understanding what drives return, liquidity and fees.
- Use **absolute-return funds on a 3-year horizon** and judge them on correlation to equities, not on the 12-month number alone.

## Worked Example
Inputs: US real equity return 7.9% (1985-2014) versus 6.5% (1915-2014); bonds 5.0% versus 1.7%.
Steps: excess of the recent window over the full century = 1.4 points for equities, 3.3 points for bonds (7.9 - 6.5 = 1.4; 5.0 - 1.7 = 3.3).
Result and reading: bonds are the asset class most flattered by the recent window; a plan that extrapolates 5% real from bonds embeds a rate-decline tailwind that cannot repeat from today's yields. This is the quantitative core of the argument for sourcing return elsewhere.

## Anti-patterns
- Selling alternatives as a single homogeneous class; the guide stresses extreme heterogeneity.
- Expecting hedge funds to protect in downturns because they are "non-directional": several panelists say non-directional strategies proved to carry market risk and the 2007 promise was not met.
- Putting illiquid assets in a service or wrapper that promises daily liquidity (Mediolanum limits portfolio-management use to daily-liquidity funds and, for illiquid ones, to professional clients).
- Assuming size is no issue: top managers are closed to most investors, so accessible funds may deliver disappointing returns.
- Using income-oriented alternatives only because ZIRP removed coupons; the panel expects that rationale to fade as policy normalizes.

## Practitioner Notes
- Round-table consensus: diversification weight falls over time, "other" (impact, ESG, infrastructure) rises; family offices lean to private debt, equity, venture and infrastructure after disappointing hedge-fund experience; pension boards remain reluctant.
- Strategy outlook: private equity and real estate dominate; infrastructure and clean-energy resources up; traditional fossil resources down; hedge funds cannibalized by UCITS liquid alternatives; timber and agriculture niche.
- Vehicle outlook: closed-end funds and investment clubs up, listed vehicles often trade at a discount to NAV, evergreen marginal.
- A EUR 500,000 minimum ticket for non-UCITS alternatives limits diversification for individual investors.
- Tell: when a distributor says "liquidity is no longer a problem" because of the secondary market, verify depth and pricing before relying on it (see ch19).

## Key Takeaways
1. Past bond and equity returns were inflated by disinflation and falling rates; plan with lower expected returns.
2. Define alternatives by exclusion and map them into liquid alternatives, hedge funds and illiquid private markets.
3. Illiquid strategies earn an illiquidity premium but demand long horizons and acceptance of lock-ups.
4. Italy is structurally underweight alternatives (0.2% versus 0.9% Europe, 3.4% North America).
5. Education, transparency and due diligence are the binding constraints on growth, ahead of regulation and operations.
6. Match vehicle structure to the liquidity of the underlying.
7. Absolute-return funds need roughly three years and should be selected on low correlation, not past return.

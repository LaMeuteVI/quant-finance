# Chapter 28: SME and Family-Business Governance - When the "Good Family Father" Is Not Enough
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private (AP Advisor Private n. 13, Sep-Oct 2018), *Investimenti ESG*, Chapter 9 "PMI e governance: quando 'il buon padre di famiglia' non basta" | Patrizia Saviolo, CFA

## Core Idea
Governance (the G of ESG) is usually discussed for listed firms with dispersed shareholders, where the problem is the **agency conflict** between owners and managers. In Italian SMEs and family firms (about 85% of Italian businesses), the conflict is different: the entrepreneur is typically majority shareholder and chief executive, and the real conflicts arise between **family members, generations, majority and minority holders, founder and incoming investors**. With private equity increasingly buying majority and qualified minority stakes (22% of 2017 deals were qualified minorities), governance becomes the pre-condition for attracting capital and executing an exit. The chapter gives a practical governance toolkit: board over sole director, reserved matters, minority rights, tag/drag-along, remuneration rules, family pact and family council, plus lessons from startups (governance as design, advisory boards, founder team stability).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Agency theory (Jensen and Meckling)**: a principal delegates to an agent; opportunistic behaviour cannot be eliminated, so the principal bears **monitoring and incentive costs** and the agent bears **bonding costs**. Roots: Adam Smith (1776) on managers of other people's money; public companies with separation of ownership and control from the early 1900s. OECD definition: procedures and processes by which an organisation is directed and controlled, specifying rights and responsibilities among board, managers, shareholders, other stakeholders and the decision rules. The scope has widened from shareholder protection to broader stakeholders.
- **Why governance matters to SMEs now**: internationalization after the Great Crisis (about 33% of family firms' 2015 turnover from exports, 43% with direct foreign presence, AIDAF 2017); private and family firms were 67% of 2017 deals; 75% of operations were majority acquisitions; qualified minority acquisitions 22% in 2017 (17% in 2016; Private Equity Monitor).
- **Two conflicts in family firms**: (1) `Entrepreneur = majority shareholder = CEO` so the classic shareholder-manager conflict is weak; (2) conflicts among holders with different goals (majority, minorities, founders, new investors). If unregulated, even a strong growth and exit outlook can disappoint. Two recipes: understand the SME's culture and design governance with the entrepreneur; borrow from startups.
- **Culture as obstacle**: family firms resist change because roles are predefined and durable. Deloitte Next Gen Survey 2017: only 39% of future family-business leaders say their executives have the skills for radical change; 16% say the same about employees; family capability assessment is lower still. The Civil Code (art. 230 bis) defines family enterprises: major decisions are taken by majority of participating family members.
- **Listed-company governance gaps (Italian Corporate Governance Code, 2015)**: 90% of listed companies adhered at end-2016 (199 firms), yet at end-2015 over 58% of adherents had no Nomination Committee (over 95% of those gave no substantive explanation), only 29 companies declared succession plans, and in only a quarter of board renewals in 2015-2016 did the outgoing board express guidance on composition. About 60% of listed Italian firms are family-controlled.
- **What investors want (for any firm they back)**: (1) compliance at all levels; (2) clear strategic lines; (3) execution capacity and risk management. Italian PE funds usually invest only where a majority stake is obtainable, because a "one-man show" does not work when the entrepreneur will exit or embodies the culture to be changed; a team, not just a good board, is needed (prepared, motivated managers with real delegations, not constant referral of ordinary matters).
- **Classic family-firm governance model (Figure 3)**: **family pact** and **family council** (the body to settle family conflicts and manage the family-board relationship), family committee (social activities, training, identifying leaders, communication), shareholders' assembly (appoint governing bodies, mission, member information, share transfers, family-company relations), family office (shared services, historical wealth management), family foundation (philanthropy), family holding above the operating companies and venture funds, and the board of directors.
  - **Generational handover**: family pacts (Civil Code arts. 458, 768-bis) identify ex ante one or more suitable successors, transfer them the shares and compensate non-designated heirs; the pact cannot be challenged by other legitimate heirs. Risks: about 1 in 4 leaders is over 70 and firms led by older entrepreneurs show weaker financial performance (AUB observatory); the founder who "does not let go" blocks the next generation; dispersal across many heirs produces shifting alliances or exits.
- **AIDAF Code of Self-Discipline for unlisted family-controlled firms (Oct 2017)**: principle: overcome single-person-in-command and "family in all roles" models. Rules: shareholders' assembly with an information role; board of at least 3 members; board competences (medium-long-term strategy, organizational, administrative and accounting adequacy, non-strategic operations, succession planning for key managers); at least one non-family, ideally independent, director; mix of skills, age and gender; a single managing director; quarterly board meetings at least; chair without management powers; annual risk analysis; related-party procedures; periodic review of articles; independent auditors with rotation; external annual audit suggested. Claimed benefits: growth, lower risk, separation of family and business wealth, calling card for stakeholders, catalyst for outside managers, compass for succession, rehearsal for listing.
  - Core documents are the **articles of association and shareholder agreements**. Minimum content: classification of decisions by competent body and quorum; matters reserved to the assembly; matters needing qualified majorities; criteria to appoint, revoke and renew board and chair/CEO; share-transfer rules (cohesion and exit opportunities). Two common errors: assuming sale equals generational handover; assuming the bank is the only financial counterpart (a creditor does not prioritize governance, an equity investor does). Articles bind erga omnes and are public; shareholder agreements bind only signatories but are private and flexible.
- **Startups as model (governance in the DNA)**: investors' scouting criteria always include governance; term sheets contain clauses protecting founders, majority and minority: pre-emption, approval (gradimento), **tag-along**, **drag-along**, **liquidity preference**, and investor vetoes on extraordinary decisions. Features: (1) founder team usually stays (unlike mature firms where PE often changes the CEO/CFO); (2) strong commitment to minorities (board seats for minority holders; mixed quorum combining capital share and per-head votes); (3) when the startup is founded by several companies, a power game risk; one solution is an external CEO; (4) governance is "already thought out", not "we will think about it"; the **advisory board** (no decision role, high influence, networking) is a startup-born tool. Why SMEs resemble startups: direct personal relation with capital providers, lean organization, international ambitions, intersecting generations.
- **Governance compass for SMEs (summary table)**: prefer a board to a sole director; regulate minority voting rights; define reserved matters with qualified majorities; at least tag-along (and consider drag-along) to manage exit conflicts; define remuneration criteria and use of new capital ex ante; set up periodic reporting to all shareholders and top management, including risk analysis.

## Key Concepts
- **Agency cost**: monitoring, bonding and residual losses from misaligned interests.
- **Principal-agent relationship**: delegation of tasks to someone with different incentives.
- **Corporate governance (OECD)**: system of rights, responsibilities and decision rules.
- **Family pact (patto di famiglia)**: legal instrument for planned succession.
- **Family council**: body that manages family conflict and links family to the board.
- **Reserved matters**: decisions needing qualified majority or specific approval.
- **Tag-along**: minority right to sell on the same terms when the majority sells.
- **Drag-along**: majority right to force minorities to sell to a buyer.
- **Liquidity preference**: investor right to be paid first on exit.
- **Pre-emption (prelazione) / approval (gradimento) rights**: first-offer and veto rights on share transfers.
- **Shareholders' agreement (patto parasociale)**: private contract among holders on governance.
- **Advisory board**: non-executive panel supporting management with expertise and network.
- **Nomination committee**: board committee for appointments.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
No formulas. Reference figures: family firms 85% of Italian businesses; 60% of Italian listed firms family-controlled; PE deals 2017: 67% private/family targets, 75% majority stakes, 22% qualified minorities (17% in 2016); AIDAF code board minimum 3 members, quarterly meetings.

## Mental Models
- Use "who are the real conflicting parties?" before choosing a governance design: shareholders vs managers (listed) or family vs family, majority vs minority (SME).
- Use the board-not-sole-director rule when preparing for external capital.
- Use articles plus shareholders' agreement as the rule book; the agreement is flexible but weaker legally.
- Use tag/drag-along together to align exit incentives.
- Use startups' ex-ante discipline: decide governance before the term sheet.

## Worked Example
No numerical example. Applied checklist: a family manufacturer (founder aged 72, two children as co-owners, a cousin with 10%) wants a PE partner. Steps: (1) form a board of at least 3 including one independent non-family director; (2) write articles with reserved matters and qualified majorities; (3) put a family pact in place for succession and compensate the non-designated heirs; (4) add tag-along and drag-along to avoid blocked sales when cousins disagree; (5) create a family council for conflict and dividend policy; (6) set periodic reporting.
- Result: investors can see rule clarity on control, succession and exit, reducing the deal-breaking risk described in the chapter (a sale dropping at the last moment because of a family dispute).

## Anti-patterns
- One-man show with no real delegation to managers.
- Founder who does not let go while the next generation is formally in charge.
- Assuming sale means passing the firm to children.
- Governance rules only in a private agreement when public articles are needed (or vice versa) without understanding the legal difference.
- Treating governance as a listing-only topic.
- Dispersing shares among many heirs without a pact.

## Practitioner Notes
- For ESG analysis of an SME, governance is often the dominant material factor; assess succession plan, board composition, minority protections and reporting.
- Ask whether the CEO is also the majority owner, and what happens to decision-making if the founder exits.
- The author's evidence is observational (Deloitte, AUB, AIDAF, Private Equity Monitor); treat percentages as context.
- Related-party procedures and risk analysis are investor red-flag checks.

## Key Takeaways
1. SME governance centres on family and shareholder conflicts, not only manager-owner conflicts.
2. Private equity needs control rights and a team beyond the founder.
3. A board of at least three with an independent and a single CEO is the core structure.
4. Articles of association and shareholder agreements are the rule book.
5. Tag-along, drag-along, reserved matters and minority voting rights prevent deadlock at exit.
6. Family pacts, family councils and succession planning address the generational risk.
7. Startups show governance as a deliberate early design with advisory boards.

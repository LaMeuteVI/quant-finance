# Chapter 1: Trading Psychology and Discipline (Part 1 — Emotions)
> **Source**: Patrick Riguet, *Ichimoku Kinko Hyo — La formation totale* (French), Introduction and Part 1 "Les émotions en trading" | retail FX/CFD/index trader-educator

## Core Idea
Riguet argues that an indicator does not make the trader: emotional control and strict money management (ch02) matter more than mastery of Ichimoku. A mediocre chart reader who is disciplined will beat a brilliant analyst who is impulsive. Every later rule in this skill (wait for candle close, respect the filter, refuse trades below 2:1, never move the stop) is a discipline rule first and a chart rule second.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cold execution loop**: analyse → fix stop and take profit → let the trade run without intervening.
  - When to use: every trade, at every time frame.
  - How: (1) find a signal per the plan; (2) place the stop at a technical invalidation level (never a round-number guess); (3) set the target from chart structure; (4) check risk/reward ≥ ~2:1 (ch02); (5) enter at the candle close; (6) do not widen the stop, do not add "hope" reasons to stay.
  - Why it works / failure mode: it removes discretion at the moment emotions peak (price near the stop). Failure: moving the stop "because it will turn" converts a 1% loss into a ruin-path loss.
- **Abstention as a position**: no signal means no trade; staying out for days is part of the job.
  - When to use: when no setup is validated on the higher time frames (ch06) or the risk/reward is below threshold.
  - How: scan many instruments (the author watches about 20–25 at once) not to trade more, but to raise the odds that one valid signal exists; if none, prepare plans (ch09) instead.
- **Emotion audit of a losing trade**: classify each loss as (a) analysis error, (b) stop placement error, (c) emotional violation of the rules, (d) normal loss inside the plan. Only (d) is acceptable to repeat.
  - When to use: after every trade (journal).
  - How: see the GBPCAD case in Worked Example.

## Key Concepts
- **Hope**: the belief that price will return to profitability; in trading it is the main cause of stop-widening and ruin. The author says it must be "killed".
- **Fear**: fear of missing the move (FOMO) makes beginners chase accelerations; fear of loss makes them cut winners. Both are to be removed, not managed.
- **Greed / appetite for risk**: adrenaline-seeking has no place; trading is not casino betting.
- **Impatience**: the most damaging trait; the method needs days of waiting for a valid signal.
- **Mass psychology**: the author's justification for default settings and for support/resistance: traders act en masse on the same visible levels (see ch03).
- **Stop loss (stop)**: a pre-defined exit at the level where the trade idea is invalidated; it also determines position size (ch02).
- **Take profit (TP)**: a structural target fixed before entry, not "wherever it goes".
- **Let gains run, cut losses**: the author's repeated maxim; the stop is cut immediately, winners are trailed (ch08).
- **Discipline over indicator skill**: the author's central claim; not empirically tested in the book (an opinion, but widely shared by practitioners).
- **Trading plan (plan de trading)**: pre-analysed scenarios (bullish and bearish) with trigger, stop and target, executed without opinion (ch09).
- **Conviction bias**: building a directional view from news, macro or politics; the author calls it a major error because nobody knows which way the market will go.

## Mental Models
- **Driving analogy**: beginners try to consciously check gears, mirrors and signs; with practice it becomes automatic. Ichimoku reading (distance from Tenkan, higher-time-frame obstacles, cloud reading) is meant to become a glance, so do not memorise, practise.
- **The market is mass psychology, not rationality**: levels work because many watch them. Use this to choose conventional settings (ch03) and to expect reactions at visible flats.
- **Probabilities, never certainties**: phrase every thesis as "high probability of X unless Y breaks"; this makes stop-outs acceptable.
- **Analyse, then be indifferent to direction**: a good plan has both a long and a short trigger; you act on whichever fires.

## Worked Example
**Case (author's own error, GBPCAD H4)**: analysis was sound (breakout out of an old range, target from higher time frames). The rule says: place the stop a few points under the old range's upper bound and wider still for CAD pairs ("killer wicks"); enter on the breakout only if risk/reward ≥ 2:1, else wait for the pullback. Fear of missing the move led to entry on the breakout with the stop just under Tenkan-sen. Result: stopped out for 48 pips, then price reached the target anyway.
Audit: analysis correct; stop placement wrong (too tight); emotion violated the rule (fear + greed). Lesson in the author's words in substance: Ichimoku was not the cause; the stop and the emotional decision were. He also refuses to loosen the stop after entry because that breaks the pre-agreed ratio (a 5–6 point adjustment at most, exceptionally).

## Anti-patterns
- **Selling or buying accelerations**: chasing a fast move away from Tenkan-sen (ch03) out of fear of missing it.
- **Moving the stop away to "give the trade room"**: destroys the risk/reward on which the edge rests.
- **Forcing a trade every day**: abstention is a valid, frequent outcome.
- **Building conviction from macro news or politics**: replaces probabilities with belief.
- **Judging a system in pips**: pips mean little; the ratio and size rule decide the result (ch02).
- **Changing indicator settings to "fit" the market** (see ch03).

## Practitioner Notes
- Author's claim: "hope makes you lose in trading"; a stop is set by structure, so size is adapted to it, never the reverse.
- A plan prepared during idle time beats an improvised entry; most plans may take days or weeks to trigger, or never trigger.
- He does not hold trades overnight routinely and stops monitoring after 19:00 (his rule; adapt to your own schedule).
- Friday evening with thin liquidity: he prepares plans but does not open trades.
- Emotional rule-breaking should be logged as such; in his own examples he publishes losing trades (DowJones 5-min, GBPCAD) to show that losses belong to the method.

## Key Takeaways
1. Emotional control and money management outrank indicator mastery; they are the precondition for every rule that follows.
2. Remove fear and hope; execute in cold blood: analyse, set stop and TP, let it run.
3. No signal means no trade; abstention is often the correct position.
4. Always use a stop, placed at a technical invalidation level, never at random.
5. Prepare bullish and bearish plans ahead rather than forming a directional opinion.
6. Log every loss by cause (analysis, stop placement, emotion, normal loss).
7. Never widen a stop after entry; accept the loss rather than break the ratio.

# Chapter 1: Value Investing: An Introduction (Graham, Dodd and the Margin of Safety)
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private, *Value Investing* guide (2020), Ch. 1 "Value Investing: un'introduzione" | Dennis Marco Montagna, CFA; plus insert "Value Investing ai tempi del Covid-19" (Angelo Meda, CFA, CIPM, Banor SIM; sponsor content, attributed where used)

## Core Idea
**Value investing** is an analytical, long-horizon equity style: estimate what a business is worth, buy only at a meaningful discount to that estimate, and let price converge to value. It was born after the 1929 crash in Benjamin Graham and David Dodd's *Security Analysis* (1934) and formalised for the individual in Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* (1949). Its core separation is **investor vs speculator**: the investor values the business, the speculator bets that the price will rise. It is a philosophy with screening rules, not a mechanical algorithm; Graham himself warned that mechanical use erodes the inefficiencies the rules exploit.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Investment operation test (Graham-Dodd)**
  - What: an operation is an investment only if, after thorough analysis, it promises safety of principal and an adequate return; otherwise it is speculation.
  - How: (1) analyse the company and its business in depth; (2) compute an expected return; (3) plan capital protection before buying.
  - Why it works / fails: forces valuation-first discipline; fails when "analysis" is a rationalisation of a price already paid.
- **Margin of safety**
  - What: `MoS = intrinsic value - market price` (often quoted as `1 - P/V`). It is a buffer against estimation error and market volatility.
  - How: estimate V conservatively, demand a discount (the guide's practitioner rule of thumb: upside of at least 20-30% to purchase price), buy only when price is below `V x (1 - MoS)`.
  - Why it works: the discount protects the principal if V is overestimated and pays through convergence if V is right. Fails if the business is impaired (value trap): the discount must not be caused by genuine destruction of value.
- **Mr. Market allegory**
  - What: price quotes come from a moody business partner who offers to buy or sell at his mood-driven price every day.
  - How: form your own valuation from reports on operations and financial position; transact only when his quote is absurdly high (sell) or low (buy); otherwise ignore him.
  - Why it works: separates price (information about the mood) from value (information about the business). Value timing is set by price relative to value, not by the calendar.
- **Graham defensive-stock screen (Intelligent Investor criteria)**
  - What: seven filters for a conservative, "defensive" portfolio. Treated as guidelines, not hard cut-offs; a breach can be accepted if other facts compensate.
  - How: apply the table below, then do the qualitative analysis (management, capital allocation).
- **Graham valuation formula (revised editions)**
  - What: a quantitative anchor for a "good price", `V = E x (8.5 + 2g) x 4.4 / Y`. The guide cites the formula without legible detail; the form above is the standard published one.
  - How: see Formulas section; use as a sanity check, never as the decision.
- **Investor profile: defensive vs enterprising**
  - What: required return scales with the intelligent effort one can deploy. Passive investor: minimum return, safety, freedom from worry (today: low-cost passive funds). Active, enterprising investor: higher expected return in exchange for competence, time and tolerance of variability.
  - How: choose the profile that fits your skills and time; if you lack competence, index.
  - Behavioural conditions: appropriate knowledge, invest only money you can afford to lose, focus on data and method rather than the market, stay unemotional.
- **Portfolio concept (10-30 stocks)**
  - What: holdings between about 10 and 30 names. Below that, risk (or the time to reach acceptable gains) rises; far above, the portfolio behaves like the market and selection effort is wasted.
  - Connects value investing to Markowitz diversification and later passive allocation.

## Key Concepts
- **Intrinsic value**: worth of the business from assets, earnings power and prospects, independent of the quoted price.
- **Margin of safety**: positive gap between intrinsic value and price, protecting against error and shocks.
- **Mr. Market**: Graham's personification of volatile market prices as an offer, not an instruction.
- **Investor vs speculator**: investor values the company; speculator forecasts price moves.
- **Defensive stock** (Graham): a security selected by prudence criteria (size, balance sheet, record, price), not membership of a "defensive sector"; the early value investors did not classify by sector.
- **Earnings yield**: `E/P`, the inverse of P/E; compared with the risk-free rate in Graham-style rules.
- **Value vs high-dividend**: value investing is often confused with high-dividend investing; dividends are part of return but retention for growth and balance-sheet strength matters equally. No search for high yield as such.
- **Mean reversion ("return to the mean")**: assumption that after bad periods conditions normalise, so buying low plus compounding gives good absolute and relative returns.
- **Nifty Fifty**: 50 high-quality US stocks that reached very high P/Es in the late 1960s/early 1970s; they lost about 60% in the following 10 years (as cited in the guide's introduction). Quality is not enough without a valuation discount.
- **Compounding with low inflation**: even modest, solid growth compounds meaningfully and defends against inflation, hence "little but solid".
- **Quality of management**: governance and capital allocation are assessed qualitatively; engaged shareholders speak up when results or actions fall short.
- **Competitive advantage durability**: moats seldom last 10-20 years (Coca-Cola holds; Nokia collapsed in a few years after Apple).

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `V = E x (8.5 + 2g) x 4.4 / Y`
  - V = intrinsic value per share; E = trailing 12-month EPS; g = expected long-run annual growth rate (in %, typically over 7-10 years); 8.5 = P/E of a no-growth company; 4.4 = average AAA corporate bond yield (%) at the time of the 1962 formula; Y = current AAA yield (%).
  - The 1962 version has no yield term: `V = E x (8.5 + 2g)`. The guide describes the last parameter as a bond-yield ratio, consistent with `4.4/Y`.
- `Margin of safety (%) = (V - P) / V`
- `Earnings yield = E / P = 1 / (P/E)`

**Graham defensive criteria (as reported in the guide)**

| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Size | Revenue not below 100 million (industrial company) |
| Financial position | Total assets at least twice total debt |
| Earnings stability | Profits in each of the last 10 years |
| Dividend record | Uninterrupted dividends for 10 years |
| Earnings growth | Annual growth above 3% over 10 years |
| Moderate P/E | Price / average earnings of last 3 years not above 15x |
| Price to book | P/B not above 1.5 (Graham's combined rule is usually P/E x P/B <= 22.5; the guide cites each limit separately) |

**Meda's three steps to "true" value investing**: (1) understand the sector and its main dynamics; (2) select companies with strong, lasting competitive advantages; (3) buy only at a significant discount to intrinsic value.

**Graham's rate-linked rules (cited in the Covid insert)**: buy when earnings yield is at least twice the risk-free rate, and P/E is at least 40% below its 5-year average. With rates near 0.65% the first rule admits almost any stock (P/E ceiling of roughly 77-83x) and the second leaves little to buy.

## Mental Models
- **Use "investor or speculator?" when evaluating any idea**: if your return depends on someone else paying more rather than on business value, it is speculation.
- **Use margin of safety when your estimate is uncertain**: the more uncertain the cash flows or the macro picture, the larger the required discount.
- **Use Mr. Market when prices move sharply without news about the business**: re-check V; if V is unchanged, the price drop is an opportunity, not a signal.
- **Use the 10-30 stock rule when sizing the book**: fewer names raises idiosyncratic risk, many more dilutes into the index.
- **Use the "discount from impairment?" test before buying a cheap stock**: if the price fall reflects real asset or earnings destruction, it is not a margin of safety.

## Worked Example
Margin of safety (Buffett-style illustration, recomputed).
- Inputs: business worth 1,000; shares fairly priced at 1,000; market falls to 700 without any destruction of the business or its assets.
- Steps: MoS = (1,000 - 700)/1,000 = 30%. Upside to value = 1,000/700 - 1 = 42.9%. Price can fall a further 30% only if value itself is impaired.
- Result: buying at 700 yields 43% on convergence, plus any value the business adds in the meantime.
- Interpretation: the 30% discount sits inside the guide's 20-30% rule of thumb; the case depends on the premise that value is intact.

Graham formula (illustrative, not from the source).
- Inputs: E = 5.00, g = 6%, Y = 5.5%.
- Steps: 8.5 + 2x6 = 20.5; V = 5 x 20.5 x 4.4/5.5 = 82.0 (the 1962 version without Y gives 102.5).
- Result: buy only below about 82 less a margin of safety, e.g. about 57 at 30%.
- Interpretation: a higher AAA yield lowers V; the formula is rate-sensitive, which is why it struggles at near-zero rates.

## Anti-patterns
- Applying Graham's screens mechanically; the guide stresses that value investing is a philosophy, and published simple rules get arbitraged away.
- Equating value investing with high dividend yield or with defensive sectors.
- Buying quality at any price (Nifty Fifty).
- Buying a "cheap" stock whose price reflects impaired assets (value trap).
- Following the crowd; Graham treats it as the characteristic error of the speculator.
- Using today's ultra-low rates as a permanent input into valuation (Meda: rates likely to normalise to levels below the 50-70 year average but above current ones).
- Over-diversifying to dozens of names, losing the benefit of selection.

## Practitioner Notes
- Historical context drives the doctrine: three fears of the 1930s were capital loss, difficulty rebuilding wealth, and inflation. The answer was solid businesses bought cheap, modest dividend payout (reinvest in growth), and equities as an inflation hedge.
- Updating the doctrine after Covid-19 (Meda): the method stays; add one question per sector, "has the 2020 experience structurally changed competitive dynamics or consumer habits?" Distinguish structural from cyclical effects. Many sectors saw existing trends accelerate (online commerce, new media).
- Crises reveal moats: strong firms gain share and returns as weak rivals exit; low-leverage firms can keep investing in marketing and service while indebted ones must preserve cash.
- Intangible-heavy economies (software, internet) change which assets and sectors dominate, but not the logic of paying less than value.
- Valuation models (DDM, DCF, multiples) all take a risk-free rate as input; lower rates raise present values mechanically, so stress-test with normalised rates.

## Key Takeaways
1. Value investing = estimate intrinsic value, buy at a discount, wait for convergence; the investor values the business, the speculator forecasts the price.
2. The margin of safety, introduced in *Security Analysis*, is the central risk-control idea; it protects against estimation error and volatility.
3. Graham's criteria are a guideline set (size, balance sheet, 10-year earnings and dividends, P/E <= 15 on 3-year earnings, P/B <= 1.5); breaching one is acceptable if other evidence compensates.
4. Return should match the intelligent effort invested: passive funds for those without skill, active value for those with it.
5. Hold roughly 10-30 stocks; fewer is risky, more mirrors the market.
6. Dividends matter but value investing is not a high-dividend strategy.
7. Simple, widely published rules lose force as adoption spreads (Graham's own caution), so analysis, not formula, is the edge.
8. Low rates distort rate-linked valuation rules; use normalised rates and larger margins.

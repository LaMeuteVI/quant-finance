# Chapter 2: A Story of Value (Principles, Multiples and Why Value Is Rarely Practised)
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private, *Value Investing* guide (2020), Ch. 2 "Una storia di valore" | Dennis Marco Montagna, CFA

## Core Idea
This chapter turns Graham's philosophy into a working toolkit: why value investing is hard to stay with (anxiety, time, benchmark risk), what its two pillars are (**intrinsic value** and **margin of safety**), and how to read the basic multiples that give a first estimate of value. Its message is that value investing is a set of guidelines that keep you away from bad stocks more than a rulebook that picks good ones, and that the real scarce resource is temperament, not information.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two pillars of value investing**
  - What: (1) buy at or below **intrinsic value**; (2) require a **margin of safety**.
  - How: estimate the value of the business from assets and earnings, ignore what the market says day to day, buy when price is near or below value, and add an extra discount for estimation risk.
  - Why it works / fails: value as a "floor" limits losses because you do not pay for expectations. It fails when value is overestimated or the business deteriorates, or when the market ignores value for years.
- **Temperament plus asset choice (the two practical pillars)**
  - What: a value investor needs the patience to wait months or years for recognition, plus the discipline to hold assets priced low relative to the underlying business.
  - Cost of that patience: the investment may look wrong for a long time, other less logical choices are rewarded, and the benchmark is a poor guide.
- **Value as the dual of momentum and growth**
  - What: momentum cares about price reaction and self-reinforcement; growth cares about rapid expansion of the business and investors pay a premium for it; value compares value with price and ignores the narrative.
  - How: use as a diagnostic of what you are really paying for (price trend, growth story, or assets and earnings).
- **Direct vs indirect multiples**
  - What: a **direct multiple** has the economic quantity on top and the market quantity below (higher is better): dividend yield, earnings yield. An **indirect multiple** has the market quantity on top (lower is better): P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B.
  - How: invert an indirect multiple to compare with yields and inflation; for example P/E of 5 equals an earnings yield of 20%.
- **Multiple triangulation**
  - What: no single ratio gives the full picture; combine equity multiples (P/E) with enterprise multiples (EV/EBITDA, which accounts for debt) and use each in absolute terms and relative to history and peers.
  - Why: earnings alone hide leverage; Graham started as a credit analyst and always looked at debt, not just earnings capacity.
- **Sell and hold discipline**
  - What: when a holding falls below purchase, re-analyse the company case by case rather than applying a mechanical stop. When price clearly exceeds intrinsic value, sell and realise the gain.

## Key Concepts
- **Intrinsic value**: value of the business the way a fully informed buyer would assess it; the anchor for the entire method.
- **Margin of safety**: buying at a discount to value; Graham's rule of thumb in the guide is paying about two-thirds of value, a 33% discount.
- **Floor value**: intrinsic value seen as a minimum worth the market will eventually recognise; extreme version: value the assets conservatively and deduct twice the analysts' loss estimates (the Citigroup-after-Lehman anecdote).
- **Dividend yield** (direct): `DPS / price`.
- **Payout ratio**: `DPS / EPS`; the share of earnings distributed. A balance between payout and retention supports growth and value accretion.
- **Earnings yield** (direct): `EPS / price`; an annual return on the capital invested and a time measure (years to recover the price at constant earnings).
- **P/E** (indirect): price divided by trailing 12-month earnings per share.
- **EV/EBITDA**: enterprise value (equity plus debt, net of cash) over earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation; includes debt and capital structure.
- **Visibility**: the quality of earnings forecasts; value investors favour boring businesses with a solid track record, where estimation errors are contained.
- **Benchmark risk**: value strategies can lag a benchmark for long periods while intrinsic value accumulates; the guide suggests treating the benchmark as secondary.
- **Wealth effect**: higher equity prices lift corporate balance sheets and household consumption; ties equity returns to the economic cycle.
- **Buffett's coin-flip argument**: if enough people flip coins in knockout rounds, a few will have long winning streaks by chance; but if the survivors come from one "zoo" (Graham-Dodd school), the pattern is skill, and its common feature is buying below business value.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
| Measure | Formula | Type | Better when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividend yield | `DPS / P` | Direct | High |
| Earnings yield | `EPS / P = 1 / (P/E)` | Direct | High |
| Payout ratio | `DPS / EPS` | Ratio | Balanced vs reinvestment need |
| P/E | `P / EPS` | Indirect | Low |
| EV/EBITDA | `(Market cap + net debt) / EBITDA` | Indirect | Low |
| Margin of safety | `1 - P / V` | Ratio | High |

- Symbols: P = price per share; DPS = dividend per share; EPS = earnings per share; V = intrinsic value per share.
- Real return check: with inflation `pi`, a value of 100 should be worth `100 x (1 + pi)` a year later to stand still in real terms (102 at 2%).
- Value after one year with retention: `V1 = V0 + (EPS - DPS)` if retained earnings add to value at par (illustrative simplification used by the guide).

## Mental Models
- **Use "years to get my money back" (1/P/E) when comparing equities with bonds or inflation**: it turns a ratio into a time measure.
- **Use EV multiples when leverage differs across candidates**: a cheap P/E on a heavily indebted firm hides the risk that debt service eats the profits and hits equity first.
- **Use visibility as a filter when estimates are shaky**: prefer stocks where a 10% earnings miss still leaves a discount.
- **Use relative multiples only as a fallback**: in a high-multiple market choosing the cheapest within a peer group is no longer pure value because absolute value is far above intrinsic value.
- **Use the "business-owner" test for time**: a partner asking for profits after one month is absurd; judge equity over years of business results, not weeks of prices.

## Worked Example
Dividend, earnings and inflation arithmetic (guide's example, recomputed).
- Inputs: intrinsic value and price both 100; EPS 20; DPS 10; inflation 2%.
- Steps: dividend yield = 10/100 = 10%; P/E = 100/20 = 5; earnings yield = 20/100 = 20%; payout = 10/20 = 50%. Retained earnings = 10, so value reaches 110. Inflation-adjusted starting point = 102.
- Result: real gain on value = 110 - 102 = 8, plus 10 of dividends received (pre-tax).
- Interpretation: a 20% earnings yield comfortably covers 2% inflation; the retained half is what compounds intrinsic value.

Margin-of-safety stress test (**⚠ Correction**).
- The source buys at 95 an asset worth 100 and calls it a 15% discount; the correct discount is 5%. It then lets EPS fall from 20 to 18 (-10%) and states the residual discount as 3 (3%).
- Recomputation: at constant P/E of 5, value falls to 90; price 95 is now above value by 5.6%, so the margin of safety is negative, not +3%.
- Interpretation: a thin margin disappears under a 10% forecast error. A 33% margin (price 67) would still leave a 26% discount to the reduced value of 90 (1 - 67/90 = 25.6%). This is why margin must exceed your typical forecast error.

## Anti-patterns
- Evaluating a value manager against a short-horizon benchmark and forcing a style change after underperformance.
- Chasing growth narratives with impossible extrapolations (e.g. the dot-com projections that implied 15+ hours a day of phone use per person).
- Treating a single multiple as the decision; ignoring debt and track record.
- Buying "cheap relative to peers" in an expensive market and calling it value.
- Mechanically cutting losses without re-analysing the business, or holding after price exceeds value.
- Confusing a lower purchase price with a margin of safety when the estimate itself is fragile.
- Reading dividend as the whole return; ignoring tax drag and reinvestment alternatives.

## Practitioner Notes
- Few professional investors (the guide cites 5-10%) practise pure value, because the anxiety of watching prices and the opportunity cost during manias outweigh its logic; Graham's own answer to why the market was slow to recognise value was that experience shows it eventually catches up.
- Current context (guide's view): low rates and slow growth push multiples up; opportunities in absolute terms are rarer, so pay for quality sparingly and watch leverage.
- Practical process: set conservative estimates, use several multiples, track record and visibility, buy only when price is acceptable, and be patient since markets rise and fall quickly and opportunities recur.
- Rule of thumb from the guide: the only rule in value investing is to pay little, and the corollary is not to waste money.

## Key Takeaways
1. Two pillars: intrinsic value and margin of safety; both protect against paying for expectations.
2. Value requires temperament: recognition may take months or years and the benchmark will mislead in the meantime.
3. Value is the opposite of momentum (price trend) and growth (business expansion premium).
4. Direct multiples (yields) want high values; indirect multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA) want low values; one is the inverse of the other.
5. Use several multiples with debt-aware ones, absolute and relative to history and peers.
6. Forecast error erodes thin margins: a 10% earnings miss can wipe out a 5% discount.
7. Prefer visible, boring earnings with a good track record.
8. Re-analyse losers case by case; sell when price clearly exceeds value.

# Chapter 9: Origins and Peculiarities of Systematic Investing
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private, *Systematic Investments* guide (2019), Ch. 1 "Le origini e le peculiarità degli investimenti sistematici" | Marco Castagna, CFA; plus GAM insert "Investimenti sistematici: la scienza applicata alla finanza" (sponsor content, attributed where used)

## Core Idea
**Systematic (quantitative) investing** is an approach that makes investment decisions with mathematical models, computing power and data analysis instead of case-by-case human judgement. It is not the opposite of discretionary management: both start from a rule or idea, and the real differences are **process purity, portfolio breadth and risk profile**. The honest way to evaluate it is to separate **false myths** (all quants are alike, systematic = passive, systematic = black box, a model outperforms forever) from **real limits** (algorithmic-trading market impact, cyber risk, lack of flexibility).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Systematic vs discretionary comparison**
  - What: three structural differences that follow from "model decides" vs "human decides".
  - How to apply: classify any fund on these axes before comparing performance.

  | Axis | Discretionary | Systematic |
  |---|---|---|
  | Process | Flexible: seeks the traits in the universe but weighs other factors case by case | Pure: selects only on the coded factors |
  | Portfolio | Concentrated (fewer names, more time per name) | Diversified (whole universe scored, many small positions) |
  | Average outcome | Higher active share and excess return, higher relative risk | Lower tracking error vs benchmark, lower relative risk |

  - Why it matters: repeatability of the model lets it score every name cheaply, so breadth replaces depth. Studies cited (AQR "Systematic vs Discretionary") find higher risk and return for discretionary but **similar Information Ratios** and **low correlation** between the two, so the conclusion is "combine them", not "pick the winner".
  - Failure mode: concluding one approach is superior from data sets that mix strategy classifications and are not homogeneous; the source warns these studies often conflict.
- **Same idea, two vocabularies (convergence example)**: a fundamental value manager looks for cheap stocks, a catalyst, and conservative accounting; a quant house codes the same three ideas as **Value, Momentum and Earnings Quality** factors. Use this to explain that factor investing is the systematic restatement of familiar styles.
- **Myths vs real limits** (guide Figure 1)
  | False myth | Why it is false | Real limit | Why it is true |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | All systematic strategies are alike | Models use different inputs (fundamentals, technicals, alternative risk premia, mathematical relations); correlation between quant strategies is low, similar to that between discretionary ones | Algorithmic trading market impact | Passive plus systematic flow estimated near 60% of equity trading volume vs about 10% discretionary (JPMorgan estimate cited); episodes of sharp moves with no news (5 Feb 2018: Dow about -4% in 15 minutes; May 2010: Dow -9.2% intraday) |
  | Systematic = passive | Broad diversification can raise risk-adjusted return yet still leave significant tracking error vs benchmark; it is a real limit only if over-diversification erases all active bets | Cyber security | Systems trade thousands of times per second, so a hijacked model could move markets fast |
  | Systematic = black box | Assumptions are transparent even if each formula is not known; inputs fixed, rules explicit | Lack of flexibility | No human intuition in stress; AI that updates models is the suggested evolution |
  | A model can outperform forever | True, but it is not specific to quants: regimes change, edges get copied, margins compress; discretionary managers face the same | | |

- **Origin narrative** (use as a credibility map, not as proof): Graham's seven quantitative criteria in *The Intelligent Investor* (1949: size, earnings stability, financial condition, dividend record, earnings growth, P/E, P/B) as a proto-quant screen; Renaissance Technologies' Medallion fund (fully model-driven since 1988, staffed by mathematicians and physicists rather than finance people); successors such as AHL, AQR, Cantab, Winton, Two Sigma.

## Key Concepts
- **Systematic / quantitative investing**: decisions generated by advanced mathematical models, computing power and data analysis under pre-defined rules.
- **Discretionary investing**: the final decision-maker is a human using information that is not always codifiable.
- **Active share**: difference between portfolio weights and benchmark weights; discretionary funds tend to raise it to chase more excess return.
- **Tracking error**: volatility of active return vs the benchmark; typically lower for systematic funds.
- **Information Ratio**: excess return per unit of tracking error; similar across the two approaches in the cited studies.
- **Repeatability**: the property that lets a model score a whole universe with the same rule, producing many small positions.
- **Black box (myth)**: belief that quant outputs cannot be understood; in practice the assumptions and rules are knowable.
- **Algorithmic trading**: execution driven by mathematical rules; its volume share raises the risk of news-free volatility spikes.
- **Hybrid process**: fundamental manager using a quantitative screen or scoring; the middle ground.
- **Share of assets**: by the studies cited, systematic strategies grew in two decades from about 9% to about 14% of traditional fund assets and reached about 26% of hedge fund assets; discretionary still dominates AUM.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `Information Ratio = (Rp - Rb) / TE` where `Rp` = portfolio return, `Rb` = benchmark return, `TE` = standard deviation of `Rp - Rb`.
- `Active share = 0.5 * Σ |w_p,i - w_b,i|` (standard definition; the guide only describes it in words).

## Mental Models
- Use "same destination, different engine" when a client asks whether quant is opposed to fundamental: both seek mispricing, they differ in how repeatably and broadly they apply the rule.
- Use **breadth vs depth** when sizing: model alpha comes from hundreds of small positions, discretionary alpha from a few researched ones; combine them to diversify the source of alpha.
- Use **IR, not return, to compare** styles: higher return with higher TE can hide an identical IR.
- Use the **myth/limit split** in due diligence: dismiss "black box" and "all alike" by asking for inputs and correlation to peers; probe seriously on crowding, execution, cyber risk and what happens in regime change.
- Use **risk-profile weighting** when blending: the source suggests raising the discretionary share for more risk-tolerant investors, which is the source's heuristic, not a CFA rule.

## Worked Example
Inputs: discretionary fund alpha +3.0% with TE 6.0%; systematic fund alpha +1.5% with TE 3.0%.
- IR discretionary = 3.0 / 6.0 = **0.50**; IR systematic = 1.5 / 3.0 = **0.50**.
- Interpretation: the discretionary fund looks twice as good on return, but per unit of active risk they are equal. If the two are lowly correlated, a mix delivers a higher IR than either alone, which is the practical meaning of "combine, do not choose". (Illustrative numbers; the guide reports only that IRs are similar.)

## Anti-patterns
- Calling a fund "passive" because it holds hundreds of names without checking tracking error and active share.
- Assuming every quant fund diversifies another quant fund: crowded signals break that (see ch10, August 2007).
- Treating "no emotions" as "no bias": humans still write the rules (see ch11).
- Comparing systematic vs discretionary returns from mixed, non-homogeneous data sets.
- Ignoring operational risks (execution algorithms, cyber security) in a systematic due diligence.

## Practitioner Notes
- Pitch systematic exposure as a **diversifier of the alpha source**, not a replacement for active management; this is also the sponsor's own framing (GAM: systematic complements discretionary and offers low correlation to equities and bonds, with transparency of rules and positions, supervised by humans and a large research team). Sponsor claims about its own team and results are marketing, not evidence.
- Ask for: signal inputs, universe, turnover, capacity, crowding indicators and drawdown behavior in 2007 and 2018.
- Quant funds' assets are still dominated by discretionary managers; the growth trend is the story, not current dominance.

## Key Takeaways
1. Systematic investing = rule-based, model-driven, data-heavy; it is a way of expressing familiar styles, not a different philosophy of value.
2. Differences vs discretionary: purer process, more diversified portfolio, lower relative risk and lower active share.
3. Evidence on which approach is better is mixed; IRs are similar and results are lowly correlated, so combination is the robust answer.
4. Most criticisms (all alike, passive, black box) are myths; the real limits are algorithmic market impact, cyber risk and inflexibility.
5. No model outperforms forever, but this applies equally to discretionary managers.
6. Quant teams hire physicists and mathematicians; the source treats scientific research culture as a differentiator.

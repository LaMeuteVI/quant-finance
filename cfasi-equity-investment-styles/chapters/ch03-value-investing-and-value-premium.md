# Chapter 3: Value Investing and the Value Premium (Meaning of Investing, Fama-French HML, Profitability, Value Traps, Technological Cycles)
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private, *Value Investing* guide (2020), Ch. 3 "Value Investing e Value Premium: riflessioni sul significato di investire" | Dennis Marco Montagna, CFA; plus insert "Quando un titolo Value è anche Growth?" (Tomaso Mariotti, Banor SIM; sponsor content, attributed where used). Data as of April 2020; charts credited in the guide to Dimensional, Bloomberg, Russell and OSAM.

## Core Idea
The **value premium** is the return reward for owning low price-to-book stocks, formalised by Fama and French as **HML (High minus Low P/B)**. The chapter links it to the investor's own arithmetic: expected return, required return (risk-free rate plus premia), and the way price converges to value through **book value growth, ROE and dividends**. Its message is that the premium is real but time-varying, that **cheap P/B alone fails when profitability collapses (value traps)**, that profitability is the natural second filter, and that a cheap multiple can hide a changing industrial structure, so value investors must read the cycle, not just the ratio.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Return decomposition: realised, expected, required**
  - What: realised return `RI = dividends + (sale price - purchase price)`; expected return `RA = (D1 + P1 - P0)/P0`; required return `rR` = minimum acceptable return.
  - How: decide to buy when `RA >= rR`; sell when price exceeds the estimated value and further return no longer compensates the risk.
  - Why it works: ties the decision to an explicit hurdle. Risk: all three are estimates.
- **Required return builders (CAPM and Fama-French)**
  - CAPM: `rR = rf + beta x ERP`. Fama-French: `rR = rf + beta x ERP + s x SMB + h x HML` (plus profitability factor in the extended model).
  - How to use: build a value portfolio by choosing stocks with the largest exposure to HML (lowest P/B), compute rR, compare with the expected return.
- **HML/SMB definitions**
  - SMB = Small minus Big (size). HML = High minus Low P/B, i.e. returns of high book-to-market (value) minus low (growth) stocks. Long-run average value premium in the guide's chart: about 4.7% a year over roughly 1928-2018, with wide dispersion by year.
- **Two stories on the origin of the premium**
  1. Risk/leverage story: value firms carry medium-high debt supported by stable assets; leverage raises profitability but amplifies sensitivity to cycles and so demands a premium.
  2. Quality/safety story: value investing in Graham's sense means solid balance sheet, steady (even slow) growth, certain assets and visibility; here the discount reflects mispricing, not risk.
  - Practical conclusion: P/B captures both kinds of company; separate them with profitability, leverage and cash generation.
- **Value x Profitability matrix**
  - What: sort stocks on P/B (value vs growth) and profitability (low vs high) and compare average monthly returns.
  - Result (US, 1963-2018, % per month): growth/low profit 0.74, growth/high profit 0.90, value/low profit 0.93, value/high profit 1.33. Profitability premium within value = 0.40; value premium within high profitability = 0.42. Same ranking in non-US developed (1990-2018) and emerging markets (1995-2018) with the value/high profit cell at 0.69 and 1.12 respectively (non-US developed's best cell is mid-profit value at 0.74).
  - How to use: combine bottom-up value analysis with a mechanical profitability screen; the best basket is cheap and profitable.
- **P/B as a function of ROE, growth and discount rate (Gordon/residual-income form)**
  - Derivation: next-year earnings `E1 = ROE x BV0`; if book grows at `g` through retention, dividend `D1 = E1 - g x BV0`; with constant growth `P0 = D1/(K - g)`, so `P0/BV0 = (ROE - g)/(K - g)`.
  - Reading: high ROE or low K or high g raises justified P/B; a low P/B reflects low ROE, high discount rate or low growth, hence value traps when ROE has structurally dropped.
- **Overprice recovery test (the "sovraprezzo" analysis)**
  - What: split price into book value and overprice (price minus book). Track how fast `book growth + dividends` absorbs the overprice.
  - Example logic: P/B 1.25 means 80 of book per 100 invested and 20 of premium; value investors are repaid by value creation and dividends rather than by price change.
  - Evidence (Russell 1000 Value vs Growth): the value index's overprice is 30-60% and is on average absorbed in 8-9 years; growth's overprice stays above 60% and is about 50% still unrecovered after 10 years. A 10-year horizon matches the estimated equity cash-flow duration of 8-12 years.
- **Equity duration view**
  - What: equity cash flows have an effective duration of about 8-12 years; the higher the multiples and the less stable the cash flows, the longer the duration and the greater the risk. Growth is long duration, value shorter.
  - Implication: falling rates help long-duration growth stocks; value shows resilience in downturns and better compounded returns at 9-10 years in the guide's data (index data, not a guarantee).
- **Technological-revolution cycle (Perez) applied to value**
  - Phases: irruption, frenzy (installation, "creative destruction"), turning point after a crash, synergy, maturity (deployment, "creative construction").
  - How to use: in installation, cheap incumbents of the old paradigm are often traps (utilities and transport in 1925-37, energy and banks after 2007). After the turning point leaders mature, build oligopolies, return capital through dividends and buybacks and look like value stocks.
  - Evidence: annualised value-over-growth spreads by phase (1926-1974 oil/auto/mass production age): P/B spread -6.13% (1926-41), 4.41% (synergy 1942-59), 3.14% (maturity 1960-74); P/E 8.49% and 4.14% in the last two. Value does best around the transition.
- **Value trap diagnosis**
  - Tell: low P/B or P/E with falling ROE, cut dividends, high leverage, a structurally shrinking industry. Both leading explanations of the premium (cyclical leverage risk, deteriorating quality) hit the same stocks in 2008 and in the 2020 sell-off.
  - Use: before buying, test whether the fall in ROE is cyclical or structural.

## Key Concepts
- **Value premium**: excess return of low-P/B over high-P/B stocks, reward for bearing risk (or mispricing).
- **HML**: Fama-French value factor; high book-to-market minus low.
- **SMB**: size factor; small cap minus large cap.
- **Required return (rR)**: hurdle built from rf plus risk premia; the discount rate.
- **Expected return (RA)**: forecast of dividends plus price change over the holding period.
- **Book value (BV)**: accounting equity; the anchor of the Fama-French definition of value; intangible-heavy firms understate it.
- **ROE**: return on equity; the engine of book growth and a determinant of P/B.
- **Overprice (sovraprezzo)**: price minus book value; what the investor must recover through value creation.
- **Beta**: sensitivity to the market; value index beta jumped in 2008 and in the 2020 sell-off but is typically lower than growth's.
- **Buyback as variable dividend**: low-cost debt funded repurchases boosted per-share earnings and demand for growth stocks, adding a financial rather than operating source of growth.
- **Value trap**: stock that looks cheap on multiples but whose fundamentals are deteriorating.
- **TINA**: "there is no alternative"; low rates pushing investors into equities.
- **Risk-free rate effect**: lower rf lowers discount rates and raises asset values, favouring long duration and risk-on positions.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `RI = D + (P_sale - P_buy)`; in % divide by P_buy.
- `RA = (D1 + P1 - P0) / P0`
- `rR(CAPM) = rf + beta x ERP`
- `rR(FF) = rf + b x ERP + s x SMB + h x HML (+ profitability factor)`
- `P/B = (ROE - g) / (K - g)`, valid for `K > g` and constant ROE and growth.
- `Compound return = (1 + CAGR)^n - 1`: a 5% annual return for 10 years yields about 62.9% (100 becomes about 162.9).
- Symbols: D = dividends; P0, P1 = price now and at horizon; rf = risk-free rate; ERP = equity risk premium; K = discount rate; g = growth of book/earnings.
- Graham combined rule (Mariotti insert): `P/E x P/B <= 22.5` (15 x 1.5), also earnings yield at least twice the risk-free yield.

## Mental Models
- **Use a P/B screen only with a profitability screen**: cheap and profitable beats cheap alone in every region tested.
- **Use "P/B = (ROE - g)/(K - g)" to ask why a stock is cheap**: if ROE fell or K rose, the discount may be fair.
- **Use the overprice test when paying above book**: if book growth plus dividends cannot absorb the premium within about 10 years, you are relying on price appreciation, which is speculation.
- **Use the cycle map when a whole sector looks cheap**: ask which technological phase it is in; sectors losing share to a new paradigm are traps.
- **Use normalised rates when discounting**: with 10-year yields below 1% (March 2020) mechanical multiples overstate value; the P/E-vs-rates relation suggests higher multiples could be justified but that is conditional.
- **Use "value is also growth" as a lens**: the quality of the business (moat, ROCE, balance sheet) matters as much as the multiple (Mariotti).

## Worked Example
Decision rule from expected and required returns (guide numbers, recomputed).
- Inputs: share priced 100, dividend 4, analysts' year-end price 116.
- Steps: RA = (4 + 116 - 100)/100 = 20%; dividend yield 4%; price gain 16%.
- Result: buy only if rR is below 20%. If rf = 2%, beta 1.1, ERP 5%, then rR = 2 + 1.1 x 5 = 7.5%, large margin.
- Interpretation: the margin between RA and rR is the cushion; analysts' target is an estimate, so haircut it.

Overprice absorption (illustrative, recomputed; not from the source).
- Inputs: price 100, P/B 1.25 (book 80, overprice 20); book growth 5% a year; dividends 2 a year not reinvested.
- Steps: after 3 years 80 x 1.05^3 + 6 = 98.6; after 4 years 80 x 1.05^4 + 8 = 105.2.
- Result: overprice absorbed in about 4 years, close to the 8-9 year average the guide found when the premium was 30-60%.
- Interpretation: a growth stock at P/B 6 (book 16.7) with book growth 8% and 0.5 dividends reaches only about 27 after 5 years and 41 after 10 years against a 100 price, so it needs multiple support.

P/B justified value (illustrative).
- Inputs: ROE 12%, g 4%, K 9%. Steps: (12 - 4)/(9 - 4) = 1.6x. If ROE falls to 8%: 0.8x. If ROE rises to 15%: 2.2x.
- Interpretation: a P/B drop from 1.6 to 0.8 is exactly what a ROE collapse does; cheap and trap look alike.

## Anti-patterns
- Buying low P/B without checking profitability trend, leverage and dividend sustainability (energy, banks, 2007-2020).
- Treating the factor premium as constant; it is volatile and was weak after 2006 and especially after 2015 (value-over-growth ratio near historic lows in 2020).
- Believing that "boring stocks" or "bond-like stocks" are risk-free substitutes for bonds: equities carry business risk.
- Ignoring that book value understates intangibles (licences, patents, software), which makes tech stocks look like growth when they may be underpriced on true capital.
- Ignoring buyback and leverage effects: debt-financed buybacks lift per-share growth but disappear when credit tightens (2020 suspensions of buybacks and dividends).
- Extrapolating the present rate regime; the 2020 rate floor inflates valuation but also signals low real growth.
- Judging value on a one-to-three year window; the guide's evidence uses roughly 10-year horizons.

## Practitioner Notes
- Style performance chronology (value vs growth, Russell indices): value won in the early 2000s after the dot-com bubble through 2006; lagged in 2008-09 (forced selling and leverage), drifted from 2011-12 and sharply after 2015 (negative in 2015, rebound in 2016, underperformance 2017-2019 and early 2020). Value index beta rose sharply during the two sell-offs, then dropped as buyers returned.
- Index composition is itself a signal: in 2017 the Russell Value index still looked like the 2007 market (energy 10.4%, banks 9.3%) while the market itself moved to software and media; ten largest value names returned modestly versus the S&P 500 (the guide cites 6% vs 33.5%, Jan 2017-Apr 2020, with Apple the only strong value holding).
- Mariotti's "third way" (Banor): buy only high-quality, cash-generative, moat-protected companies, and only when price is at least 30% below DCF-based intrinsic value; use lower long-run discount rates; avoid both value traps (European banks, oil, transport lost over 50% in a month in Q1 2020) and unprofitable growth (Tilray, WeWork, Beyond Meat in 2019). Case logic: Apple at under 10x expected earnings ex-cash in 2016 (market over 18x), Facebook at about 16x in 2018, Amazon adjusted for third-party sales (13% commission revenue treated as gross sales) traded at a discount to physical retailers.
- Valuation choice: capitalised-multiple methods are relative (the "Aristotelian" view); DCF-based intrinsic value is absolute (the "Platonic" view). Both carry market risk: multiples can compress with prices, DCF with rising risk premia.
- Buffett-style discipline: Graham/Buffett liquidated or avoided when they found no value, and widened sectors (Buffett moved to Microsoft and Apple).

## Key Takeaways
1. Value is rewarded as a risk premium: the guide's HML average is about 4.7% a year over roughly 90 years, but with long droughts.
2. Required return starts from rf, adds equity premium times beta and, in Fama-French, size and value tilts.
3. `P/B = (ROE - g)/(K - g)`: value must be read together with profitability and discount rate; low P/B with falling ROE is a trap.
4. Value plus high profitability produced the best average returns across US, developed ex-US and emerging markets.
5. Value pays back through book growth and dividends in 8-9 years on average; growth often cannot absorb its overprice within 10 years.
6. Value outperformed in crises but suffered leverage-driven selling in 2008 and 2020; its beta spikes then falls.
7. Technological transitions turn old-economy leaders into value traps and mature leaders into value stocks; assess the cycle phase.
8. Book value understates intangibles; adjust before labelling a stock growth.
9. Rates near zero lift valuations and favour long duration; stress-test with normalised rates.

# Chapter 24: Emerging-Market Bonds: Key Aspects and ESG Sustainability Dynamics
> **Source**: CFA Society Italy × Advisor Private, *Emerging Markets* guide (2021), Ch. 3 "Le obbligazioni nei mercati emergenti: aspetti chiave e dinamica di sostenibilità" | Anna Paola Marchi, CFA and Thomas Odermatt; plus Capital Group insert "Debito in valuta locale, serve selettività" (Peter Becker, sponsor content, attributed)

## Core Idea
Emerging-market debt (EMD) has matured: more than 55% of hard-currency debt is investment grade and the asset class offers roughly twice the yield per unit of duration of developed-market bonds. It is **heterogeneous**: three sub-markets (hard-currency sovereign, local-currency sovereign, hard-currency corporate) behave differently, and in local-currency bonds the **currency is as important as the bond**. Traditional macro-financial credit analysis of sovereigns misses governance and social risks that drive long-run solvency, so **ESG integration** is especially material in EM, where weights on governance, social and environmental factors should be higher than in developed markets.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three EMD sub-markets (index size as cited)**
  | Segment | Index (size) | Drivers | Notes |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Hard-currency (HC) sovereign | JPM EMBI Global (USD 1,352bn) | US Treasury rates plus EM spread (spread is the key performance driver); more than 80 countries issue | Lower FX risk; lets small countries tap global investors |
  | Local-currency (LC) sovereign | JPM GBI-EM (USD 1,867bn) | Local rates, inflation, fiscal-monetary policy, and FX | Needs market size, maturity and currency stability; issuers include Mexico, Indonesia, China |
  | Corporate | JPM CEMBI (USD 1,375bn) | Idiosyncratic credit risk; financials largest, then natural resources; LatAm and Asia (China) lead issuance | Can have lower country risk than sovereign; higher Sharpe and much shorter duration; USD corporate market now exceeds USD sovereign in size |
- **Ten-year performance (USD, Table 1)**
  | | HC sovereign | LC sovereign | HC corporate | Bloomberg Global Aggregate |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Annualised return | 5.52% | 0.26% | 5.27% | 1.98% |
  | Annualised volatility | 5.56% | 9.44% | 3.09% | 4.51% |
  Interpretation: strong USD appreciation over the decade made LC debt underperform; the corporate USD index had the lowest volatility. LC is an FX exposure, not just a bond exposure.
- **Correlation facts**: over the last 20 years HC vs LC EM debt 0.72, and EM debt vs US debt 0.88 (these two figures are from this chapter; **⚠ Note** ch22 Table 6 gives HC-LC 0.80 over 2002-2020 with different indices; treat 0.7-0.8 as the range). Takeaway: hard-currency EM debt is closely tied to US fixed income; LC debt behaves differently.
- **Maturation indicators**: S&P rating evolution 2000 to 2021 (Figure 1: several EM sovereigns upgraded, DM downgraded); EM share of world GDP (PPP) rising (Figure 2); the proportion of IG issuers in corporates fell recently, showing market confidence in lower-rated names; return per unit of duration improved for corporates (Figure 4); EM corporates show attractive spread versus leverage compared with US corporates in the same rating buckets (Figures 5-6).
- **Corporate credit lens**: leverage = debt service burden vs available cash (net debt/EBITDA style) compared with spread; EM compensation per turn of leverage is generally favourable versus US credit.
- **Risks list**: default and recovery; political instability; immature markets; unstable growth; currency risk (EM currencies tend to appreciate in calm markets thanks to rate differentials and depreciate sharply in crises as investors flee to low-yield safe havens); for HC bonds currency risk shifts to the issuer's balance sheet; structural themes: ageing demographics, China's debt, climate change, weak governance. Weight in global indices about 8-10% of Bloomberg Global Aggregate but over 35% of its return contribution (author's claim).
- **ESG in sovereign credit (PRI Sovereign Fixed Income working group)**
  | Environmental | Social | Governance |
  |---|---|---|
  | Climate change; water resources and pollution; biodiversity; energy resources and management; biocapacity and ecosystem quality; air pollution; natural disaster; natural resources | Human rights; education and human capital; health levels; political freedoms; demographic change; employment levels; social exclusion and poverty; trust in society/institutions; crime and safety; food security | Institutional strength; corruption; regime stability; political rights and civil liberties; rule of law; regulatory effectiveness and quality; accounting standards; government finances |
  - Why: macro-financial indicators (debt/GDP, reserves, FX policy, growth, balance of payments) are easy to quantify and good short-term, but miss corruption, democratic institutions, rights, resource management that drive long-term stability.
  - Cases: **Greece 2009**: tax evasion by self-employed about EUR 28bn (about 30% of the deficit); the government revealed in autumn 2009 that the budget deficit was 13.6% of GDP instead of the 5% announced (about 2.7x), triggering the crisis. **Arab Spring/Egypt**: repressive regimes plus educated, connected populations create unrest risk.
  - Pricing evidence: since 2007 an inverse relation between sovereign ESG quality and CDS spread; governance has the largest effect, then social, then environmental (Allianz GI 2017). Environmental weakness in prices may reflect markets' difficulty in pricing it.
- **ESG in corporate credit**: better ESG scores correlate with lower CDS spreads (Calvert); higher CO2 emissions with higher default risk (Gianfrate, EDHEC 2021). **BP Deepwater Horizon (2010)**: Sustainalytics ex-post found BP already had high operational risk (safety, environmental incidents); its CDS jumped from about 50 bps to about 600 bps after the spill. PRI corporate factor list: Environmental (climate change, biodiversity, energy management, ecosystem quality, pollution, natural disaster, renewable/non-renewable resources); Social (employee relations, human rights, product responsibility, health and safety, diversity, consumer relations, skilled labour, demographics); Governance (business integrity, shareholder rights, incentive structure, audit practices, board expertise, fiduciary duty, transparency, accountability, financial policy).
- **EM vs DM ESG approach**: same factors but different weights. In EM, regime type, income per capita, foreign-currency debt, and access to water and electricity weigh more because governments have less financial flexibility to absorb shocks, institutions and regulators are weaker, corruption higher, social indicators lower and environmental laws looser.
- **Open issues**: (a) divergent ESG ratings (Berg-Kolbel-Rigobon 2020: six agencies disagree most on human rights and product safety); (b) no global reporting standard; (c) benchmark constraints (excluding states with the death penalty would exclude the US, Japan, China, etc.); (d) over-regulation and greenwashing; "tick-the-box" risk.
- **Capital Group view on LC debt (sponsor)**: income was the main return source of GBI-EM GD in USD unhedged (2002-2021 chart); LC can diversify US-rate cycles because markets follow local policy (especially with low foreign ownership, such as India); governments are replacing external debt with LC; EM currencies weakened over the decade but look undervalued on the in-house FEVER model (PPP-based with time-varying real equilibrium rate); procyclical, so global growth sets timing; productivity slowing, so be selective; active managers can hedge case by case, use inflation-linked bonds, interest-rate swaps to adjust duration without FX risk (at counterparty risk), and avoid index weights (for example Central and Eastern Europe). Examples of duration opportunities: current-account-surplus Asia such as China and Malaysia.

## Key Concepts
- **EMD**: emerging-market debt (sovereign and corporate).
- **EMBI / GBI-EM / CEMBI**: JPM indices for HC sovereign, LC sovereign, HC corporate.
- **Hard vs local currency debt**: issued in USD/EUR vs domestic currency.
- **Return per unit of duration**: yield/spread efficiency measure.
- **CDS spread**: market price of credit risk; used to test ESG pricing.
- **Leverage (corporate)**: debt service relative to cash flow.
- **PRI**: UN Principles for Responsible Investment.
- **Materiality**: ESG factor weight depends on development stage.
- **Greenwashing**: presenting products as green without substance.
- **Procyclical currency**: currencies that fall with global risk-off.
- **Real equilibrium exchange rate**: PPP-linked value that can drift over time (FEVER).
- **Interest-rate swap overlay**: duration exposure without currency risk.

## Mental Models
- Use **FX is the bond** for LC EMD: decide currency view first, then rates.
- Use **HC EMD as US spread product**; use **LC EMD as macro diversifier**.
- Use **ESG as governance first**: the largest CDS impact came from governance, then social, then environmental.
- Use **materiality re-weighting**: same ESG factor list, higher weights on governance and social factors for developing countries.
- Use **leverage vs spread** (Figures 5-6) to compare EM and US credit in the same rating bucket.
- Use **index-free hedging**: active managers can ignore low-conviction countries despite index weight.

## Worked Example
Why LC debt lagged HC over ten years (Table 1).
- LC annual return 0.26% vs HC 5.52%: gap = **5.26 pts**.
- If LC bond local returns were roughly comparable (income-driven, per Capital Group chart), the gap is mostly FX: an average currency loss of about 5 pts a year, equivalent to a cumulative `(1 - 0.05)^10 - 1` = **-40%** currency drift over ten years, compensated by carry.
- Interpretation: LC EM bonds required a view on USD strength; investors who treated them as plain bonds earned bond-like risk with no return. (Illustrative decomposition; the source does not give the split.)

## Anti-patterns
- Judging EM bonds on index yield alone without hedge/FX view.
- Using macro-financial ratios only for sovereign credit.
- Averaging ESG ratings from different providers without checking methodology (low agreement).
- Treating all EM bonds as high risk: more than half of HC debt is IG.
- Excluding countries by single ESG criterion without checking benchmark implications.
- Ignoring the CDS signal: markets already penalise low-governance sovereigns.

## Practitioner Notes
- Source headline numbers (35% return contribution; 55% IG share; twice the yield per duration) are from asset-manager marketing in 2021.
- Sponsor insert advocates active management; the hedging and swap techniques are valid tools but not free (hedging cost, counterparty risk).
- For ESG: do both country scores (governance, social, environmental) and engagement; watch data gaps.

## Key Takeaways
1. EMD has three segments: HC sovereign (USD 1.35tn), LC sovereign (USD 1.87tn), corporate (USD 1.38tn).
2. Over ten years HC sovereign returned 5.52% with 5.56% vol; LC 0.26% with 9.44% vol; corporate 5.27% with 3.09% vol.
3. LC debt is FX plus rates; HC debt is tied to US Treasuries plus spread.
4. Corporate EMD offers better risk-adjusted return and shorter duration; check leverage.
5. ESG, especially governance, explains CDS spreads for sovereigns and corporates.
6. EM ESG weighting should differ from DM; rating divergence and reporting gaps remain.
7. Active management can use hedging, inflation-linked bonds and swaps to shape duration and currency.

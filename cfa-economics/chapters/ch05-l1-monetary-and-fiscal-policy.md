# Chapter 5: Monetary and Fiscal Policy
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2020, Level I, Reading 16 · Economics | Andrew Clare, Stephen Thomas

## Core Idea
Monetary policy (central bank control of money and credit, usually through a policy rate set relative to a neutral rate) and fiscal policy (taxes, spending and the deficit) both steer aggregate demand, but through different channels, with different lags, and with different failure modes. Central banks buy credibility with independence, transparency and a clear inflation target; they run out of ammunition at the zero bound and cannot control how much money banks create. Fiscal policy has a multiplier but suffers recognition, action and impact lags, crowding-out risk and debt-sustainability limits. The analyst's job is to read the stance of each (rate versus neutral; cyclically adjusted deficit, not the headline deficit), identify the shock type (demand or supply), and judge the policy mix.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Functions of money**: medium of exchange (solves the double coincidence of wants), store of value, unit of account. A medium of exchange must be acceptable, of known value, divisible, high in value per unit of weight, hard to counterfeit. Fiat money (not convertible into a commodity, legal tender) places the entire burden of value on the issuer's credibility.
- **Fractional-reserve money creation**: with reserve requirement `R`, a new deposit `D` supports up to `D/R` total deposits; the simple money multiplier is `1/R` (a maximum: excess reserves or cash leakage reduce it). Central banks can start the chain by buying securities (credits seller's bank reserves) or lending reserves.
- **Money definitions**: narrow money (currency plus highly liquid deposits) vs broad money (adds savings, time deposits, money-market balances). Labels (M0, M1, M2, M3, M4) differ by country, so cross-country comparison needs care.
- **Quantity theory**: `M·V = P·Y`. With velocity constant, spending (`PY`) is proportional to `M`. Money neutrality: in the long run money growth changes `P`, not `Y` or `V`. Monetarists argue causality runs from money growth to inflation; the UK 1979-85 money-target experiment failed because deregulation changed velocity, so the money to nominal income link was unstable.
- **Demand for money (three motives)**: transactions and precautionary (both rise with GDP/transactions volume); speculative or portfolio (falls as returns on other assets rise, rises as their perceived risk rises). Money market: vertical nominal supply, downward-sloping demand, rate clears excess balances; a supply increase lowers the rate in the short run and raises the price level in the long run.
- **Fisher effect**: `R_nom = R_real + E(π)`; the real rate is assumed stable, so changes in nominal rates reflect changes in expected inflation. Full decomposition: nominal rate = required real return + expected inflation + risk premium for inflation/real uncertainty.
- **Costs of inflation**: expected inflation: menu costs and shoe-leather costs (smaller in a cashless economy). Unexpected inflation additionally: wealth transfer from lenders to borrowers (or the reverse when below expectation), risk premia in borrowing rates and asset prices, and noisier relative-price signals that can amplify booms and busts. This is why central banks care most about uncertainty and volatility of inflation.
- **Central-bank roles**: monopoly currency supplier, banker to government and to banks, lender of last resort, regulator/overseer of the payments system, manager of FX and gold reserves, conductor of monetary policy, (often) bank supervisor (not universal; UK moved supervision back to the Bank of England in 2013). Objective: overarching price stability, sometimes also full employment (Fed dual mandate plus moderate long rates; RBA includes full employment; ECB: price stability first, then support other policies).
- **Three tools**:
  - Open market operations: buy government bonds (adds bank reserves, expands lending via the multiplier, lowers rates) or sell (drains reserves).
  - Policy rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to banks, enforced through repo (collateralized, typically overnight to two weeks). Examples: Bank of England two-week repo rate, ECB refinancing rate, US discount rate, and the federal funds rate (overnight interbank reserves, targeted by FOMC through open market operations). Banks reset base rates because they will not lend below what the central bank charges.
  - Reserve requirements: powerful but blunt and disruptive; little used in developed economies (BoE sets none), still used in developing ones. Raising them is contractionary.
- **Transmission mechanism (four interlinked channels)**: (1) short-term rates and bank lending rates; (2) asset prices (discount rates, wealth effect); (3) expectations and confidence (a move signals the future path); (4) exchange rate (higher rate tends to appreciate currency, lowering import prices and net exports). These reduce domestic and net external demand, then inflation. Long rates depend on the expected path of short rates and on credibility: if markets think policy is too tight, long yields can fall and ease conditions ("bond market vigilantes" act the other way when policy looks loose).
- **Inflation targeting (New Zealand 1988-89)**: explicit, symmetric, forward-looking medium-term target (typically 2% with ±1 band; ECB close to but below 2%), operational independence (some banks also target-independent, like the ECB; BoE, RBNZ, Riksbank are operationally independent only), credibility, transparency (regular inflation reports covering money and credit, financial markets, real economy, prices, plus a two-year-ahead forecast). Target is above zero to keep a buffer against deflation and below levels that make inflation volatile; policy aims at inflation about two years ahead because of lags. Exceptions: Bank of Japan (persistent deflation, no formal target) and the Fed (dual mandate; watches core PCE around 2%).
- **Exchange-rate targeting (peg, band, managed float, dollarization)**: "import" the anchor country's inflation; defending the peg forces domestic rates up when domestic inflation exceeds the anchor's (sell FX reserves, buy domestic currency, tighten) and down in the opposite case. Interest rates and money supply become subordinate and volatile; credibility is required or speculators attack (sterling leaving the ERM in 1992; Thai baht 1997).
- **Neutral rate and stance**: `neutral = real trend growth + long-run expected inflation (target)`. Policy rate above neutral is contractionary, below is expansionary; neutral should match the average policy rate over a cycle; setting policy at neutral is consistent with stable inflation. Neutral is hard to estimate and moves (lower trend growth after leveraged-credit busts).
- **Shock diagnosis**: demand-pull inflation (confidence-driven spending) justifies tightening; a supply shock (oil) does not, because higher rates deepen the slump while the price effect is one-off.
- **Limits of monetary policy**: unstable transmission and velocity; cannot force banks to lend or the public to deposit; liquidity trap (demand for money infinitely elastic, extra reserves just held, usually in deflation); zero (or slightly negative) lower bound for nominal rates; deflation raises real debt, encourages postponed spending, creates a trap (Japan after 1990); emerging-market obstacles (illiquid bond and interbank markets, unknown neutral rate, financial innovation, poor record, weak independence).
- **Unconventional policy**: forward guidance (promise to keep rates low long) and quantitative easing (large-scale purchases of government or private securities, creating reserves; BoE bought gilts, Fed bought MBS and Treasuries across QE1 to QE3). Risks: banks may hoard reserves; credit losses can impair central-bank credibility; large government purchases amount to monetization of deficits (independence becomes questionable).
- **Fiscal policy purposes**: influence aggregate demand and output; redistribute income and wealth; allocate resources. Expansionary: lower taxes (income, indirect, corporate, savings) or more spending; contractionary: opposite. Keynesians: potent when spare capacity; monetarists: temporary effect, prefer monetary tools; Ricardian equivalence: households save the tax cut in anticipation of future taxes, so no demand effect.
- **Fiscal tools**: spending (transfers excluded from GDP and from G; current spending; capital expenditure) and revenue (direct: income, wealth, corporate, payroll, capital gains, inheritance; indirect: excise, VAT/sales). Speed: indirect taxes change fastest; direct taxes and transfers need months; capital spending takes years but raises productive capacity. Tax-policy attributes: simplicity, efficiency, fairness (horizontal and vertical equity), revenue sufficiency (these conflict).
- **Fiscal multiplier**: with MPC `c` and net tax rate `t`: `multiplier = 1/[1 − c(1 − t)]`; without taxes `1/(1 − c)`. Spending raises output by more than a tax cut or transfer of equal size because part of the latter is saved (the IMF study: government spending much more potent than non-targeted transfers; targeted transfers to the poor roughly double the non-targeted ones). Balanced-budget multiplier (G and T both up by the same amount, lump-sum) equals 1.
- **Deficit, debt and stance**: deficit `= G − T + B` (B transfers); debt is accumulated deficits. Headline deficits mislead because automatic stabilizers move them; use the structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit (what it would be at full employment) and consider real, inflation-adjusted interest. Automatic stabilizers act without legislation and shrink the multiplier; discretionary changes need decisions. A budget move is expansionary only if it raises the structural deficit.
- **Debt-sustainability logic**: the debt ratio worsens if the real interest rate on debt exceeds real growth (with a zero primary balance, `Δ(D/Y) ≈ (r − g)·(D/Y)`); inflation erodes the real value of debt, deflation does not. Arguments for concern: higher future tax rates and disincentives, monetization risk, crowding out of private investment. Arguments against: debt owed domestically, funds invest in capital or human capital, high unemployment means no diversion of resources, Ricardian equivalence, tax reforms can reduce distortions. Short-run crowding out may be small; long-run capital accumulation suffers.
- **Implementation difficulties (fiscal)**: recognition lag, action lag, impact lag (monetary has them too); unknown or shifting full-employment level; forecast error and surprise shocks; private-sector reaction; inflation and unacceptable-deficit constraints; if slack is supply-side (labor shortage) demand stimulus only adds inflation; politics (easier to loosen than tighten; automatic stabilizers do most of the tightening).
- **Policy-mix matrix**:

| Fiscal | Monetary | Interest rates | Output | Sector sizes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | Easy | Lower (if monetary effect dominates) | Strongly up | Public and private grow |
| Easy | Tight | Higher | Up (private demand crowded) | Public share up |
| Tight | Easy | Lower | Private-led | Private share up, public down |
| Tight | Tight | Higher | Down | Both shrink |

  - Tight fiscal plus easy monetary favors private investment and potential growth; investment-oriented governments prefer it. Fiscal multipliers are much larger when monetary policy accommodates (IMF: cumulative multiplier for government spending 3.9 with accommodation vs 1.6 without). Persistent deficits lift real rates and inflation expectations, cutting the stimulus by roughly half and, if debt rises permanently, lowering potential output. Credibility and commitment to discipline matter.

## Key Concepts
- **Barter and double coincidence of wants**: the inefficiency money removes.
- **Legal tender / fiat money**: accepted by law, value by decree and acceptance.
- **Excess reserves**: reserves above the requirement; they support new loans.
- **Policy (official) rate; repo rate; base rate; discount rate; fed funds rate**.
- **Operational vs target independence**: how vs what the central bank decides.
- **Money neutrality**: long-run irrelevance of money for real variables.
- **Liquidity trap**: horizontal money-demand curve where extra money does not lower rates.
- **Quantitative easing; monetization**: asset purchases at scale; central bank financing the deficit.
- **Menu costs; shoe-leather costs**: costs of expected inflation.
- **Automatic stabilizer vs discretionary policy**.
- **Structural (cyclically adjusted) budget balance**: deficit at full employment.
- **Crowding out**: government borrowing raises rates and displaces private investment.
- **Ricardian equivalence**: private saving offsets deficit-financed tax cuts.
- **Fiscal multiplier; balanced-budget multiplier**.
- **Neutral rate**: policy rate that neither stimulates nor restrains.
- **Bond market vigilantes**: bond investors who push long yields up when policy looks inflationary.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- Money multiplier `= 1/R`; money created from new deposit `= D/R`
- `M·V = P·Y`; `M/P = kY`, `k = 1/V`
- Fisher: `R_nom = R_real + E(π) (+ risk premium)`
- Neutral rate `= real trend growth + inflation target`
- Policy rate > neutral: contractionary; < neutral: expansionary
- Fiscal multiplier `= 1/[1 − c(1 − t)]`; no taxes `1/(1 − c)`; balanced-budget multiplier `= 1`
- Disposable income `YD = (1 − t)·Y`; MPC with taxes `= c(1 − t)`
- Government budget balance `= T − G − B`; debt-ratio drift `≈ (r − g)(D/Y) − primary balance` (r and g real)

| Tool | Expansionary move | Contractionary move |
|---|---|---|
| Open market operations | Buy bonds (add reserves) | Sell bonds |
| Policy rate | Cut | Raise |
| Reserve requirement | Lower | Raise |
| QE | Large asset purchases | Tapering, balance-sheet runoff |
| Fiscal | Tax cut, more G or transfers | Tax rise, less G |

## Mental Models
- **Stance = policy rate minus neutral**, not policy rate versus last year's rate.
- **Credibility is an instrument**: anchored expectations let one rate move do more work.
- **Fiscal stance = change in the structural balance**, never the raw deficit.
- **Diagnose the shock before choosing the response**: demand shocks answer to rates; supply shocks mostly do not.
- **Multipliers are state-dependent**: large with slack and accommodating money, small near full employment or when monetary policy offsets.
- **Zero bound means switch tools**: forward guidance, QE, fiscal.

## Worked Example
**1. Money creation (recomputed).** 10% reserve: €100 deposit gives deposits 100 + 90 + 81 + … = €1,000 (multiplier 10). At 5% reserves the same €100 supports €2,000.

**2. Fisher.** Required real return 2%, expected inflation 2%: T-bill yield 4%. If expected inflation rises to 3% the yield must rise to about 5% (exact: `1.02 × 1.03 − 1 = 5.06%`).

**3. Neutral rate and stance.** Trend growth 3%, target 2%: neutral 5%. A 4% policy rate is expansionary; 6% contractionary. With trend 2.5% and target 2%, neutral is 4.5%.

**4. Fiscal multiplier.** `c = 0.9`, `t = 0.20`: `1/[1 − 0.9 × 0.8] = 1/0.28 = 3.57`; extra G of 1bn lifts output by 3.57bn. With `c = 0.8, t = 0.25`: 2.5; no taxes: 5 (`c = 0.8`).

**5. Balanced budget.** Lump-sum tax and G both up 200 with c = 0.9: first-round demand change 200 − 180 = +20, which the 1/(1 − c) = 10 multiplier turns into +200 of output: multiplier 1. Cross-check: G-only effect 2,000, tax-only effect −1,800, net +200.

**6. Debt dynamics (recomputed).** Debt/GDP 100%, primary balance 0: with real rate 3% and growth 2%, the ratio reaches 134% after 30 years; with rate 2% and growth 3% it falls to 75%; with r 3%, g 2% and a 1% of GDP primary surplus the ratio stays near 99%. Stabilizing surplus now `≈ (r − g)/(1 + g) × 100% = 0.98%` of GDP.

**7. Policy-mix reading.** Fiscal expansion plus central-bank tightening: higher output (if fiscal dominates), higher rates, larger government share, crowded-out private investment: government share grows relative to private.

## Anti-patterns
- Raising rates against a supply-driven inflation spike without checking expectations: deepens the downturn.
- Treating a bigger deficit as automatic proof of fiscal expansion.
- Comparing money aggregates across countries without checking definitions.
- Assuming the money multiplier is fixed; excess reserves and cash leakage lower it, and banks can hoard under stress.
- Assuming long rates follow policy rates one for one.
- Targeting zero inflation (deflation risk, no room to cut) or assuming QE always raises lending.
- Ignoring implementation lags; late stimulus can arrive in the recovery.
- Expecting tax cuts to work when Ricardian households save them.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- Money multiplier is inversely related to reserve requirement; new deposit D creates `D/R` (not `D × R`).
- Speculative money demand moves inversely with returns on other assets (not with their risk); transactions and precautionary demand move with GDP.
- Central banks most dislike unexpected moderate or high inflation (uncertainty costs); policy at neutral promotes stable inflation, not balanced budgets or employment.
- Neutral rate uses trend growth plus inflation target; classify stance by comparing the policy rate with it.
- A central bank that sets its own target, definition and horizon is target independent and operationally independent; one given a target is operationally independent only.
- Under an exchange-rate target, if domestic inflation exceeds the anchor's, short rates rise and reserves fall (not rise).
- Monetary tools versus fiscal: transfer payments, public financing of a power plant, tax changes are fiscal; mortgage deposit requirements, reserve requirements, open market purchases are monetary or macroprudential.
- Indirect taxes (excise, VAT) are not payroll or employment taxes (direct); indirect taxes change fastest, capital spending slowest.
- Fiscal multiplier uses `c(1 − t)`; balanced-budget multiplier is 1.
- Government spending has a bigger impact than tax cuts or non-targeted transfers; targeted transfers to the poorest have larger impact than broad ones; fiscal multipliers are larger with accommodative money.
- Contractionary fiscal policy means a fall in the deficit or rise in the surplus, not necessarily lower spending; a pay-as-you-go rule is neutral; a freeze on discretionary spending is contractionary.
- Cyclically adjusted (structural) deficit is the appropriate indicator of fiscal stance.
- Ricardian equivalence leads governments toward monetary policy.

## Key Takeaways
1. Money is medium of exchange, store of value and unit of account; banks create deposits up to `1/R` times reserves (a ceiling).
2. `MV = PY`; money is neutral in the long run (prices only) but central banks act as if it is not neutral in the short run; Fisher: nominal = real + expected inflation (+ risk premium).
3. Unexpected inflation is the costly kind; central banks therefore aim at low, stable, credible inflation (target about 2%, above zero).
4. Tools: open market operations, policy rate via repo, reserve requirements; transmission runs through lending rates, asset prices, expectations and the exchange rate.
5. Stance is the policy rate versus neutral (trend growth plus target); identify demand versus supply shock before acting.
6. Limits: liquidity trap, zero bound, uncontrollable bank money creation; QE and forward guidance are the fallback, with monetization and balance-sheet risks.
7. Fiscal multiplier `1/[1 − c(1 − t)]`; spending beats tax cuts; the structural balance measures stance; debt worsens when real rates exceed growth.
8. Fiscal policy is hampered by three lags, forecast error, crowding out and politics; mix (easy/tight combinations) determines composition of demand and rates.

# Chapter 10: Economics of Regulation
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2022, Level II, Reading 8 · Economics | Chester S. Spatt, PhD (regulatory-analysis content contributed by Christopher Decker, University of Oxford)

## Core Idea
Competitive markets are efficient only if there are no frictions, no externalities and constant returns to scale (fundamental theorem of welfare economics). Regulation exists where those conditions fail: informational frictions (adverse selection, moral hazard), externalities (pollution, systemic risk), weak competition and social objectives. Regulators choose among price tools (taxes, subsidies), mandates and restrictions, public goods and public financing, and should judge them by cost-benefit analysis, direct and indirect. For an analyst the job is different: estimate the probability a proposed rule is adopted, then its effect on revenues, costs and business risk, and put it into forecasts and the discount rate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rationale for regulation (when markets fail)**: Welfare theorem: with constant returns, no frictions and no externalities, competitive equilibrium is Pareto optimal (nobody can be made better off without hurting someone) and regulation is needed only for consumer protection and privacy. When to use: to classify why a rule exists. How: test four failure sources.
  - *Informational frictions*: adverse selection (private information lets the informed gain at others' expense) and moral hazard (incentive conflicts from delegation or from contracts, including insurance). Regulatory response: rights and responsibilities, a level playing field in information (disclosure, insider-trading bans).
  - *Externalities*: spillover costs (pollution, systemic risk, contagion) or benefits (neighbors gain from a renovation). Response: taxes, subsidies, cap and trade, mandates.
  - *Weak competition*: dominant firm or collusion gives high prices, less choice, less innovation. Response: antitrust.
  - *Social objectives*: public goods (non-rival consumption: defense, police, education) or obligations on firms (discounts for vulnerable customers, universal service in remote areas).
- **Classification of regulation by objective**: safety, privacy, protection (intellectual property), environmental, labor/employment, commerce/trade (consumer and investor protection, antitrust), financial system (prudential supervision, capital requirements, insider trading).
- **Financial-market regulation**: purposes are investor and consumer protection, confidence and capital formation, market integrity (efficient, informed, non-exploited markets), safety and soundness of institutions, smooth payment systems, access to credit; central banks add price stability, employment, growth. Three families: (1) disclosure (accounting standards, prospectuses, proxy, mutual-fund and price-transparency rules; MiFID II, 2018, opened fixed-income, derivatives and OTC markets and requires full disclosure of costs, charges and third-party payments with an illustration of their cumulative effect on return); (2) agency-conflict rules (fund fees and governance, proxy voting, best execution, inducements); (3) prudential supervision (capital adequacy, liquidity, diversification, risk monitoring, deposit/insurance funds funded by mandatory premiums, recovery and resolution plans). Retail investors get the focus; hedge funds, private equity and venture funds, with institutional or affluent investors, get a "buyer beware" stance, lighter disclosure (offering memoranda) and antifraud rules. Market-integrity regulation (process) differs from financial-stability regulation (outcomes). Costs: mandated insurance creates moral hazard; higher capital requirements reduce capital available to lend.
- **Regulation of commerce**: frames contracting and coordination. Global externalities (climate, nuclear waste) need international mechanisms because national governments coordinate best within their borders. Areas: trade agreements, legal framework for contracts, labor rights and safety, immigration, product safety (drugs, food, devices), intellectual property (country-specific; weak global enforcement), technical standards and domain names, data privacy (GDPR; proposed e-Privacy Regulation). A predictable legal environment (contract law, liability, bankruptcy) is needed for long-term commitments with sunk costs: without it the counterparty can exploit the **holdout problem** and renegotiate once costs are sunk.
- **Antitrust framework**: goal is to promote competition (preventing activities that restrict or distort it). Targets: (1) mergers that substantially reduce competition (regulatory approval or notification; regulators may block, require remedies such as divestiture, and decide between competing bids); (2) anticompetitive arrangements (price collusion, cartels, exchange of sensitive information; individuals can face prison in the US, UK, Germany, Denmark, Ireland, France, Australia); (3) abuse by dominant firms: exclusive dealing, refusal to deal, price discrimination, predatory pricing. Evaluate a strategy's antitrust risk by: market power of the firm, whether the practice raises rivals' costs or excludes them, the consumer-benefit defense (low prices, quality), and the number of jurisdictions that must agree (US DOJ, EU, others; a unified global strategy is usually advantageous). Cases: Google (own shopping service favored) and Intel (exclusive dealing) in the EU; competition law can also be a competitive weapon (Microsoft v Google). Privacy and data protection are not antitrust.
- **Classification of regulators**: (a) *government departments/agencies* (state-funded, legal authority within a mandate); (b) *independent regulators* (statute-backed autonomy, typically fee-funded, less political pressure; e.g., UK Financial Conduct Authority, PCAOB, overseen by the SEC); (c) *industry self-regulatory bodies* (private, represent and regulate members, authority from members' consent, can expel, no force of law); (d) *self-regulating organizations (SROs)*: self-regulatory bodies recognized and given enforcement authority by government, funded independently (FINRA under the SEC). Role of SROs varies: strong in the US and common-law UK; limited in civil-law Europe and removed in Australia (2001). Regulators worry about SRO governance and conflicts of interest. Regulators also reference outside bodies (IASB, FASB, credit-rating agencies) and enforce their standards; rating references are being reduced because of paid-by-issuer conflicts.
- **Classification of regulations**: statutes (laws by legislatures, broad), administrative regulations (rules by agencies that fill in detail), judicial law (court interpretation and sanctions). Also substantive law (rights, responsibilities, relationships) versus procedural law (protection and enforcement). EU: regulations are binding as national law (like administrative regulations), directives set outcomes that member states legislate (like statutes), decisions bind specific parties (like judicial law). Some bodies are ambiguous (Singapore statutory boards such as ACRA).
- **Regulatory interdependencies**: (1) *Regulatory capture* (Stigler 1971): regulation advances the interests of the regulated, via entry barriers, coordinated quality or price rules, industry expertise, revolving doors; most associated with SRO reliance. (2) *Regulatory competition*: regulators bid for firms with friendlier rules; extreme form is a "race to the bottom". (3) *Regulatory arbitrage*: firms exploit differences in economic substance versus regulatory treatment across regimes. (4) *Cooperation* on global issues (systemic risk, money laundering, terrorism financing, climate): Basel Committee (bank capital and risk-management standards), IOSCO (principles for securities regulation; members are national regulators; a standard setter, not a regulatory authority). (5) *Conflicting objectives among domestic regulators*: bank supervisors may withhold stress-test results to protect stability while securities regulators want disclosure for investors. Climate illustrates difficulty of designing permits and equity across countries.
- **Regulatory toolkit**: price mechanisms (taxes, subsidies, cap and trade); restrictions (insider trading, short-selling limits); mandates (bank capital, registration); provision of public goods; public financing of private projects. Coase theorem: if the externality can be traded and transaction costs are zero, the allocation is efficient regardless of initial assignment of rights (the initial allocation of permits still matters for who pays, and historical-use allocation distorts incentives). Systemic risk and contagion are negative externalities; the European Systemic Risk Board advises national macroprudential bodies. **Bail-in**: shareholders are wiped out and creditor claims cancelled or reduced so taxpayers do not pay and the bank keeps operating (contrast with 2008 bailouts). Effectiveness is hard to prove: future crises differ, absence of a crisis is not proof of success, rules may shift risk elsewhere. Several options usually exist for one problem (conflict of interest: bar, divest, disclose; insiders: trading ban, mandatory disclosure of trades, company blackout periods). Desirable regulatory environment is stable (predictable, effective, time-consistent, enforceable), not unchanging. Enforcement: fines on the firm are weak when shareholders are the victims (they pay twice); individuals are hard to prosecute (detection, reputational incentives to fight, indemnification funded by the firm).
- **Cost-benefit analysis (regulator's view)**: regulatory burden = private costs of the regulated entity; **net regulatory burden = private costs − private benefits**. Costs: direct implementation (compliance staff) and indirect (altered decisions and allocations), often larger. "Unintended" splits into unanticipated implementation costs and indirect costs from unintended consequences; if identified in consultation filings beforehand, they are not truly unanticipated; they reflect policy risk. Retrospective (before and after) analysis is easier than prospective; pilots, natural experiments, phase-ins and regulatory sandboxes (UK, Singapore, Australia, UAE, Malaysia) generate data, best for high-frequency trading rules (short sales, post-trade reporting, tick size). US administrative law requires CBA and courts have struck down rules for inadequate analysis (2004 SEC mutual-fund independent chair rule; 2011 proxy-access rule); the EU and Australia require impact assessments. Illustration: EU SME-listing proposal tested against a baseline of unchanged MiFID II and MAR (targeted change versus overhaul versus new provisions such as simpler delisting, easy transfer of listings, lower free float); benefits are capital access and growth, costs are extra oversight and confidence spillover from small-firm failures to other firms' cost of capital.
- **Analyst's regulatory impact framework (three steps)**: (1) *Likelihood of change*: regulator's intent, its CBA framework, engagement with the industry, public and political pressure. (2) *Sector impact*: revenues (price/tariff/fee caps, bans, mandated warnings, allowed rate of return for natural monopolies, workaround revenue such as fees replacing banned commissions, price transparency), costs (opex or capex, labor-hours rules, compliance, data protection, pass-through depending on competitive position, lost flexibility), business risk (fines, compensation, activity bans: assign probabilities or add a risk premium to the discount rate or accept a lower multiple). (3) *Valuation and recommendation*. Question checklist: design (exemptions, thresholds, uniform or national tailoring, sunset), participant impact (who gains, scale, one-off or recurring, entry or exit, crowding out bank finance), wider impact (stability, diversification, spillovers, macro). The regulator's legal status is the least relevant question for the analyst.
- **Regulation changes business models (examples)**: Regulation NMS (2005) ended NYSE's quasi-monopoly on liquidity and fragmented trading; Regulation Q (deposit-rate ceiling) created money market funds in the early 1970s; the 2008 Reserve Fund run led to government insurance and a rise in the FDIC limit from USD 100,000 to 250,000; natural monopolies in transmission and distribution (utilities) require regulated prices and returns, especially when adjacent stages are competitive.

## Key Concepts
- **Pareto optimal**: no reallocation can improve one agent without worsening another; competitive equilibrium without frictions achieves it.
- **Informational friction**: lack of or inadequate information that causes sub-optimal outcomes (adverse selection, moral hazard).
- **Externality**: spillover cost or benefit on third parties to a transaction.
- **Public good**: consumption by one does not reduce availability to others (non-rival); usually government-funded.
- **Systemic risk**: risk of failure of the financial system; its realization via spread of shocks is contagion (a negative externality).
- **Prudential supervision**: monitoring the safety and soundness of institutions to protect customers and stability; part of prudential regulation (capital adequacy and liquidity standards).
- **Market integrity regulation**: ensures efficient, transparent markets and informed investors; distinct from financial stability.
- **Self-regulating organization (SRO)**: self-regulatory body recognized by government with enforcement power, independently funded.
- **Independent regulator**: autonomous body with statutory authority, usually fee-funded, not a government agency per se.
- **Regulatory capture**: regulation that benefits the regulated.
- **Regulatory arbitrage**: exploiting differences between economic substance and regulatory treatment across regimes.
- **Regulatory competition / race to the bottom**: jurisdictions lowering standards to attract firms.
- **Net regulatory burden**: private costs minus private benefits of regulation.
- **Bail-in**: loss absorption by shareholders and creditors rather than taxpayers.
- **Substantive vs procedural law**: rights and responsibilities versus enforcement of them.
- **Regulatory sandbox**: controlled live trial of a rule or business model with limited scope.
- **Holdout problem**: counterparty exploits sunk costs to force renegotiation.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `Net regulatory burden = private costs of regulation − private benefits of regulation`
- `Total cost of regulation = direct implementation costs + indirect costs (changed behavior, misallocation) ± unintended consequences (policy risk)`
- Compound annual growth for a policy-response comparison: `CAGR = (V_end / V_start)^(1/years) − 1`
- Valuation channel: regulatory risk enters via expected cash flow (probability-weighted) or via the discount rate/multiple: `V = Σ CF_t / (1 + r + regulatory risk premium)^t`

## Mental Models
- **Use "which friction?" first**: map any rule to information, externality, competition or social objective before judging it.
- **Use the three-step filter for investments**: probability of adoption, then revenue/cost/risk effect, then valuation (cash flow or discount rate).
- **Use "who is captured and who competes?"** to predict drift: SRO reliance raises capture risk; light-touch jurisdictions create arbitrage and a race to the bottom.
- **Use indirect costs over compliance headcount** when judging net burden: behavior change and misallocation dominate.
- **Use retrospective evidence over forecasts**: regulators' prospective claims are weak; sandboxes and phase-ins give data.

## Worked Example
Genovia electricity generation (TWh): fossil 462 → 426, hydro 186 → 273, other renewables 97 → 154 over two years (totals 745 → 853, consistent). Question: which policy (fossil tax, hydro subsidy, renewables subsidy) was most effective?
- Steps: `CAGR = (Y2/Y0)^0.5 − 1`. Fossil: `(426/462)^0.5 − 1 = −3.98%`; hydro: `(273/186)^0.5 − 1 = +21.15%`; other renewables: `(154/97)^0.5 − 1 = +26.00%`; total `(853/745)^0.5 − 1 = +7.00%`.
- Result: the subsidy on other renewables gives the highest growth, so it ranks as most effective by this metric.
- Interpretation and caveat: this is a growth-rate ranking on different bases; in absolute terms hydropower added 87 TWh versus 57 TWh for other renewables, and fossil fell 36 TWh. The data also cannot isolate the policy effect (no counterfactual), echoing the reading's point that effectiveness is hard to establish even after the fact.

Analyst application (illustrative): for a proposed tariff cap, the analyst first estimates the probability of adoption, then cuts forecast revenue only for the firms the cap binds on, classifies any compliance spending as one-off capex or recurring opex, and, if fine or litigation risk cannot be quantified, applies a lower multiple versus peers or a higher discount rate.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating extensive voluntary disclosure as a reason for regulation (it is an outcome of regulation or good practice).
- Counting only compliance headcount as the cost of a rule; ignoring indirect behavioral costs.
- Concluding a rule worked because no crisis followed.
- Classifying SRO, independent regulator and government agency interchangeably.
- Assuming sanctions on a company always punish wrongdoers when shareholders are the victims.
- Ignoring jurisdiction hopping and arbitrage when a country adopts rules ahead of peers.
- Assuming all firms in a sector bear a regulation equally.

## Exam Focus & Traps
| Trap | Correct reading |
|---|---|
| Extensive disclosure as a rationale | Not a reason for regulation; informational frictions and systemic risk are |
| Prudential supervision | Safety and soundness of financial institutions (not inducements, not retail product information) |
| Independent regulator vs SRO vs agency | Independent regulator: statutory authority, fee-funded, autonomous; SRO: private body given enforcement power by government; self-regulatory body: authority from members, no force of law |
| SRO tools | Behavior restrictions and standards; price mechanisms are least likely |
| Regulator easing listing rules to attract firms | Regulatory competition (not coordination or capture) |
| Capture concern | Highest with SRO reliance |
| Country imposes stricter rules than peers | Risk of regulatory arbitrage / business flowing out |
| Blackout periods | Set by companies, not regulators |
| Capital requirements | Government tool against systemic risk; insider-trading restrictions answer informational frictions |
| Hiring compliance lawyers | Unanticipated implementation cost; contract and employer-rights standards are intended effects |
| Regulation Q (deposit ceiling) | Raises demand for money market funds |
| Cost-benefit analysis scope | Both implementation and indirect costs; easier retrospectively |
| Analyst vs regulator CBA | Analyst cares about company cost, revenue and risk, not the regulator's CBA method or legal form |
| Antitrust scope | Mergers, collusion, dominant-firm abuse; not privacy |
| Free-trade objective | Floating currency fits; tariffs and subsidies restrict trade |
| Courts and CBA | US courts have overturned SEC rules for inadequate cost-benefit analysis |
| Sunk costs and long-term contracts | Strong liability, contract and bankruptcy law encourages long-term commitments by foreign firms |

## Key Takeaways
1. Regulation addresses failures of the welfare theorem: information problems, externalities, weak competition and social objectives.
2. Financial regulation targets disclosure, agency conflicts and prudential safety; market integrity and financial stability are different aims and can conflict.
3. Regulators are government agencies, independent regulators, self-regulatory bodies or SROs; regulations are statutes, administrative rules or judicial law.
4. Regulatory capture, competition and arbitrage describe interdependence; Basel and IOSCO provide coordination without a global regulator.
5. Tools: taxes and subsidies, restrictions, mandates, public goods and financing; bail-in shifts failure costs from taxpayers to investors.
6. Net regulatory burden includes indirect costs; retrospective analysis and sandboxes improve evidence.
7. Analysts assess likelihood, then revenue, cost and risk effects, then value the firm; uncertain regulatory risk belongs in probabilities, multiples or the discount rate.
8. Stable (predictable, enforceable) rules support long-term investment; unstable rules raise the required return.

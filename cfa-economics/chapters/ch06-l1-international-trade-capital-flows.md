# Chapter 6: International Trade and Capital Flows
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2020, Level I, Reading 17 · Economics | Usha Nair-Reichert, PhD; Daniel Robert Witschi, PhD, CFA

## Core Idea
Countries gain from trade by specializing where their **opportunity cost** is lowest (comparative advantage), even if they are worse at everything in absolute terms. Trade barriers (tariffs, quotas, VERs, export subsidies) shrink total welfare and differ mainly in who captures the rents. The balance of payments is a double-entry ledger, and its macro identity `CA = Sp + Sg − I` tells you that a current-account deficit is always a savings shortfall financed by foreign capital, which is why it matters for currencies, risk premia and country selection.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Comparative advantage test (opportunity-cost method)**: the country with the lower opportunity cost of a good has the comparative advantage in it.
  - When to use: any "who should export what" question, with or without absolute advantage.
  - How: (1) per worker-day output table; (2) opportunity cost of good A in units of B = output of B / output of A; (3) the country with the smaller number exports A; (4) mutually beneficial terms of trade lie strictly between the two autarkic opportunity costs; (5) the closer the world price is to the *partner's* autarkic price, the larger your gain.
  - Failure mode: comparing raw output levels (absolute advantage). A country can lack absolute advantage in every good and still gain.
- **Ricardian model**: labor is the only variable factor; comparative advantage comes from **technology / labor productivity** differences. Small country may specialize completely; large one only incompletely.
- **Heckscher-Ohlin (factor proportions) model**: labor and capital both variable, **identical technology across countries**, differing factor endowments. A country exports goods intensive in its **relatively abundant factor**. Opening to trade raises the price of the export good, which raises the return to the abundant factor and cuts the return to the scarce factor (income redistribution). In the pure model free trade equalizes goods prices and therefore factor prices; in practice factor prices only converge partly.
  - Ricardo and H-O are complementary, not rival. Tastes and scale economies add more (intra-industry trade in monopolistic competition models).
- **Small-country tariff welfare analysis** (importer; world price P*, tariff price Pt = P* + t):
  - Consumer surplus −(A+B+C+D); producer surplus +A; tariff revenue +C; **deadweight loss = B + D** (B production inefficiency, D lost consumption).
  - When to use: sizing any tariff or quota question. Quota with rents captured abroad: loss = B + C + D. Quota with licenses auctioned by the importing government: loss = B + D, same as tariff.
  - Large-country exception: an importer big enough to move the world price can gain via better terms of trade if (1) no retaliation and (2) the terms-of-trade gain exceeds B + D. Global welfare still falls (the partner loses more).
- **Policy comparison matrix** (see tables below): tariff, import quota, VER, export subsidy.
- **Integration ladder**: free trade area (FTA) → customs union → common market → economic union → monetary union. Each rung adds one thing.
- **Trade creation vs trade diversion** (static effects of a customs union): net welfare gain only if creation exceeds diversion.
- **BOP identity and national accounts link**: `Y = C + I + G + X − M`, so `CA = X − M = Y − (C + I + G) = Sp + Sg − I`.
  - How: ask which of the three terms drives the deficit: low private saving, government deficit, or high investment.
  - Why it matters: if the deficit funds investment (I up) the repayment capacity grows; if it funds consumption it does not.
- **Capital controls toolkit**: price controls (taxes on foreign returns, taxes on transactions, unremunerated reserve requirements), quantity controls (ceilings, authorization for foreign borrowing), outright prohibitions, administrative approval.

## Key Concepts
- **GDP vs GNP**: GDP = output produced within borders by anyone; GNP = output by a country's citizens' factors wherever located. GDP is the standard measure of domestic activity.
- **Terms of trade**: export price index / import price index (index, base year = 100). Rising = improvement: fewer exports buy the same imports. Currency units cancel, so it is units of imports per unit of exports.
- **Net exports**: exports minus imports. Strictly the trade balance, loosely used as the current account.
- **Autarky / closed economy**: no trade; the **autarkic price** is the no-trade equilibrium price. **Open economy** trades at the **world price**.
- **Excess demand / excess supply**: at a world price below autarky the country imports the gap; above autarky it exports the surplus.
- **Free trade vs trade protection**: protection stops market forces from setting import/export quantities and prices.
- **Absolute advantage**: lower absolute resource cost per unit. **Comparative advantage**: lower opportunity cost.
- **Intra-industry trade**: two-way trade in the same product category, explained by product differentiation and scale economies.
- **FDI vs FPI**: FDI = long-term investment in productive assets abroad (makes the firm a multinational). FPI = shorter-term holdings of foreign securities, much more volatile and prone to capital flight.
- **Intra-firm trade**: trade between related subsidiaries (46% of US imports are between related parties).
- **Knowledge spillovers**: benefits of R&D or knowledge creation that reach other firms (about a quarter of a G-7 country's R&D benefit accrues to partners).
- **Small country (trade sense)**: price taker in the world market for the good, regardless of size. Brazil is a price taker in cars.
- **Quota rent**: excess profit from scarce import licenses, captured by foreign exporters, importers or the government depending on design.
- **Voluntary export restraint (VER)**: exporter-imposed quota, so the rents go to the exporter.
- **Countervailing duty**: duty an importer levies on subsidized imports.
- **Infant industry argument**: temporary protection until a new industry matures.
- **Regional trading agreement (RTA)**: members cut barriers among themselves, with preferential treatment versus non-members.
- **Capital flight**: rapid outflow of capital, typical when inflows were short-term portfolio money.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
```
Terms of trade = P_X / P_M       (export price index / import price index)
Opportunity cost of A in B = output_B / output_A        (per worker-day)
Y = C + I + G + X − M
CA = X − M = Y − (C + I + G)
Disposable income Yd = Y + R − T = C + Sp        (R net transfers, T taxes)
CA = Sp − I + (T − G − R) = Sp + Sg − I          (Sg = T − G − R, government saving)
Sp + Sg = I + CA
Sp = I + CA − Sg
Tariff (small country): ΔCS = −(A+B+C+D); ΔPS = +A; Rev = C = t × (Q3 − Q2); DWL = B + D
BOP: CA + KA + FA + statistical discrepancy = 0
```
Triangle geometry in the tariff diagram: A = rectangle `t × Q1` plus triangle `½ t (Q2 − Q1)`; B = `½ t (Q2 − Q1)`; C = `t (Q3 − Q2)`; D = `½ t (Q4 − Q3)`.

**Policy comparison (small country unless stated)**

| | Import tariff | Import quota | VER | Export subsidy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic price | up | up | up | up (exporter's home market) |
| Consumer surplus | down | down | down | down |
| Producer surplus | up | up | up | up |
| Government revenue | up | mixed (license auction yes, foreign capture no) | none (rent abroad) | falls (spending rises) |
| Trade | imports down | imports down | imports down | exports up |
| National welfare | down (large country: may rise) | down, usually more than tariff | down | down, more for large country (world price falls) |

**Integration ladder**

| Stage | Internal free trade in goods | Common external trade policy | Free movement of labor and capital | Common institutions and policy coordination | Common currency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FTA (NAFTA) | yes | no | no | no | no |
| Customs union (Benelux) | yes | yes | no | no | no |
| Common market (MERCOSUR) | yes | yes | yes | no | no |
| Economic union (EU) | yes | yes | yes | yes | no |
| Monetary union (euro area) | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |

**BOP structure**

| Account | Contents |
|---|---|
| Current | merchandise trade; services (tourism, transport, licensing fees); income receipts (dividends, interest, profits of foreign-owned plants); unilateral transfers (remittances, aid) |
| Capital | capital transfers (debt forgiveness, migrants' transfers); purchases/sales of non-produced, non-financial assets (patents, mineral rights, leases) |
| Financial | claims on foreigners (reserve assets, gold, FX, FDI, securities, bank claims) and foreign claims on residents |

Double-entry convention: **debit** = increase in assets or decrease in liabilities; **credit** = decrease in assets or increase in liabilities. Exports of goods are credits (real asset leaves) with an offsetting debit (a claim or cash received). Imports are debits with an offsetting credit.

## Mental Models
- **Trade follows opportunity cost, never absolute productivity.** Always convert an output table into opportunity costs first.
- **Every CA deficit has a mirror financing inflow.** Ask "who is funding it, and in what form?" FDI and long-term debt are sticky. Short-term bank and portfolio money is the 1997 East Asia pattern: deficits financed by short-term foreign borrowing plus a peg ended in crisis.
- **Tariff vs quota = who gets rectangle C.** Government (tariff), foreign exporter (VER, or quota with foreign licensing), or whoever holds the licenses (quota). Check this before judging an investment in an importer: an importer holding licenses can be better off under a quota than a tariff, but under a VER the rent goes entirely abroad.
- **CA is the gap between national saving and investment.** Fiscal consolidation (spending cuts plus taxes) cuts imports and improves CA, all else equal. Booming investment widens the deficit without hurting repayment capacity.
- **Persistent deficit leads to a rising risk premium.** Short run: a strong economy, high rates and capital inflows can appreciate the currency. Long run: growing foreign claims demand a premium and the currency tends to depreciate.
- **Deeper integration costs policy independence.** Free capital and goods flows undermine independent price/quantity controls; in a monetary union devaluation is not available as an adjustment tool (Greece 2010).

## Worked Example
**1. Comparative advantage and gains (UK vs India, one worker-day).** UK: 4 machines or 8 yards of cloth. India: 2 machines or 16 yards.
- Opportunity cost of 1 machine: UK 8/4 = **2 yards**; India 16/2 = **8 yards**. UK has the comparative advantage in machines; India (1 yard = 1/8 machine vs UK 1/2) in cloth.
- Terms of trade must lie between 2 and 8 yards per machine. Take 1 machine = 4 yards.
- Autarky world output: 300 machines, 1,200 yards. Full specialization: UK 400 machines, India 1,600 yards (world 400 m and 1,600 y).
- UK exports 160 machines for 640 yards: consumes 240 m and 640 y (vs 200 m and 400 y, so +40 m and +240 y). India consumes 160 m and 960 y (+60 m and +160 y). Both gain, world gains 100 m and 400 y.
- Even if India made only 6 yards, opportunity cost of cloth would be 1/3 machine vs UK 1/2: India still has the comparative advantage though the UK has absolute advantage in both.

**2. Tariff on paper (small country).** Domestic demand 200k tons, supply 110k, world price $5, 20% tariff gives $6. After tariff supply is 130k, demand 170k, imports 40k.
- Consumer surplus loss = 1 × 170,000 + ½ × 1 × 30,000 = **$185,000**
- Producer surplus gain = 1 × 110,000 + ½ × 1 × 20,000 = **$120,000**
- Tariff revenue = 1 × 40,000 = **$40,000**
- Deadweight loss = 185,000 − 120,000 − 40,000 = **$25,000** (= ½×20,000 + ½×30,000).
- Interpretation: the country transfers $145k to producers and government but destroys $25k net. If it were a licensing quota with foreign rents, the loss would be $65k.

**3. BOP and the saving-investment identity.** Sp = 22, Sg = −4 (deficit), I = 20: CA = 22 − 4 − 20 = **−2**, financed by a financial-account inflow of 2. Cut the government deficit to zero and CA = +2 (twin-deficit logic).

## Anti-patterns
- **Using absolute productivity to predict trade direction**: ignores opportunity cost.
- **Assuming "gains from trade" means everyone wins**: winners could compensate losers, but import-competing workers can be permanently worse off.
- **Treating a quota like a tariff for welfare**: the rent may leave the country. A VER is the worst case for the importer.
- **Calling a large country's tariff a free lunch**: it needs no retaliation and ToT gain > DWL, and the world still loses.
- **Reading CA/FA signs mechanically**: a CA deficit shows up as a *positive* financial-account (net inflow) balance in the source's sign convention; financial outflows are shown as increases in claims (negative).
- **Equating trade diversion with creation**: replacing low-cost non-member imports with higher-cost member imports is diversion (bad); replacing high-cost domestic output with member imports is creation (good).
- **Evaluating capital controls on the crisis alone**: Malaysia 1998-2001 stabilized but was dropped from the MSCI developed index, raising the long-run cost of capital.
- **⚠ Source ambiguity**: the reading's wording of debit/credit examples (Exhibit 13 text) is loosely phrased ("payments received for exports" under debits). Rely on the asset/liability rule above: asset up or liability down = debit.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- **Which is the base of terms of trade?** Export over import prices. A rise is *better* (the oil exporters in 2007-08).
- **"Small" country** means price taker, not small GDP.
- **Tariff vs quota table**: quota and tariff identical on price/production/consumption; they differ only in rent C. A VER has the importer's welfare loss equal to a quota whose rent goes abroad.
- **Export subsidy hits the exporter's welfare**, and the large-country case is worse as its world price falls and part of the subsidy goes abroad.
- **Integration definitions are cumulative**: an FTA with separate external tariffs is NOT a customs union (Chile-Australia is an FTA). Customs union + factor mobility = common market. Common currency makes it monetary union; the euro area is a subset of the EU.
- **H-O vs Ricardo**: technology (Ricardo) vs factor endowments (H-O). Only H-O gives income redistribution and (in theory) factor price equalization.
- **CA formula**: `CA = Sp + Sg − I`. Sg = T − G − R. Government deficit alone (all else equal) widens the CA deficit.
- **Capital account is small**: the "capital account" in the narrow sense is capital transfers and non-produced assets; practitioners lump capital + financial accounts under "capital account".
- **Trade deficit means financing, not "loss"**: consumption-driven vs investment-driven matters for solvency.
- **IMF vs World Bank**: IMF = exchange-rate and BOP stability, temporary lending with conditionality. World Bank (IBRD, IDA) = long-term development.
- **Numbers to recall**: Bretton Woods 1944 (45 governments); IMF formal 1945; GATT 1947 (23 signatories); WTO 1 January 1995; Doha round launched Nov 2001 (stalled); China joined WTO Dec 2001; IBRD funds via AAA bonds; IDA replenished every 3 years with 35-40 year interest-free loans.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute opportunity costs, not output gaps; trade is gainful at any terms of trade between the two autarkic opportunity costs, and the gain grows as the world price moves away from autarky.
2. Ricardo = technology, H-O = endowments (plus income redistribution toward the abundant factor); real trade patterns reflect both, plus scale economies and tastes.
3. Small-country tariff: ΔCS = −(A+B+C+D), ΔPS = +A, revenue +C, DWL = B + D. Quota/VER rents go abroad unless licenses are auctioned.
4. Climb the integration ladder one feature at a time; gains depend on trade creation exceeding diversion, and deeper integration removes devaluation and independent monetary policy.
5. `CA = Sp + Sg − I`: a persistent deficit means foreigners are accumulating claims, so watch financing quality (FDI vs hot money) and risk-premium drift.
6. Capital controls buy time and policy independence (Malaysia) but impose administrative costs, signaling costs and index-status risk.
7. Know institutional roles: IMF (liquidity, surveillance, conditionality), World Bank (development, AAA supranational bond issuer), WTO (rules, disputes, negotiations).

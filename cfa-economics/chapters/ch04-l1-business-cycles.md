# Chapter 4: Understanding Business Cycles
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2020, Level I, Reading 15 · Economics | Michele Gambera, Milton Ezrati, Bolong Cao

## Core Idea
Business cycles are recurrent-but-not-periodic swings of aggregate activity (trough, expansion, peak, contraction) that hit most sectors at about the same time. The skill is to locate the economy in the cycle by combining resource-use patterns (inventories, capex, hours, housing, trade), unemployment/inflation readings and leading/coincident/lagging indicators, and to know which school of thought produces which policy prescription. Markets move before the data: equities typically bottom 3-6 months before the economy does.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- describe the cycle and its phases; how resource use, housing and external trade vary through it
- describe theories of the cycle (neoclassical, Austrian, Keynesian, Monetarist, New Classical/RBC, New Keynesian, Minsky)
- describe types of unemployment and compare unemployment measures
- explain inflation, hyperinflation, disinflation, deflation
- explain construction of price indexes; compare inflation measures (uses, limitations)
- distinguish cost-push from demand-pull inflation
- interpret a set of economic indicators (leading, coincident, lagging) and their limits

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Burns and Mitchell definition**: expansions occur at about the same time in many activities, followed by general contractions and revivals; the sequence is recurrent but not periodic; duration 1 to 10-12 years.
  - When to use: to reject calendar "rules of thumb" and to remember that cycles differ in length and intensity.
  - How: four phases (trough, expansion, peak, contraction); peak and trough are turning points; "boom" is the late, overheating part of expansion; "recession" = contraction, "depression" = exceptionally severe one.
  - Failure mode: the popular rule "two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP" misses a negative/slightly positive/negative sequence. The NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee instead looks at employment, industrial production, sales (and GDP), and dates turns with a long delay because data are revised for up to a year. Without such a committee use GDP growth, unemployment, industrial production and inflation, not interest rates, money supply or stocks.
- **Phase characteristics (Exhibit 1)**:

| Phase | Activity | Employment | Spending | Inflation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early expansion (recovery) | GDP, industrial production stabilize then rise | Layoffs slow, no net hiring yet; overtime and temps used; unemployment still high | Upturn first in housing, consumer durables, light producer equipment | Moderate, may still fall |
| Late expansion | Accelerating growth | Full-time rehiring, overtime up, unemployment falls | Broad-based; heavy equipment, construction ordered | Picks up modestly |
| Peak | Decelerating growth | Hiring slows, unemployment still falling but at a decreasing rate | Capex still strong but its growth rate slows | Accelerates further |
| Contraction | Outright declines | Hours cut and hiring frozen first, then layoffs; unemployment rises | Cuts in industrial production, housing, durables, equipment orders | Decelerates, with a lag |

- **Resource-use sequence through the cycle**:
  - Downturn: demand falls, inventories pile up, utilization drops, new orders for inventory and equipment stop, overtime goes, then headcount (retaining staff is cheaper than fire-and-rehire if the slowdown looks temporary). Banks tighten, a downward spiral can follow.
  - Turn: falling input prices and policy rates revive demand; low finished-goods stocks trigger production, then restocking, which lifts demand for intermediates; capex and then hiring follow.
  - Boom: shortages, wage pressure, overbuilding (fiber-optic in the 1990s, housing in the 2000s). Excess capacity is a typical trigger of the next recession.
- **Capital spending in three stages**: (1) early contraction: abrupt cuts, technology and light equipment first (short lead times), structures and heavy equipment later; (2) early expansion: capex rises on recovering cash flow, first orders are for fast-obsolescing efficiency gear (software, systems); watch capital-equipment orders (use "core" orders excluding defense and aircraft, which are lumpy and multi-year); (3) late expansion: orders for capacity (heavy equipment, warehouses, factories), placed early because of long delivery lags and because capacity statistics may not match current needs.
- **Inventory-sales ratio in three stages**: near the peak, sales slow faster than output so the ratio rises (masking weakness; use "final sales" excluding inventory change); firms then cut production below sales to destock, deepening the downturn; ratio falls back; when it normalizes, production rises even with weak sales, marking the cyclical turn; early in recovery sales outpace output, ratio falls, then a production surge restocks. Inventories are small but swing so violently they move GDP growth.
- **Consumer behavior**: households are ~70% of US GDP. Durables > non-durables > services in cyclicality (durables are postponable). Disposable (after-tax) income is a better guide than confidence surveys, which often misreport actual behavior. Permanent income (excludes temporary gains/losses) guides baseline consumption; durables follow disposable income regardless of source. Rising saving rate = caution (precautionary saving) in the short run, but a large stock of savings means capacity to recover.
- **Housing**: sensitive to mortgage rates; internal cycle driven by price-to-income and cost of owning. Late in expansions prices and rates rise disproportionately, sales slow, unsold inventory builds, construction falls; expected price gains pull in "late buyers", so overbuilding and a sharper correction (2008-09). Secular demand follows household formation (proxy: 25-40-year-old cohort growth), which governs how fast a market corrects excess (China vs Italy/Japan).
- **External trade**: imports follow domestic GDP growth; exports follow foreign GDP growth; net exports can offset or mask the domestic cycle. Currency appreciation makes imports cheaper and exports dearer; effects only matter when currency moves cumulate in one direction for a sustained period.
- **Schools of thought**:

| School | Cause of cycles | Policy prescription |
|---|---|---|
| Neoclassical | Flexible prices/wages clear markets (Say's law: supply creates its own demand); only temporary disequilibria; Schumpeter's creative destruction produces industry-level, not economy-wide, cycles | No intervention; cannot explain the Great Depression |
| Austrian (Hayek, Mises) | Low interest rates (often central-bank-driven) fuel credit growth and overinvestment in low-return projects; bust when rates rise; money and government matter | Limited intervention; let the adjustment (including wage and price falls) happen fast |
| Keynesian | Swings in aggregate demand; sticky nominal wages; wage cuts would shift AD further left; low business confidence ("animal spirits") can make low rates ineffective | Deficit-financed fiscal stimulus in serious crises (not continuous fine-tuning). Critiques: debt build-up, overheating later, implementation lags |
| Monetarist (Friedman) | Money supply growth (M2) too fast = boom/inflation, too slow = recession; policy lags make activism destabilizing | Steady, predictable money growth; minimal government role |
| New Classical / RBC (Lucas, Prescott, Kydland) | Rational agents optimize; cycles are efficient responses to real shocks (technology, input prices, tastes); money neutral; AS shifts matter | No discretionary policy (anticipated countercyclical policy can itself distort behavior) |
| New Keynesian (neo-Keynesian) | Micro-founded like New Classical but with sticky prices/wages (menu costs, wage stickiness, reorganization time); markets stay in disequilibrium | Policy can usefully speed return to equilibrium |
| Minsky | Long expansions breed complacency, risk premia too low, leverage, speculative credit; a "Minsky moment" triggers asset sell-offs and a credit crunch (2008 subprime) | Anticipate financial excess; precursor of behavioral finance |

  - Convergence: a unified DSGE-type framework now merges New Classical and New Keynesian approaches (Woodford). Keep personal politics out of the investment analysis.
- **Labor-market toolkit**: employed, labor force (employed + actively seeking), unemployed, long-term unemployed (>3-4 months), frictionally unemployed (between jobs, new/re-entrants; short-term), voluntarily unemployed (outside the labor force by choice), underemployed (employed, counted as employed), discouraged workers (stopped looking; outside the labor force).
  - Unemployment rate = unemployed / labor force. Activity (participation) ratio = labor force / working-age population (16-64).
  - Why unemployment lags: (a) the labor force shrinks in downturns (discouraged workers leave, flattering the rate) and expands in recoveries (returning seekers can lift the rate even as the economy improves); (b) firms hesitate to lay off and hire only after exhausting retained staff. Working-age-population denominators avoid (a) but create other biases (counts the disabled).
  - Better cyclical reads: payroll employment (coincident; misses small business), hours worked and overtime, temporary staffing, productivity (output per hour falls first as firms hoard labor, rebounds first in recovery).
  - Unemployment is highest at the start of the recovery, lowest at the peak; at the peak most unemployed are frictional.
- **Inflation vocabulary**: inflation = sustained rise in the aggregate price level; deflation = sustained fall (rate < 0%), raises real debt burdens and can spiral; disinflation = lower but still positive inflation; hyperinflation = e.g. 500-1000%+ per year, usually money-financed government spending (Germany 1923-24, Hungary 1946, Zimbabwe 2008); stagflation = high inflation with high unemployment and weak growth. Inflation is pro-cyclical with a lag of a year or more. Developed-market consensus target is about 2%.
- **Price index construction**: index = cost of basket in current period / cost in base period × 100; inflation = % change in the index.
  - Laspeyres (fixed base basket) has three upward biases: substitution, quality, new products. Fixes: hedonic pricing (quality), basket updates (new goods), chained indexes (substitution), Fisher index.
  - Indexes: CPI (CPI-U covers urban US households), PCE price index (broader coverage, Fisher-type, Fed's preferred since 2000), PPI/WPI (producer prices, by stage of processing, no retail margins, can lead CPI), HICP (ECB's target, aggregated by Eurostat with country weights), GDP deflator (domestic output prices; the right deflator for nominal GDP). Weights differ by country (food ~30-47% in China/India CPI vs 14-15% in Germany/US).
  - Headline (all items) vs core (excl. food and energy). Core is a cleaner signal of domestic trend; headline is the actual cost of living but noisy. Sub-indexes and relative prices matter for industry analysis (use CPI sub-index for an industry's output prices and PPI/WPI sub-index for its inputs).
- **Cost-push vs demand-pull** (both ultimately demand exceeding capacity to respond):
  - Cost-push: rising input costs, chiefly wages. Gauges: unemployment vs NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, or natural rate NARU), wage trends, unit labor cost, commodity prices. Skills mismatches (tech sector) can raise NAIRU, so shortages can appear at high headline unemployment. NAIRU is not observable and changes over time.
  - Demand-pull: actual vs potential GDP, capacity utilization, money growth vs nominal GDP growth (monetarist case is a special case of demand-pull), commodity prices (but global).
  - Expectations: once embedded they sustain inflation (1970s stagflation) and drive the wage-price spiral; gauge them from past inflation, surveys, and the breakeven between nominal and inflation-linked yields.
- **Indicator taxonomy**: leading (turn before the economy, forecast), coincident (identify the present), lagging (confirm the past). Composite indexes (Conference Board, OECD CLI, ECRI, CFNAI via principal components from 85 series, Euro-Coin) combine them; composition evolves and relationships break with structural change.

## Key Concepts
- **Trough / peak**: lowest and highest points of the cycle; turning points between contraction and expansion.
- **Output gap**: real GDP minus potential GDP; indicator of slack resources (unemployment, idle capital).
- **Inventory-sales ratio**: inventories divided by sales; rises near peaks, falls early in recovery; lagging indicator.
- **Final sales**: sales excluding inventory changes; shows underlying demand.
- **Permanent income**: income households believe they can rely on; excludes temporary gains/losses.
- **Say's law**: supply creates its own demand (neoclassical basis for market clearing).
- **Animal spirits**: Keynes's term for business confidence that can keep low rates from reviving investment.
- **Minsky moment**: sudden panic after a period of complacency and speculative leverage.
- **Real business cycle (RBC) model**: money-free New Classical model where real shocks cause efficient fluctuations.
- **Discouraged worker**: stopped job search; outside the labor force; a form of hidden unemployment.
- **NAIRU / NARU**: unemployment rate below which wage-push pressure appears; structural, unobservable.
- **Unit labor cost (ULC)**: labor compensation per hour divided by output per hour; rising ULC means cost-push pressure.
- **Hedonic pricing**: adjusting price measurement for quality change.
- **Diffusion index**: share of components moving in the same direction; measures breadth, insensitive to outliers.
- **Beige Book / ISM / PMI / Tankan**: qualitative or survey-based cyclical gauges (Fed districts; US supply managers; global PMIs; Japan's business survey).

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- Unemployment rate: `u = Unemployed / Labor force`
- Participation (activity) ratio: `Labor force / Working-age population`
- Price index: `I_t = [Σ P_t·Q / Σ P_0·Q] × 100`; inflation `π = I_t / I_{t-1} − 1`
- Laspeyres (base basket): `I_L = Σ P_t·Q_0 / Σ P_0·Q_0 × 100`
- Paasche (current basket): `I_P = Σ P_t·Q_t / Σ P_0·Q_t × 100`
- Fisher: `I_F = √(I_L × I_P)`
- Unit labor cost: `ULC = W / O` (W = compensation per hour, O = output per hour); `%ΔULC ≈ %ΔW − %ΔProductivity`
- Velocity: `V = Nominal GDP / Money supply`; falling V from a larger money denominator = inflation risk, falling V from a smaller GDP numerator = cyclical correction; rising V = disinflation/deflation concern
- Market-implied inflation (breakeven): `π_e ≈ y_nominal − y_inflation-linked` (e.g. 3.5% − 1.5% = 2%); distorted by the small size of linked markets and liability-matching demand
- Diffusion index: `DI = [Σ score_i / N] × 100`, score = 1.0 if component rises > 0.05%, 0.5 if within ±0.05%, 0 if falls > 0.05%
- Inflation by scope: headline > core when food/energy spike

| Indicator class | Conference Board US components |
|---|---|
| Leading (10) | Avg weekly hours (manufacturing); initial jobless claims; new orders consumer goods/materials; ISM new orders index; new orders non-defense capital goods ex-aircraft; building permits; S&P 500; Leading Credit Index; 10-yr Treasury minus fed funds spread; consumer expectations |
| Coincident (4) | Nonagricultural payrolls; real personal income less transfers; industrial production; manufacturing and trade sales |
| Lagging (7) | Average duration of unemployment; inventory-sales ratio; change in unit labor costs; bank prime rate; commercial and industrial loans outstanding; consumer installment debt/income; change in CPI for services |

  - US LEI leads contractions by 8-20 months and expansions by 1-10 months (variable); coincident index matches NBER dates closely. Eurozone leading index: 8 components (sentiment, permits, capital goods orders, Euro Stoxx, M2, spread, manufacturing PMI, services expectations). Japan: 10 components (machinery orders, operating profits, overtime, dwelling starts, labor productivity, business failures, Tankan, stocks, real M2, spread).
  - A wider yield spread anticipates short-rate rises and an upswing; a narrowing spread anticipates a downturn.

## Mental Models
- **Businesses adjust hours before headcount and inventories before capex.** Read the cycle first from overtime, temps, claims, orders and inventory-sales, not from the unemployment rate.
- **Leads forecast, coincidents locate, lags confirm.** A turn is credible only when signals from all three groups line up; one indicator (e.g. stocks up, composite flat) is not enough.
- **Markets lead the data.** Risk assets reprice 3-6 months before the trough; safe assets (government bonds, staples, utilities) are favored in contraction, cyclicals (discretionary) in recovery; boom pushes risky assets up and safe-bond prices down.
- **Policy view depends on the school.** Keynesian stimulates AD, monetarist steadies money growth, Austrian lets the bust clear, RBC does nothing, New Keynesian fixes frictions. Know whose prescription is driving a given central bank or government.
- **Think "slack vs bottleneck" for inflation.** Demand-pull = actual GDP near potential, high utilization, money growth > nominal GDP; cost-push = unemployment < NAIRU with ULC rising. Then overlay expectations.
- **Prefer core for trend, headline for cost of living; prefer GDP deflator for deflating GDP, PPI sub-indexes for input costs.**

## Worked Example
**Price indexes (Exhibit 3).** Basket: rice 50 kg at ¥3 → 70 kg at ¥4; gasoline 70 L at ¥4.4 → 60 L at ¥4.5 (Jan → Feb).
- Laspeyres: base basket costs 50×3 + 70×4.4 = 458; same basket in Feb 50×4 + 70×4.5 = 515; `I_L = 515/458 × 100 = 112.45` (inflation 12.45%).
- Paasche: Feb basket at Feb prices 70×4 + 60×4.5 = 550; at Jan prices 70×3 + 60×4.4 = 474; `I_P = 550/474 × 100 = 116.03`.
- Fisher: `√(112.45 × 116.03) = 114.23`.
- Interpretation: here Paasche exceeds Laspeyres (consumers shifted toward the good whose price rose most, rice, since quantities rose for it). That is the opposite of the usual substitution pattern, where Laspeyres is higher; the textbook bias claim is about typical substitution away from the relatively dearer good, not an arithmetic law.

**Diffusion index.** Four components. Month 1: stocks +2.0% (1.0), money +1.0% (1.0), orders flat (0.5), confidence −0.6% (0) → 2.5/4 × 100 = 62.5. Month 2: stocks −0.8% (0), money +0.5% (1.0), orders +0.5% (1.0), confidence +3.5% (1.0) → 3.0/4 × 100 = 75. A 20% rise in the index means broader participation, so more confidence than a headline composite jump driven by one component.

**Diagnosing inflation (Example 12).** Wage growth 3.4%, ULC −0.25% (so productivity growth ≈ 3.65%), nominal GDP +3.4%, money +6.7%, breakeven 2.2%. Wages are not pushing costs (ULC falling) and expectations are modest; money growing at twice nominal GDP points to demand-pull pressure.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading a falling unemployment rate as strength** without checking participation: discouraged workers leaving the labor force flatter the rate; a recovery can raise it.
- **Using the unemployment rate (lagging) or a single indicator to call a turn.** Triangulate leading, coincident and lagging groups.
- **Headline CPI alone to infer trend inflation.** Food and energy are globally driven and noisy; look at core, sub-indexes and expectations.
- **Treating "two negative quarters" as the definition of recession.** Dating committees use many series and publish late.
- **Assuming the same indicator set works everywhere or forever.** Compositions differ by country and evolve; relationships (e.g. German building permits after 2010) break after structural shocks.
- **Equating disinflation with deflation**, or high inflation alone with stagflation (needs high unemployment/weak growth too).
- **Letting political preference decide the school of thought you apply.** Analysts must set personal biases aside.
- **Reading commodity prices as a purely domestic signal** for a single economy; they reflect global demand.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- ⚠ **Correction vs. the owner's personal notes (cfa-level1-curriculum ch04)**: lists inventory-sales ratio rising near the peak (correct) but the "Okun's law" line is not part of this reading, and "money neutral" belongs to RBC/classical, not New Keynesian. Also in the notes the dropped hours-before-heads sequence is exact: cut hours/overtime, freeze hiring, then layoffs.
- Inventory-sales ratio is a **lagging** indicator; inventory changes are volatile, not large. Orders for light equipment fall first in a downturn (easier to cancel) and are reinstated first in recovery (efficiency gear).
- Durables are the most cyclical consumer category because they can be postponed (not because they are needed more).
- Imports respond to domestic growth; exports to foreign growth.
- Discouraged workers are not in the labor force; underemployed workers are employed.
- At a peak, unemployed workers are mostly frictional; at the start of a recovery the unemployment rate can still rise.
- Laspeyres = fixed base basket, upward biased (substitution, quality, new products); Fisher = geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche; PCE is the Fed's index since 2000; GDP deflator for deflating GDP.
- Stagflation cannot be identified from the inflation rate alone; high unemployment must accompany it.
- NAIRU/NARU are not specific to monetarist models; they are unobservable and drift.
- Indicator-question logic: leading indicators all deteriorating plus coincident softening plus lagging still strong = near a peak (Example 13.4); improving leading + coincident = recovery even if a lagging series (inventory-sales falling) agrees.
- Deflation hurts because revenues fall while nominal debt does not; aggregate demand causes inflation, not the other way round.
- Equity indexes bottom 3-6 months before the economy; the S&P 500 is a leading indicator.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cycles are recurrent, not periodic (1 to 10-12 years); capex, inventories and housing are the swing components, services the stable one.
2. Employers adjust hours and temps first, headcount last, so unemployment lags and productivity/claims/orders lead.
3. Inventory-sales ratio rises near the peak and falls in early recovery; use final sales to see true demand.
4. Each school has a diagnosis and a prescription: Keynesian (stimulate AD), Monetarist (steady money), Austrian (liquidate), RBC (do nothing), New Keynesian (fix frictions), Minsky (watch leverage).
5. Laspeyres overstates cost of living (substitution, quality, new goods); Fisher/Paasche and chaining reduce it; core inflation filters food and energy noise.
6. Distinguish cost-push (unemployment below NAIRU, ULC up) from demand-pull (output above potential, money > nominal GDP), then add expectations (breakeven = nominal minus linked yield).
7. Combine leading, coincident and lagging indicators; diffusion indexes show breadth.
8. Deflation raises real debt burdens and can spiral; policymakers aim for about 2% inflation.

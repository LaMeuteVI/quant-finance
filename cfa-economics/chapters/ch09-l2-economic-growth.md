# Chapter 9: Economic Growth
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2022, Level II, Reading 7 · Economics | Paul R. Kutasovic, PhD, CFA

## Core Idea
Sustainable growth equals the growth of potential GDP, which is the ceiling for long-run earnings growth and therefore for equity appreciation and the level of real interest rates. Potential growth decomposes into labor input growth plus productivity growth, and productivity comes from capital deepening (subject to diminishing returns) and technology (total factor productivity, TFP, the only source of permanent per-capita growth in the neoclassical model). The three growth paradigms (classical, neoclassical, endogenous) disagree on whether saving, R&D and openness can lift the long-run growth rate, and on whether poor countries converge; the evidence says convergence is conditional on institutions, openness and investment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Equity-return decomposition (P = GDP × E/GDP × P/E)**: `%ΔP ≈ %ΔGDP + %Δ(E/GDP) + %Δ(P/E)` (log growth rates; issuance and buybacks ignored). Over long horizons profit share and the P/E cannot trend forever, so price appreciation tracks nominal GDP growth. Evidence (S&P 500, 1946-2007): price appreciation 7.15% a year against nominal GDP 6.95% (real 3.01% + inflation 3.94%); EPS/GDP −0.12% and P/E +0.32% contributed almost nothing to return but most of the volatility (standard deviations 17.6% and 23.8%); dividend yield 3.67%; total return 10.82%. When to use: sanity-check capital-market assumptions, long-run equity return forecasts. Failure mode: endpoints at a bubble or crisis; index EPS growth also lags economy-wide earnings growth because new companies enter late.
- **Why potential growth matters (fixed income and policy)**: actual growth above (below) potential raises (lowers) inflation pressure (a cyclical, not long-run, trade-off); faster potential growth raises the equilibrium real interest rate (savers require more to postpone consumption) and expected real asset returns; it improves credit quality (rating agencies use potential growth for sovereigns); the output gap guides central banks; the structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit is the budget balance at potential GDP.
- **Production function and Cobb-Douglas**: `Y = A F(K, L)`; `F(K, L) = K^α L^(1−α)`. Competitive factor pay equals marginal product, so `α` = capital's share of income and `1 − α` = labor's share; they are also output elasticities. Constant returns to scale in K and L together, diminishing marginal returns to each alone. Per worker: `y = Y/L = A k^α`, `k = K/L`. Expanded inputs: natural resources N, labor quantity L, human capital H, ICT capital, non-ICT capital, public capital, TFP A.
- **Growth accounting (Solow)**: `ΔY/Y = ΔA/A + α ΔK/K + (1 − α) ΔL/L`. Use to (1) estimate TFP as the residual, (2) attribute growth to capital, labor, technology, (3) forecast potential GDP from trend inputs. With a US-like labor share of 0.7 (capital 0.3), a 1% labor increase raises output about 0.7%, a 1% capital increase 0.3%. Unspecified inputs (resources) are absorbed in TFP. Steps: pick α from the labor share of factor cost (`α = 1 −` labor cost/total factor cost); compute K, L growth over a consistent window; take TFP from a smoothed estimate; add weighted terms.
- **Labor-productivity method**: `g(potential GDP) = g(labor force or hours) + g(labor productivity)`. Simpler, no capital stock or TFP needed, but productivity mixes capital deepening and TFP. Growth from capital deepening = labor-productivity growth − TFP growth.
- **Capital deepening vs technological progress**: capital deepening raises `K/L` and moves the economy along the per-worker production function with diminishing returns (large effect where capital per worker is low, little where high); TFP growth shifts the whole function up proportionally and raises the marginal product of capital. Developed economies depend on TFP; developing economies can still gain from deepening, FDI and imitation. Note that once TFP grows, `K/L` must keep rising just to hold the marginal product of capital at its cost.
- **Labor input drivers**: population growth (fertility, mortality; raises GDP but not per capita GDP), age structure (a rising 65+ share and falling under-16 share is a burden; the reverse is a demographic dividend), participation (raises per capita GDP, but a transition to a new level, not a permanent growth rate), net migration, average hours (secular decline in rich countries; cyclical). Labor quality: human capital from education, training, health; education has spillovers and can raise innovation and therefore permanent growth.
- **Natural resources**: renewable vs non-renewable; ownership is not necessary (access via trade) nor sufficient. "Resource curse": weak institutions and Dutch disease (resource-driven currency appreciation hollows out manufacturing, which carries TFP progress). Depletion fears are overstated because of technology, substitution via prices, and falling resource shares of income.
- **Classical (Malthusian) model**: land fixed; population grows when income exceeds subsistence; diminishing returns bring per capita income back to subsistence; technology yields a larger, not richer population. Failed because population growth fell as income rose, and technology outran diminishing returns.
- **Neoclassical (Solow) model**: variable K and L with diminishing returns; investment `I = sY`, `ΔK = sY − δK`. Per-worker capital growth `Δk/k = sY/K − δ − n`. Steady state (constant output-to-capital ratio, output and capital per worker grow at the same rate): per capita growth `Δy/y = θ/(1 − α)` where `θ = ΔA/A`; output growth `θ/(1 − α) + n`. Equilibrium output-to-capital `Ψ = (1/s)[θ/(1 − α) + δ + n]`. In the steady state, marginal product of capital `α Y/K` is constant and equals the real rate.
  - Transition: `Δy/y = θ/(1 − α) + α s (y/k − Ψ)`; `Δk/k = θ/(1 − α) + s (y/k − Ψ)`. If actual `y/k > Ψ` (capital-poor), growth exceeds steady state and decays as `y/k` falls.
  - Comparative statics (levels, not steady-state growth): higher saving raises k and y (temporary growth boost); higher n or higher depreciation lowers k and y; higher θ lowers the level for a given moment but steepens the future path.
  - Implications: capital accumulation changes the level, not the long-run growth rate; only TFP growth (and labor growth) sets long-run growth; growth above steady state occurs early in capital accumulation; developing economies should grow faster (convergence). Critiques: TFP exogenous, saving rate unrelated to long-run growth (Mankiw finds positive cross-country correlation), no falling returns in advanced economies. Responses: augmented Solow (Jorgenson) with human capital, R&D, public infrastructure and composition of capital, and endogenous growth.
- **Endogenous growth (Romer)**: knowledge and human capital are produced inputs; R&D spillovers (social return above private return) remove diminishing returns to aggregate capital. `y = c k`; `Δy/y = Δk/k = s c − δ − n`, so a higher saving rate gives permanently higher growth; openness can permanently raise growth (selection, scale and backwardness effects); no automatic convergence. Government rationale: private R&D underinvests because ideas spill over (market failure), so subsidies, tax breaks and direct R&D can raise growth. Contrast: subsidizing all investment mostly buys capital deepening (temporary effect in Solow); subsidizing R&D/new technology with network externalities can lift growth permanently (more uncertain).
- **Convergence hypotheses**: absolute (all poor countries catch up regardless of characteristics; not implied by Solow); conditional (converge to the same level only if same saving rate, population growth and production function; otherwise the same growth rate but different levels); club (only members, countries with the right institutions, converge; outsiders fall behind; the lowest-income club members grow fastest); non-convergence trap (failed reform, or policies such as import substitution that helped early and then stalled). Two channels: capital deepening and technology imitation/transfer (needs absorptive investment). Evidence (27 economies, 1950-2018): mixed; 17 of 27 grew faster than the US; since 2000, 16 of 27 (60%) did, but not among advanced economies (France, Japan, Italy lagged).
- **Growth in an open economy**: access to global savings, comparative advantage, larger markets and scale, imported technology, competition. In Solow, openness accelerates capital-to-labor convergence (capital flows to high-marginal-product, capital-poor countries which run trade deficits; transitional growth, no permanent boost); in endogenous models openness permanently lifts growth (selection, scale, backwardness effects); small, technology-lagging countries can lose innovation incentive. Outward-oriented strategies (exports, FDI; Singapore, South Korea) beat inward-oriented import substitution (much of Latin America and Africa in 1950-80s). China and India entering the global economy raised global potential output, lowered the global labor share, and pushed down global goods prices.
- **Factors limiting developing-country growth**: low saving and investment (poverty cycle, broken by foreign investment: FDI or portfolio), weak financial intermediation (screening, risk transfer, pooling; but leverage-driven credit expansion does not raise growth), weak legal system and property rights, political instability, poor education and health, tax and regulatory burdens on entrepreneurship, trade and capital restrictions. Education mix: developed economies at the technology frontier need post-secondary and research spending; developing economies imitate and need primary and secondary education.

## Key Concepts
- **Potential GDP**: maximum sustainable output without raising inflation; its growth is the sustainable growth rate.
- **PPP-adjusted GDP**: cross-country comparison with a common set of prices, because market exchange rates are volatile and ignore cheaper non-tradables in poorer countries.
- **Total factor productivity (TFP)**: scale factor `A`; a residual that captures technology, organization, management and everything not in the measured inputs.
- **Capital deepening**: growth of capital per worker; movement along the production function.
- **Labor productivity**: output per worker or per hour.
- **Output elasticity and factor share**: `α` and `1 − α`, estimated from national income accounts.
- **Steady state / balanced growth**: capital and output per worker growing at the same constant rate, `θ/(1 − α)`.
- **ICT capital and general purpose technology**: capital whose network externalities show up in TFP (3-5% of GDP in developed economies).
- **Dutch disease / resource curse**: currency appreciation or weak institutions that crowd out the non-resource sector.
- **Demographic dividend and burden**: working-age expansion or aging that raises or lowers labor input growth.
- **Creative destruction**: Schumpeterian displacement that slows growth temporarily as innovation reallocates resources.
- **Absolute, conditional, club convergence; non-convergence trap**: the four convergence concepts.
- **Knowledge capital externality**: social return to R&D and human capital above the private return.
- **Selection, scale, backwardness effects**: the channels through which trade lifts growth in endogenous models.
- **Structural (cyclically adjusted) budget balance**: deficit at potential GDP.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `P = GDP × (E/GDP) × (P/E)`; `%ΔP ≈ %ΔGDP + %Δ(E/GDP) + %Δ(P/E)`
- Cobb-Douglas: `Y = A K^α L^(1−α)`; `y = A k^α`; `MPK = α Y/K`
- Growth accounting: `ΔY/Y = ΔA/A + α ΔK/K + (1 − α) ΔL/L`; TFP residual `ΔA/A = ΔY/Y − αΔK/K − (1 − α)ΔL/L`
- Labor-productivity method: `g_Y = g_L + g_(Y/L)`; capital deepening contribution = `g_(Y/L) − g_A`
- Capital stock: `K_t = K_(t−1) + I − D` (investment less consumption of fixed capital)
- Steady state: `Δy/y = Δk/k = θ/(1 − α)`; `Δy/y_total = θ/(1 − α) + n`; `Ψ = Y/K = (1/s)[θ/(1 − α) + δ + n]`; savings condition `s y = [θ/(1 − α) + δ + n] k`
- Transition: `Δy/y = θ/(1 − α) + α s (y/k − Ψ)`
- Level effect of a saving-rate change (A cancels): `k_new/k_old = [(Y/K)_new/(Y/K)_old]^(1/(α−1))`; `y_new/y_old = (k_new/k_old)^α`
- Endogenous: `y = c k`; `Δy/y = s c − δ − n`
- Compound growth: `g = (end/start)^(1/years) − 1`
- Real interest rate rises with potential growth; at the steady state `MPK = α Y/K = r`

| Model | Source of long-run per capita growth | Does saving change long-run growth? | Convergence? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical | None (back to subsistence) | No | No |
| Neoclassical (Solow) | Exogenous TFP | No (level only) | Yes (conditional) |
| Augmented Solow | TFP plus broader capital | No | Conditional |
| Endogenous | Knowledge, R&D, human capital | Yes | Not implied |

| Growth driver | Effect on potential growth | Durability |
|---|---|---|
| Capital deepening (low K/L) | Strong | Temporary (diminishing returns) |
| Capital deepening (high K/L) | Small | Temporary |
| TFP / R&D / human capital | Shifts frontier, raises MPK | Permanent in all models |
| Population growth | Raises GDP, not per capita GDP | While it lasts |
| Participation increase | Raises per capita output | Transition to a new level |
| Immigration | Raises labor input | Depends on policy |
| Openness to trade and FDI | Raises capital, technology, competition | Permanent only if endogenous mechanisms |

## Mental Models
- **Use `nominal GDP growth` as the anchor for long-run equity appreciation**; add dividend yield; treat extra return as an unsustainable rise in profit share or P/E.
- **Ask "deepening or technology?" before extrapolating a growth spurt**: a boom built on capital per worker with falling TFP (Spain 2006-15, Ireland post-2005, Japan, Brazil) is on a fading path.
- **Compare actual growth with potential**: the gap predicts inflation, central bank policy and sovereign credit; the long-run level of growth predicts real rates and earnings.
- **Demographics first for labor**: working-age share, participation room, immigration; do not extrapolate a one-off immigration surge or a trough-to-peak hours window.
- **Separate level from growth effects**: saving rate, population growth, depreciation and openness shift levels in Solow; only TFP changes the growth rate.
- **Read convergence as conditional**: back countries with open trade, FDI, rule of law, schooling and high investment (club members); do not buy "catch-up" by income level alone.

## Worked Example
All numbers recomputed.

**1. Compounding differences (1950-2018, 68 years).** Real GDP per capita: Argentina 6,164 to 18,255 gives `(18,255/6,164)^(1/68) − 1 = 1.61%`; Venezuela 8,104 to 9,487: 0.23%; Japan 3,048 to 39,313: 3.83%; Singapore 4,299 to 89,196: 4.56%; South Korea 1,185 to 36,756: 5.18%. If Argentina had grown like Japan, 2018 income would have been about `6,164 × 1.0383^68 = USD 79,500` (the text, rounding Japan to 3.8%, gets USD 77,854) versus 18,255. Lesson: sustained small growth gaps compound into different income classes.

**2. Equity return decomposition.** Macro service forecast: real growth 3.25%, inflation 3.75%, nominal GDP growth 7.00%. A consultant forecasting 11% price appreciation implicitly assumes a 4% a year combined rise in the profit share and P/E forever, which is impossible. Reasonable long-run appreciation is about 7% plus dividend yield (2.5% would give about 9.5% total versus 13.5%). S&P 1946-2007: `3.01 + 3.94 − 0.12 + 0.32 = 7.15%` appreciation, plus 3.67% dividend gives 10.82%.

**3. Growth accounting (Spain 2006-15, recomputed).** Capital stock: `K_2005 = 1,808`; adding `GDP × (investment% − consumption of fixed capital%)` each year gives 2,036 (2006) and about 2,897 (2015); `g_K = (2,897/2,036)^(1/9) − 1 = 4.00%`. Hours worked 35,358 to 31,527: `g_L = −1.27%`. Labor cost share averages 58.7%, so `α = 41.3%`. TFP growth: the source uses −0.68%; the geometric mean of the table's 2006-15 TFP values is −0.85% (⚠ see traps). Contributions: capital `0.413 × 4.00% = +1.65%`, labor `0.587 × (−1.27%) = −0.74%`, TFP −0.68%: potential growth `+0.23%` (about 0.06% with −0.85% TFP). Labor-productivity method: GDP per hour 48 to 54, `g = (54/48)^(1/9) − 1 = 1.32%`; `−1.27% + 1.32% = 0.05%`. Capital per hour rose from USD 57.6 to 91.9 (5.3% a year), pure deepening. Neoclassical steady state `−0.68%/0.587 − 1.27% = −2.4%` (with −0.85% TFP: −2.7%). Interpretation: growth rests on a capital-deepening phase with negative TFP and shrinking labor input, so equity upside is limited; a TFP rebound toward 1% would help, offset by weaker hours growth.

**4. Steady-state growth and transition (neoclassical vs endogenous).** Country data: TFP growth 2.0%, `α = 0.35`, `δ = 10%`, `n = 1%`. Steady-state per capita growth `0.02/0.65 = 3.08%`; `Ψ = (0.0308 + 0.10 + 0.01)/s`: with `s = 20%`, 0.704; with `s = 30%`, 0.469. Saving rise from 20% to 30%: growth jumps to `0.0308 + 0.35 × 0.30 × (0.7040 − 0.4693) = 5.54%`, then decays (4.39% at 5 years, 3.84% at 10, 3.25% at 25, 3.09% at 50, 3.08% at 100). Level effects: after 5 years `k` is 29.6% higher and per capita income 9.5% higher (`1.2959^0.35 = 1.095`); long run `k` +86.6%, `y` +24.4%, but growth returns to 3.08%. Endogenous version (`c = 0.704`, `s: 20% → 23.5%`): `Δy/y = 0.235 × 0.704 − 0.11 = 5.54%` permanently; after 10 years income is `e^(0.0246 × 10) = 1.279`, i.e. 28% higher, against 15.2% in the neoclassical case with a bigger saving increase.

**5. Neoclassical steady states versus estimated potential.** China: labor share 0.561, TFP 2.9%, labor force 1.2%: `2.9/0.561 + 1.2 = 6.37%` versus 8.5% potential, so China is still converging above steady state. Japan: `0.2/0.538 + 0 = 0.37%` (potential about 1%). Ireland: `0.9/0.574 + 0.3 = 1.87%` (potential about 2.0%): both at steady state, so only TFP and labor matter. Decomposition check, Ireland 1995-2005: capital contributed 2.6% + 0.7% = 3.3% of 7.3% GDP growth (45%), TFP 1.7%.

## Anti-patterns
- Extrapolating a country's recent growth without splitting it into labor, capital deepening and TFP.
- Forecasting equity returns from historic index returns taken at a peak profit share or P/E.
- Treating a higher saving rate or investment boom as a permanent growth rate increase (Solow) when it is capital deepening.
- Reading population growth as per capita growth.
- Assuming natural resource wealth guarantees growth, or that resource depletion caps it.
- Treating convergence as automatic; ignoring institutions, openness, schooling, financial depth.
- Using market exchange rates to compare living standards.
- Equating "financial deepening" with growth when it is leverage-driven credit expansion.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- Developed economy with high K/L: technology (TFP) is the main lever; free trade or more saving gives only incremental or transitory gains. Developing country: capital deepening, FDI and trade matter most.
- Profit share and P/E must be roughly constant in the long run: a trending-up profit share is expected to revert down.
- Higher potential growth means higher real interest rates and higher expected real returns; slower potential growth raises sovereign credit risk.
- Capital deepening contribution = labor productivity growth − TFP growth; compare countries via Exhibit-style tables (the larger the gap the more capital-driven).
- Steady-state formula uses labor share `(1 − α)` in the denominator; do not use α. Output growth adds `n`; per capita growth does not.
- Higher n, δ or θ lowers the steady-state level of `k` and `y`; higher s raises them; none of s, n or δ changes steady-state per capita growth.
- Conditional vs absolute vs club convergence: same saving, population growth and production function means conditional; "irrespective of characteristics" means absolute; institutional-change-dependent means club.
- Neoclassical model: openness speeds convergence but leaves long-run growth unchanged; endogenous: openness raises growth permanently.
- Dutch disease: currency appreciation from resource exports hollows out manufacturing; strong manufacturing exports are not a symptom.
- Participation, immigration and a shorter workweek affect level or transition; sustained growth needs productivity.
- Government rationale for R&D incentives: positive externalities mean private underinvestment.

## Key Takeaways
1. Long-run equity appreciation tracks nominal GDP growth; profit share and P/E mean-revert and drive volatility, not return.
2. Potential GDP growth equals labor force growth plus labor productivity growth; growth accounting weights K and L growth by factor shares and treats TFP as the residual.
3. Capital deepening faces diminishing returns; only TFP shifts the production function and sustains per capita growth.
4. In the Solow steady state `Δy/y = θ/(1 − α)`; saving, depreciation and population change levels, not steady-state growth; transition growth above steady state fades.
5. Endogenous growth removes diminishing returns through R&D and human-capital spillovers, so saving and openness can raise growth permanently and justify R&D subsidies.
6. Convergence is conditional or club-based: investment, openness, institutions, education and financial depth decide membership.
7. Demographics, participation and immigration shift labor input; treat one-off surges and recession-distorted hours windows with caution.
8. Developing-country growth needs saving or FDI, property rights, financial depth, human capital, light regulation and open trade; resource wealth is neither necessary nor sufficient.

# Chapter 3: Aggregate Output, Prices, and Economic Growth
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2020, Level I, Reading 14 · Economics | Paul R. Kutasovic, Richard Fritz

## Core Idea
GDP can be measured three equivalent ways (final expenditure, value added, income), and the identity `S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)` ties private saving, investment, the fiscal balance and the external balance together. The IS-LM model collapses goods-market and money-market equilibrium into a downward-sloping aggregate demand (AD) curve; combined with aggregate supply (flat, then upward sloping, then vertical at potential GDP) it classifies the economy as at full employment, in a recessionary gap, an inflationary gap or in stagflation, each with a distinct asset-class tilt. Over the long run only supply-side forces (labor, human and physical capital, natural resources, above all technology/TFP) raise potential GDP; the practical estimate is `growth of labor force + growth of labor productivity`.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three ways to measure GDP**:
  - Expenditure: `GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)` (+ statistical discrepancy). `I` is gross private investment including inventory change (otherwise unsold output would be omitted); `G` is purchases of goods and services only, excluding transfer payments (spending financed by transfers shows up in `C`).
  - Income: `GDP = GDI = compensation of employees + gross operating surplus + gross mixed income + taxes less subsidies on production and on products/imports (+ statistical discrepancy)`; equivalently net domestic income + consumption of fixed capital (CFC).
  - Value added: sum over stages of (sales value minus purchased inputs) equals the final sale price. Avoids double counting of intermediate goods.
  - What counts: current production only, market value (with imputed values only for owner-occupied rent and government services at cost), final goods. Excluded: used goods, transfers, capital gains, non-market and underground activity, unpriced by-products (pollution). Analysts usually trust expenditure data more (timelier); the US books the statistical discrepancy only on the income side.
  - GDP vs GNP: GDP is by location of production; GNP by ownership of the factors.
- **Nominal vs real GDP and the deflator**: `Nominal GDP = Pt × Qt`; `Real GDP = P_base × Qt`; `GDP deflator = (nominal/real) × 100 = (current output at current prices / current output at base prices) × 100`. Real growth is the informative growth rate; `(1 + nominal) = (1 + real)(1 + inflation)`.
- **Income ladder (national accounts)**: GDP minus CFC (and ± statistical discrepancy, ± net foreign factor income in the US) = national income; national income plus transfers minus undistributed corporate profit, corporate taxes and indirect taxes = personal income (Canada: primary household income = compensation + net mixed income + net property income); personal income minus personal taxes (net current transfers paid) = household disposable income; minus household consumption plus change in pension entitlements = household net saving. Disposable income is the figure that drives spending and saving.
- **Sectoral balances identity**: `Y = C + S + T = C + I + G + (X − M)` gives `S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)`, or `G − T = (S − I) − (X − M)`. A fiscal deficit is financed by domestic private net saving `(S − I)` or a trade deficit (foreign saving), or both. A trade deficit is a capital inflow; it must have an exactly matching surplus abroad (twin-deficits logic).
- **Behavioral functions**:
  - `C = C(Y − T)`, `MPC + MPS = 1`; consumption also rises with wealth. APC (C/Y) proxies MPC: economies with high APC (US 68%, Mexico 68% vs Germany 55%) react more to tax and income shocks.
  - `I = I(r, Y)`: falls with the real rate (cost of funds), rises with output (profitability proxy). Weak profit expectations can keep investment dormant despite low rates.
  - Fiscal balance `G − t(Y)`: tax receipts rise and transfers fall with income, so the balance improves in booms: the **automatic stabilizer**.
  - Net exports fall with domestic income, rise with foreign income and with a lower relative price of domestic goods (depreciation).
- **IS curve**: combinations of `(Y, r)` with planned expenditure equal to income, i.e. `S(Y) − I(r) = (G − T) + (X − M)`. Slopes down: a lower real rate lifts investment, requiring higher income to generate offsetting saving. Fiscal expansion shifts it right. The multiplier is `1/(MPS-type leakage)`: in the reading's example `ΔY = ΔG/(0.21 − 0.20 + 0.3 + 0.1) = 2.44 ΔG`.
- **LM curve**: combinations of `(Y, r)` with real money supply equal to real money demand, `M/P = L(r, Y)`; money demand rises with income, falls with the rate. Slopes up. An increase in M/P (higher nominal M or lower P) shifts it right. Quantity theory: `MV = PY`, equivalently `M/P = kY`, `k = 1/V`.
- **AD curve**: hold nominal M fixed; a higher price level lowers M/P, shifts LM left, raises r, lowers Y. Other channels: real wealth and fixed-income purchasing power fall, and domestic goods become dearer vs imports. AD is flatter if investment is very rate-sensitive, saving is insensitive to income, money demand is insensitive to rates or to income. Flatter AD means monetary policy has more punch.
- **What shifts AD**: household wealth (wealth effect, roughly 3 to 7% of a wealth change spent in developed markets), confidence of consumers and firms, capacity utilization (roughly 82-85% triggers capex), fiscal policy (G up or taxes down shifts right), monetary policy (reserves, reserve requirement, policy rate), exchange rate (a weaker domestic currency shifts AD right), foreign growth. A fiscal or confidence shift raises the interest rate at each price level; money expansion lowers it. A liquidity trap (banks hoard excess reserves, or money demand insensitive to income) makes monetary shifts ineffective.
- **Aggregate supply**: VSRAS horizontal (firms flex utilization and hours at fixed prices, months to quarters); SRAS upward sloping (nominal wages and input costs sticky for a year or more, so higher prices widen margins); LRAS vertical at potential GDP `Ȳ = F(K, L)` once all costs adjust (a few years to a decade; capital still fixed). If wages were indexed to prices, SRAS would be steeper.
- **What shifts SRAS (and not LRAS)**: nominal wages (use unit labor cost: `%Δ ULC = %Δ wages − %Δ productivity`), input and energy prices, business taxes (left) and subsidies (right), expected future prices (modest, temporary), exchange rate (stronger domestic currency lowers imported input costs: right). **What shifts LRAS and SRAS together**: labor supply (population, participation, immigration), human capital, physical capital, natural resources, productivity/technology. A permanent change in relative input prices (a depleted resource) can move LRAS left.
- **Four macro states and tilts**:

| State | Mechanism | Real GDP / unemployment / prices | Profits, rates, commodities | Tilt |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long-run full employment | AD = SRAS at LRAS | at potential, natural rate | steady | neutral |
| Recessionary gap | AD left (tight policy, higher taxes, weak confidence or wealth) | below potential, unemployment up, prices down | profits, rates, commodity prices, credit demand all fall | cut cyclicals, commodities, speculative equity and junk; add defensives, investment-grade/government bonds, longer maturities |
| Inflationary gap | AD right beyond potential | above potential, unemployment down, prices up | profits, rates, commodities up | add cyclicals and commodities; cut bonds, especially long ones; junk spreads tighten |
| Stagflation | SRAS left (oil, wages) | output down, unemployment up, prices up | margins squeezed, rates up, commodities up | cut bonds and most equities; hold commodities |

  - Self-correction: wages and input prices fall in a recession (SRAS right) or rise in an overheated economy (SRAS left), but slowly; policy can instead shift AD, subject to variable lags that may destabilize. Supply-side stimulus at stagflation buys output at the price of a permanently higher price level.
  - Combined shifts: AD and AS both up gives higher real GDP, price level ambiguous; both down gives lower GDP, price ambiguous; AD up/AS down gives price up, GDP ambiguous; AD down/AS up gives price down, GDP ambiguous. Demand-driven expansions come with rising inflation and rates; supply-driven expansions with falling inflation and rates.
- **Solow growth framework**: `Y = A·F(L, K)`, constant returns to scale, diminishing marginal productivity of each input. Consequences: capital deepening alone cannot sustain growth; capital-poor economies grow faster (convergence); sustained per-capita growth needs a rising `A` (TFP, mainly technology).
  - **Growth accounting**: `g_Y = g_A + W_L·g_L + W_C·g_K`, shares `W_L + W_C = 1` (US about 0.7 and 0.3). TFP growth is the residual: `g_A = g_Y − (W_L·g_L + W_C·g_K)`. Per capita: `g_(Y/L) = g_A + W_C·g_(K/L)`.
  - Five sources of growth: labor supply (total hours = labor force × average hours), human capital, physical capital (net investment positive), technology, natural resources (renewable or not; not necessary if tradable).
  - **Labor productivity route** (observable): `Y/L = A·F(1, K/L)`; `potential GDP = aggregate hours × productivity`; so `potential growth = growth of labor force (or hours) + growth of labor productivity`. TFP and labor productivity differ: productivity also rises with capital per worker.
  - Use: compare actual growth with potential; actual above potential signals inflationary pressure and likely rate rises (bad for bonds), actual below potential signals slack and falling or stable rates.

## Key Concepts
- **Final good vs intermediate good**: only goods not resold or further processed enter GDP; inventories of unsold finals do enter (as investment).
- **Gross vs net investment**: gross includes replacement; net investment = gross investment minus CFC.
- **Statistical discrepancy**: gap between expenditure and income GDP due to different data sources.
- **Transfer payments**: redistribution without production; excluded from `G`.
- **Imputed value**: estimated value for non-traded items (owner-occupied rent, government services at wage cost).
- **Underground economy**: unrecorded activity; estimated 8% of GDP in the US, 60% in Peru (2006 study), so cross-country GDP reliability differs.
- **Real interest rate**: nominal rate minus expected inflation; the rate that drives `I`.
- **Wealth effect**: higher asset prices lower saving and raise consumption (UK saving rate fell as housing rose, jumped to 7% in 2009 as prices fell).
- **Recessionary gap**: equilibrium output below potential; **inflationary gap**: above potential.
- **Stagflation**: falling output with rising prices from a SRAS decline (1973 oil quadrupling, 1979-80 doubling).
- **Potential GDP**: unobservable output at the natural rate with spare capacity and balanced job seekers and vacancies; its trend growth is estimable even if its level is not.
- **Total factor productivity**: the scale factor `A` in the production function; a residual.
- **Capital deepening**: raising K/L; subject to diminishing returns.
- **Convergence**: poorer economies growing faster because of high marginal product of capital; imperfect in practice.
- **Natural rate of unemployment**: unemployment at potential output.

## Formulas & Reference Tables
- `GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)`; `GDI = comp. of employees + gross op. surplus + gross mixed income + (taxes − subsidies on production) + (taxes − subsidies on products and imports)`
- `National income = GDP − CFC (± statistical discrepancy)`
- `S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)`; `G − T = (S − I) − (X − M)`; `X − M = (S − I) − (G − T)`
- `Real GDP = Nominal GDP / (deflator/100)`; `deflator = nominal/real × 100`; inflation = % change in deflator
- `MPS = 1 − MPC`; `ΔS = (1 − MPC)(1 − t)ΔY` with proportional tax rate t
- Multiplier with leakages: `ΔY/ΔG = 1/[MPS(1 − t) − ΔI/ΔY + t + m]` (as in the example)
- `MV = PY`; `M/P = kY`
- `% Δ unit labor cost = % Δ wages − % Δ productivity`
- `Total hours = labor force × average hours per worker`
- `g_Y = g_A + W_L·g_L + W_C·g_K`; `g_A = g_Y − W_L·g_L − W_C·g_K`
- `Potential growth ≈ g_(labor force) + g_(labor productivity)`; labor productivity = real GDP / aggregate hours

| AD shifters (increase) | Direction |
|---|---|
| Stock and house prices, consumer and business confidence, capacity utilization, G, bank reserves/money supply, global growth | AD right |
| Taxes, domestic currency strength | AD left |

| SRAS shifters (increase) | SRAS | LRAS |
|---|---|---|
| Labor, natural resources, human capital, physical capital, technology | right | right |
| Nominal wages, input prices, business taxes | left | none |
| Subsidies, expected future prices, domestic currency strength | right | none |

## Mental Models
- **Sectoral-balance accounting**: a sector cannot run a deficit unless another runs a surplus; read a fiscal deficit as the sum of private net saving and foreign saving.
- **Short run demand, long run supply**: money or fiscal expansion lifts output briefly, then only the price level (LRAS is vertical).
- **Which curve moved?** Output and prices both down or both up: AD. Output down, prices up: SRAS. Use it to pick the asset-class tilt before the market does.
- **Potential growth as speed limit**: actual growth above `labor growth + productivity growth` means the output gap is closing and rates will rise; below means slack.
- **Technology beats capital**: at a 0.3 capital share, +1% capital per worker adds only 0.3% output per head.

## Anti-patterns
- Adding intermediate goods or transfer payments into GDP; omitting inventory change.
- Using nominal GDP growth to judge activity; comparing countries on nominal figures.
- Treating the AD slope as a micro demand curve; it is an IS-LM outcome.
- Assuming higher business taxes shift AD (they shift SRAS) or that wage rises move LRAS (they do not).
- Reading a trade deficit as a pure weakness: it equals foreign willingness to fund domestic spending; the question is financing terms.
- Forecasting growth from capital accumulation alone; diminishing returns bind.
- Treating potential GDP as observed.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- GDP deflator question: `real = nominal/(deflator/100)`. Example: nominal 100 to 300 with deflator 111 to 200 gives real 90.09 to 150, +66.5% (about 67%).
- Deflator below 100 (scaled) means prices lower than in the base year; deflator falling means deflation.
- Annualize over multi-year spans: deflators 190 to 212.8 over two years is `(212.8/190)^(1/2) − 1 = 5.8%` per year, not 12%.
- GDP and national income: `GDP − CCA = national income` (22.6 and 21.1 in the standard exam problem).
- A rise in household saving with unchanged output and investment must show up as higher net exports (capital outflow) or a bigger fiscal deficit.
- Direction table: IS shifts right with G; LM shifts right with nominal M; price level changes move LM and AD movements along the curve; "a weak currency" increases AD.
- TFP is a residual (not directly measurable); labor productivity is measurable; the intercept in a fitted growth equation is TFP growth; the coefficients are income shares.
- Only convergence is predicted by diminishing returns to capital; technology explains superior growth among developed economies.
- Stagflation is a SRAS shift, not an AD shift.
- **⚠ Correction (source Example 2)**: the source calls the 2016 Canadian result "a very low rate of deflation"; the deflator rose from 113.18 to 113.93 so 2016 shows inflation of about 0.6%. (2015 versus 2014, 113.2 vs 113.9, is the deflation year.)
- **Note (source Example 3, part 4)**: the solution prints the transfers paid as 514,556 once; the data table gives 514,566 (the figure that reproduces C$40,108 and 3.48%).
- **Note (source Example 6)**: AD coefficients are rounded; exact values are `5,868.1 + 5,714.3/P`.

## Key Takeaways
1. GDP by expenditure, income and value added must reconcile; only final, current, market-valued output counts (plus imputed rent and government at cost).
2. `S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)`: fiscal deficits are funded by private surplus saving or foreign capital.
3. IS-LM gives a downward-sloping AD (price level moves real money, hence rates and output); AD is flatter when investment is rate-sensitive.
4. SRAS slopes up because input costs are sticky; LRAS is vertical at potential, so demand policy changes only prices in the long run.
5. AD shocks move output and prices together (gap states); SRAS shocks move them opposite (stagflation); each calls for a different asset-class tilt.
6. Growth accounting separates input growth from TFP; technology is the sustainable source and capital deepening faces diminishing returns.
7. Practical sustainable growth is labor-force growth plus labor-productivity growth; compare it with actual growth to read inflation and rate risk.
8. Disposable income and APC/MPC tell you how policy and shocks transmit to consumption.

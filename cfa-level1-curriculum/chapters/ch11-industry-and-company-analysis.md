# Chapter 11: Introduction to Industry and Company Analysis (Equity)

## Core Idea
Industry analysis places a company in its economic context: its peers, its life-cycle stage, the competitive forces it faces and external influences. That context explains sustainable profitability (ROIC vs WACC). Company analysis then asks how the firm competes (cost leadership vs differentiation) and what drives its demand, costs, pricing and financial ratios.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Uses of industry analysis**: understand the business and its environment, find active opportunities, and attribute portfolio performance.
- **Grouping companies**:
  1. **Products/services supplied**: by the *principal business activity* (the largest revenue source); segment reporting helps. An industry is part of a sector.
  2. **Business-cycle sensitivity**: cyclical (autos, housing, capital goods) vs non-cyclical/defensive. Limitation: sensitivity is a continuum.
  3. **Statistical similarity** (return correlations). Limitations: history may not repeat, and correlation is not causation.
- **Classification systems**:
  - **Commercial**: **GICS** (S&P/MSCI; 11 sectors → industry groups → industries → sub-industries), **ICB** (FTSE/Dow Jones), **Russell Global Sectors**. Only public, for-profit companies, and constituents are disclosed.
  - **Government**: ISIC (UN), NACE (EU), ANZSIC, NAICS (US/Canada/Mexico). They include private and non-profit firms, don't disclose constituents, and don't distinguish by size.
- **Building a peer group**:
  - Start from the commercial classification.
  - Read the company's and competitors' annual reports and trade publications.
  - Confirm that each peer earns a large share of revenue from similar activities and faces similar demand, growth and margins.
  - **Strategic groups**: sub-segments with distinct models (charter vs scheduled airlines, generic vs branded pharma).
- **Industry life cycle**:

| Stage | Growth | Prices | Competition | Profitability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Embryonic | Slow | High | Low | Often losses; high risk |
| Growth | Rapid | Falling (scale) | Low; entry threat highest | Improving |
| Shakeout | Slowing | Price cuts | Intense; excess capacity | Declining |
| Mature | Little or none | Stable | Consolidated, high barriers | Stable |
| Decline | Negative | Price wars | Rising again | Lowest returns |

  - Limitations: technology can jump stages (vacuum tubes were replaced by transistors), and regulatory, social and demographic shifts can also disrupt the sequence.
- **Experience curve**: unit cost falls with cumulative output (spreading fixed costs, learning, process and design improvements).
- **Porter's five forces**: threat of entry, supplier power, buyer power, threat of substitutes, rivalry.
- **Strategic analysis checklist**:
  - **Barriers to entry**: high barriers protect economic profits. Check the history of entrants.
  - **Concentration**: concentrated industries have less price competition; fragmented ones have more.
  - **Capacity**: tight capacity gives pricing power; overcapacity forces price cuts.
  - **Market-share stability**.
  - **Life-cycle stage**.
  - **Importance of price** to the buyer.
  - High barriers don't guarantee pricing power: price-sensitive buyers (aircraft makers) and **exit barriers / capital intensity** (autos, refining) create overcapacity.
- **External influences**: macro (GDP, rates, credit, inflation), technology, demographics, government/regulation, social trends.
- **Porter's generic strategies**:
  - **Cost leadership**: defensive, aggressive or predatory pricing; tight cost control.
  - **Differentiation**: unique quality, type or distribution.
  - **Focus**: either of the two applied to a niche.
- **Elements of company analysis**: corporate profile, industry characteristics, demand, supply and costs, pricing environment, financial ratios over time and against peers.

## Key Concepts
- **Economic profit rate**: ROIC − WACC. Industries differ persistently.
- **Principal business activity**: the source of most revenue.
- **Barriers to entry vs barriers to success**: getting in vs surviving.

## Mental Models
- **Economic profits persist only behind barriers.** Look for what stops entrants.
- **Fragmentation plus commodity products plus high fixed costs means price wars.**
- **Match the valuation approach to the life-cycle stage**: growth → growth-sensitive models; mature → dividends or FCF.

## Anti-patterns
- **Grouping companies by past return correlations alone**: the groups can be spurious.
- **Equating high barriers with high pricing power**: check buyer price sensitivity and exit barriers.
- **Applying the life-cycle model during technological disruption**.

## Worked Example
**Five-forces scan for an airline.**
- Entry: moderate (slots, capital), but low-cost carriers do enter.
- Suppliers: strong (a Boeing/Airbus duopoly, unions, fuel).
- Buyers: price-sensitive, with online comparison.
- Substitutes: rail and video calls.
- Rivalry: intense, with high fixed costs and exit barriers.
- Conclusion: structurally **low ROIC − WACC**. Favor companies with cost leadership or network and slot advantages.

## Key Takeaways
1. Group companies by product and service first, then check cyclicality and statistics.
2. GICS and ICB (commercial) vs NAICS, ISIC and NACE (government): know the differences.
3. The life-cycle stage predicts growth, pricing and competition.
4. Five forces plus barriers, concentration, capacity and price sensitivity explain sustainable profitability.
5. Company strategy is either cost leadership or differentiation. Analyze demand, supply, pricing and ratios.

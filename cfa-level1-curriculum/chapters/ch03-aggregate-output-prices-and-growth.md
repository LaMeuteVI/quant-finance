# Chapter 3: Aggregate Output, Prices and Economic Growth (Economics)

## Core Idea
Output and prices are set by aggregate demand (AD) and aggregate supply (AS). In the short run, shifts in AD or SRAS create recessionary gaps, inflationary gaps or stagflation. In the long run output returns to potential GDP, whose growth comes from labor, capital and, above all, technology.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Expenditure identity**: `GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)`. It implies **`(S − I) = (G − T) + (X − M)`**:
  - A fiscal deficit (G − T > 0) must be financed by private saving above investment and/or a trade deficit (foreign savings).
  - A trade deficit means foreigners are accumulating claims on the domestic economy.
  - **Twin-deficits hypothesis**: large fiscal deficits come with large trade deficits.
- **Behavioral functions**:
  - Consumption: C = C(Y − T), with MPC + MPS = 1.
  - Investment: I = I(r, Y).
  - Government: fiscal balance = G − t(Y). **Automatic stabilizers**: tax revenue rises with income, which dampens the cycle.
  - **Crowding out**: deficits raise interest rates and lower private investment.
- **IS–LM**:
  - **IS**: combinations of (r, Y) where planned spending = income. It slopes downward.
  - **LM**: combinations of (i, Y) where real money demand L(i, Y) = real money supply M/P.
  - Money is demanded for transactions, precaution and speculation.
  - Their intersection gives (r*, Y*). A higher price level reduces M/P, which raises r and lowers Y. That traces out the **downward-sloping AD curve**.
- **Quantity theory of money**: `MV = PY`.
- **Aggregate supply**:
  - **VSRAS** is flat: firms flex hours and capacity.
  - **SRAS** slopes up: input costs such as wages are sticky under contracts.
  - **LRAS** is vertical at potential GDP: all costs adjust.
- **What shifts AD**: household wealth (wealth effect), expectations, capacity utilization, fiscal policy, monetary policy, exchange rates, global growth.
- **What shifts SRAS**: nominal wages, input prices, expected prices, business taxes and subsidies, exchange rates.
- **What shifts LRAS**: labor supply, human capital, physical capital, technology, natural resources.
- **Four macro states** and their investment implications:

| State | Cause | Profits / rates / commodities | Tilt the portfolio toward |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recessionary gap | AD shifts left | ↓ / ↓ / ↓ | Defensives, investment-grade & government bonds |
| Inflationary gap | AD shifts right beyond potential | ↑ / ↑ / ↑ | Cyclicals, commodities, high yield; reduce bonds |
| Stagflation | SRAS shifts left (1970s oil) | margins ↓ / ↑ / ↑ | Commodities; reduce bonds and equities |
| Long-run equilibrium | AD = SRAS = LRAS | — | Neutral |

- **Solow (neoclassical) growth model**: `Y = A·F(L, K)`.
  - Constant returns to scale, with diminishing marginal productivity of each input.
  - So capital deepening alone cannot sustain growth. **TFP (technology) drives long-run per-capita growth**.
  - Capital-poor countries should grow faster, which predicts **convergence**.
- **Growth accounting**: `ΔY/Y = ΔA/A + α·ΔK/K + (1 − α)·ΔL/L`.

## Key Concepts
- **Potential GDP**: output at full (natural) employment. The long-run anchor.
- **Recession**: commonly defined as ≥ 2 consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth.
- **Wealth effect**: higher equity or housing values raise consumption.
- **Sustainable growth**: growth of potential GDP. Growth above it produces inflation and shortages.
- **APC**: consumption/income. A high APC means demand is sensitive to disposable income.

## Mental Models
- Treat **(S − I) = (G − T) + (X − M)** as an accounting constraint: one balance cannot move without another moving too.
- In the **short run demand matters; in the long run only supply matters**. Monetary expansion raises output temporarily and prices permanently.
- **Match the portfolio to the gap**: recession → duration and quality; overheating → cyclicals and real assets.

## Anti-patterns
- ⚠ **Correction vs the notes**: the production function has **constant returns to scale, i.e. it is homogeneous of degree 1** (not "degree 0").
- ⚠ **Correction**: long-run equilibrium is where **AD, SRAS and LRAS** all intersect at potential GDP, not only AD and SRAS.
- ⚠ **Correction**: higher business taxes shift **SRAS** left (they are a production cost), not AD.
- **Expecting fiscal stimulus to work fully at full employment**: crowding out and inflation absorb it.

## Worked Example
**Sectoral balances.** A country has private saving S = 20% of GDP, investment I = 18% and a government deficit G − T = 5%.
- (S − I) = 2% = (G − T) + (X − M) = 5% + (X − M), so **X − M = −3%**.
- The economy runs a current-account deficit of 3% of GDP, financed by foreign capital. This is consistent with the twin-deficits logic.

## Key Takeaways
1. GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). The sectoral balances must sum.
2. IS–LM gives a downward-sloping AD. The AS curve is flat, then upward-sloping, then vertical as the horizon lengthens.
3. Recessionary gap, inflationary gap and stagflation each imply a different asset allocation.
4. Only supply-side factors (L, K, human capital, technology) raise potential GDP.
5. Solow: diminishing returns to capital, so TFP is the long-run engine and convergence is predicted.

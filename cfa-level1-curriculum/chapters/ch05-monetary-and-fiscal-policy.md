# Chapter 5: Monetary and Fiscal Policy (Economics)

## Core Idea
Central banks pursue price stability by moving a policy rate relative to the **neutral rate** and by running open-market operations, and that rate transmits to the whole economy. Fiscal policy moves aggregate demand through spending, taxes and the multiplier. Both instruments are limited by lags, credibility, liquidity traps and debt sustainability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Functions of money**: medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account.
  - To serve as a medium of exchange, money must be accepted, of known value, divisible, high value per unit of weight and hard to counterfeit.
- **Money creation (fractional reserves)**: money multiplier = **1 / reserve requirement**.
  - Monetary aggregates: M0 (monetary base), M1 (narrow), M2 (broad).
- **Quantity theory**: `MV = PY`.
  - **Money neutrality** (classical view): in the long run, money affects only nominal variables.
- **Fisher effect**: `R_nominal = R_real + E(inflation)` (+ a risk premium for inflation uncertainty).
- **Demand for money**: transactions, precautionary and speculative motives. The speculative demand falls as interest rates rise.
- **Costs of inflation**:
  - *Expected* inflation: menu costs and shoe-leather costs.
  - *Unexpected* inflation: shifts wealth from lenders to borrowers, raises inflation risk premia, and blurs price signals.
- **Roles of central banks**: monopoly supplier of currency, banker to banks and to government, lender of last resort, overseer of payments, manager of FX and gold reserves, conductor of monetary policy.
- **Tools**:
  1. **Policy rate**: implemented through **repo** operations (lending against collateral). A higher policy rate raises banks' base rates.
  2. **Reserve requirements**: a blunt tool.
  3. **Open-market operations**: buying bonds adds reserves; selling bonds drains them.
- **Transmission channels**: a policy rate change moves banks' lending rates, asset prices, expectations and confidence, and the exchange rate. Those in turn move domestic demand and net exports, then inflation.
- **Inflation targeting** (New Zealand, 1988). It requires **independence, credibility and transparency** (e.g., published inflation reports).
- **Exchange rate targeting**: domestic rates must adjust to defend the peg.
- **Neutral rate**: `neutral = real trend growth + inflation target`.
  - Policy rate > neutral means the stance is contractionary. Policy rate < neutral means it is expansionary.
- **Limitations**:
  - **Liquidity trap**: people hoard any extra money, so monetary easing has no effect. Often associated with deflation.
  - Response: **quantitative easing (QE)**, i.e. large-scale asset purchases.
  - Developing economies add obstacles: shallow bond markets, weak independence, poor track records.
  - Supply shocks: raising rates against supply-driven inflation deepens the downturn.
- **Fiscal policy**:
  - Aims: aggregate demand, income distribution, resource allocation.
  - Tools: transfer payments, current government spending, capital expenditure, direct taxes and indirect taxes.
  - Desirable tax attributes: **simplicity, efficiency, fairness** (horizontal and vertical), **revenue sufficiency**.
  - **Automatic stabilizers**: deficits widen automatically in downturns.
- **Fiscal multiplier**: `1 / [1 − c(1 − t)]`, where c is the MPC and t the tax rate. Without taxes it is 1/(1 − MPC).
- **Debt sustainability**: stable if real growth > the real interest rate on debt.
  - Concerns: crowding out, higher future taxes, loss of confidence, monetization.
  - Offsetting arguments: debt owed domestically, debt funding capital investment, **Ricardian equivalence** (households save in anticipation of future taxes).
- **Lags**: indirect taxes can change quickly. Direct taxes and capex have long implementation lags, which is a key critique of fiscal fine-tuning.

## Key Concepts
- **Policy rate / repo / reverse repo**, **base rate**.
- **Monetary base (M0) vs broad money (M2)**.
- **Keynesian vs monetarist views of fiscal effectiveness**: Keynesians see strong effects below potential output; monetarists see only temporary effects.

## Mental Models
- **Stance = policy rate − neutral rate.** Always read it relative to neutral, not relative to history.
- **Credibility is a policy instrument**: anchored expectations make each rate change more effective.
- **The fiscal multiplier shrinks with leakages** (saving, taxes, imports) and with crowding out.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tightening against a supply shock** (for example an oil spike): inflation eases little and output suffers.
- **Mixing up easing and QE**: QE is used when the policy rate is at its lower bound.
- **Ignoring implementation lags**: late fiscal stimulus can arrive during the recovery and overheat the economy.

## Worked Example
**Fiscal multiplier.** MPC c = 0.8 and tax rate t = 25%.
- Multiplier = 1/[1 − 0.8 × 0.75] = 1/0.4 = **2.5**.
- An extra $10bn of government spending raises GDP by up to **$25bn** (before crowding out).
- Without taxes it would be 1/(1 − 0.8) = 5.

**Neutral rate.** Trend real growth is 1.5% and the inflation target 2%, so neutral = **3.5%**. A policy rate of 5% is **contractionary**.

## Key Takeaways
1. MV = PY. The Fisher effect: nominal = real + expected inflation.
2. The tools are the policy rate (via repo), open-market operations and reserve requirements.
3. Stance = policy rate vs neutral (trend growth + inflation target).
4. Inflation targeting rests on independence, credibility and transparency.
5. Fiscal multiplier = 1/[1 − c(1 − t)]. Debt is sustainable when growth exceeds the real interest rate.

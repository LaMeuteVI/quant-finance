# Chapter 1: The Behavioral Biases of Individuals
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2023, Level III, Reading 1 · Portfolio Management | Michael M. Pompian, CFA

## Core Idea
Individuals (clients, analysts, PMs: "financial market participants", FMPs) deviate systematically from the rational decision maker of traditional finance. The curriculum sorts biases into **cognitive errors** (faulty reasoning, fixable by information and education, so you *moderate* them) and **emotional biases** (impulse and feeling, hard to fix, so you often *adapt* to them). Knowing which bucket a bias belongs to tells you whether to correct the client or to change the portfolio around the client.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- compare and contrast cognitive errors and emotional biases
- discuss the commonly recognized biases (9 cognitive, 6 emotional) and their implications for financial decisions
- identify and evaluate an individual's behavioral biases

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Behavioral Finance Micro (BFMI) vs Macro (BFMA)**: BFMI studies biases that separate individual investors from the rational agent; BFMA studies market anomalies that separate markets from efficient markets. This reading is BFMI only.
- **Cognitive vs emotional classification**: cognitive = statistical, information-processing or memory errors ("blind spots"); emotional = reasoning influenced by feelings, arising spontaneously.
  - When to use: first step when diagnosing any client behavior.
  - How: ask "is this a reasoning error that better data/education could fix, or a feeling the client cannot switch off?" Cognitive means moderate. Emotional means adapt (accept the bias, adjust the plan or portfolio).
  - Failure mode: some biases mix both (overconfidence is classed emotional although it has cognitive aspects). The classification is by the dominant source. Telling a client he is "biased" about an emotional bias triggers defensiveness; ask neutral questions instead.
- **Moderate vs adapt**: *moderate* = recognize the bias and reduce or eliminate it in the individual; *adapt* = accept it and make decisions that recognize and adjust for it.
  - Exception to remember: endowment bias is emotional, but when financial goals are in jeopardy the attachment must be moderated, not adapted to.
- **Belief perseverance vs information-processing (cognitive sub-types)**: belief perseverance = clinging to prior beliefs (rooted in cognitive dissonance: selective exposure, perception, retention). Information-processing = information is used illogically, with less memory/probability-updating involvement.
- **Bayesian lens**: conservatism = overweights the prior (base rate), under-reacts to new information. Representativeness = underweights the base rate, over-reacts to new information.
- **Detect-and-mitigate routine** (the reading's template per bias): description and evidence, consequences, then detection statements/questions and guidelines. Detection gauges presence or absence, not magnitude.

## Key Concepts
- **Cognitive dissonance**: mental discomfort when new information conflicts with held beliefs; the root of belief perseverance biases.
- **Cognitive cost**: effort to process new information; abstract/statistical information has high cost and gets under-weighted, easy information gets over-weighted.
- **Conservatism bias**: maintain prior views by inadequately incorporating new information (under-reaction).
- **Confirmation bias**: look for and notice what confirms beliefs, ignore or undervalue what contradicts (a selection bias).
- **Representativeness bias**: classify new information by past categories ("best fit"); includes **base-rate neglect** (ignore the prior probability of the category) and **sample-size neglect / law of small numbers** (treat a small sample as representative of the population).
- **Illusion of control bias**: belief that one can control or influence outcomes that are in fact chance.
- **Hindsight bias**: seeing past events as predictable and remembering one's own forecasts as more accurate than they were (selective perception and retention).
- **Anchoring and adjustment**: start from an arbitrary anchor (purchase price, last year's level) and adjust insufficiently.
- **Mental accounting**: treating equal sums differently by source or intended use (non-fungible buckets); drives layered "behavioral portfolio theory" pyramids.
- **Framing bias**: answers differ with how the question is posed (gain vs loss frame; **narrow framing** = focusing on one or two attributes).
- **Availability bias**: probability judged by ease of recall. Four sources: retrievability, categorization, narrow range of experience, resonance.
- **Loss aversion / disposition effect**: losses weigh more than equal gains; hold losers too long, sell winners too soon. **House money effect**: more risk-taking with recent gains.
- **Myopic loss aversion**: loss aversion plus frequent evaluation (short horizon) plus separate mental accounts; offered as an explanation of the equity premium puzzle.
- **Overconfidence**: prediction overconfidence (confidence intervals too narrow) and certainty overconfidence (probabilities too high); amplified by **self-attribution bias** (self-enhancing: credit for wins; self-protecting: deny blame for losses).
- **Self-control bias / hyperbolic discounting**: preferring small payoffs now to larger later; under-saving, then reaching for risk to catch up.
- **Status quo, endowment, regret aversion**: inertia or default (status quo); owning raises perceived value (endowment); avoiding the pain of regret, with errors of commission hurting more than errors of omission (regret aversion, feeds herding).

### Cognitive errors

| Bias | Type | Key consequences | Detection / mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservatism | Belief perseverance | Slow to update; hold security too long; avoid complex data | Ask "how does this change my forecast?"; analyze, then act decisively; get professional help interpreting hard data |
| Confirmation | Belief perseverance | Only positive info on holdings; flawed screens; under-diversification; employer-stock overweight | Actively seek disconfirming evidence; corroborate screen signals (e.g., 52-week highs) with fundamentals |
| Representativeness (base-rate, sample-size neglect) | Belief perseverance | Chase recent returns; high manager turnover; reclassify instead of analyzing | Ask "probability X belongs to Group A vs the group it statistically belongs to?"; periodic table of returns; peer/tenure/style-drift checks; stick to asset allocation |
| Illusion of control | Belief perseverance | Excess trading; concentrated positions (own employer) | Treat investing as probabilistic; seek contrary views; keep trade records with rationale |
| Hindsight | Belief perseverance | False confidence; excess risk; unfair manager assessment | Record and review decisions (good and bad); benchmark/peer evaluation; remember cycles |
| Anchoring and adjustment | Info processing | Cling to purchase price, old EPS estimate or an economy's "state" | Ask "is this rational analysis or an anchor?"; past prices carry little information; scrutinize analysts' estimates for anchoring |
| Mental accounting | Info processing | Ignore correlations across buckets; income vs capital-gain split; chase yield and erode principal | Combine all assets into one view; focus on total return |
| Framing | Info processing | Risk tolerance mis-measured; suboptimal picks; short-term focus | Ask "gain or loss frame?"; drop gains/losses already incurred; present neutrally and in several frames |
| Availability | Info processing | Advertising-driven choices; narrow opportunity set; no diversification | Written investment policy; research before acting; long-term focus; probe where each idea came from |

### Emotional biases

| Bias | Key consequences | Detection / mitigation | Moderate or adapt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss aversion | Hold losers, sell winners (disposition effect); excess turnover; riskier-than-intended portfolio; myopic under-allocation to equities | Fundamental analysis; realistic probabilities of gain/loss; longer evaluation horizon | Adapt (losses cannot be made less painful) |
| Overconfidence | Underestimate risk, narrow ranges, under-diversify, over-trade, lag the market | Review trade records over 2+ years, log winners AND losers, post-mortems separating skill from luck, "I will do X" rules | Hard to moderate; use data and process |
| Self-control | Under-saving; later excessive risk; income-chasing allocation | Written plan, budget, saving plan, appropriate asset allocation | Adapt (commitment devices, automation) |
| Status quo | Inappropriate risk persists; no exploration; defaults rule (opt-out plans beat opt-in) | Education; quantify benefits of diversification; show concentrated-stock downside | Adapt (use defaults) |
| Endowment | Hold inherited/familiar assets; wrong asset allocation | "If this were cash, how would you invest it?"; explore the decedent's intent; small first purchases of unfamiliar assets | Moderate when goals are at risk |
| Regret aversion | Too conservative after losses; stay out of the market; herding | Education on diversification and efficient frontier; losses happen to everyone | Adapt/educate |

Related facts: DALBAR evidence that investors buy after rapid appreciation and sell after declines; Barber and Odean (2001): the average investor trailed the market by about 2 percent a year after costs; the least-active quintile (about 1 percent turnover) earned 17.5 percent vs 16.9 percent for the S&P, the most active quintile earned about 10 percent pre-tax.

## Mental Models
- **Moderate the head, adapt around the heart**: cognitive errors yield to better information; emotional biases yield to portfolio design (defaults, plans, rules).
- **Ask questions, do not accuse**: for emotional biases, a neutral question ("if this were cash, what would you buy?") opens the door; naming the bias provokes defensiveness.
- **Hold the base rate**: before accepting a vivid story, ask which group the case more probably belongs to statistically (guard against both representativeness and its mirror, conservatism).
- **Total-return, whole-portfolio view**: collapse every mental bucket into one spreadsheet; judge assets by marginal contribution to total risk/return, not by the label of the account.
- **Process over outcome**: record the rationale before the trade so hindsight and self-attribution cannot rewrite it; separate luck from skill afterwards.
- **Evaluation frequency is a risk setting**: the more often a loss-averse client looks, the more losses they see and the lower the equity allocation they will tolerate.

## Worked Example
**1. Framing in a risk questionnaire (reading's Example 3, recomputed).** Portfolio ABC: mean 10 percent, σ 16 percent. Using μ ± kσ: 1σ gives -6 to 26 percent, 2σ gives -22 to 42 percent, 3σ gives -38 to 58 percent. Question 1 shows 95 percent ranges: XYZ 0.5 to 6.5 (σ 1.5, mean 3.5: 3.5 ± 3), DEF -18 to 30 (mean 6, σ 12), ABC -22 to 42. Question 2 shows the same portfolios as mean and σ only, return first. Same data, but a client who balks at "-22 percent" in Q1 may pick XYZ and choose DEF or ABC in Q2. Interpretation: risk tolerance scoring depends on presentation, so show both frames and order effects, and do not size a portfolio off one questionnaire.

**2. Myopic loss aversion (reading's setup: risky asset 7 percent expected, σ 20 percent, safe asset 1 percent).** Probability of a negative cumulative arithmetic return, `Φ(-μT / (σ√T))`: 1 year 36.3 percent, 5 years 21.7 percent, 10 years 13.4 percent, 30 years 2.8 percent (normal approximation, iid). An investor who checks annually sees a loss about one year in three, so with loss aversion weighting losses more heavily he demands a higher premium or holds fewer equities. Advising a longer evaluation horizon (or showing 30-year compound returns) raises the equity allocation the same client will accept.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating all biases the same**: lecturing a client out of an emotional bias; it breeds defensiveness. Adapt the plan instead.
- **Judging a manager on 1 to 3 years**: sample-size neglect; luck and skill are inseparable over such windows.
- **Return chasing and hot-fund switching**: representativeness plus availability; costs and non-persistence cut realized returns (DALBAR).
- **Anchoring on purchase price or old forecasts**: the market does not know your cost basis; the decision is forward-looking.
- **Bucket investing without correlations**: offsetting positions in layers leave the aggregate suboptimal; cash and yield chasing leak total return.
- **Using income as a spendable-only return**: erodes principal, especially via high-yield instruments.
- **Selling winners and holding losers for tax-free "break-even"**: disposition effect; risk goes up, upside is cut.
- **Concentrated employer stock**: combines illusion of control, confirmation, familiarity; a job loss and a price loss arrive together.
- **Questionnaire as the sole risk-tolerance measure**: framing bias.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- **Classification traps**: representativeness, conservatism, confirmation, illusion of control and hindsight are belief perseverance. Anchoring, mental accounting, framing, availability are information processing. Overconfidence, self-control, status quo, endowment, regret aversion, loss aversion are emotional. Overconfidence has cognitive aspects but is emotional.
- **Conservatism vs representativeness vs anchoring**: conservatism overweights the prior (under-reaction); representativeness overweights new information and its category (over-reaction); anchoring overweights a numeric starting point.
- **Status quo vs endowment vs regret aversion**: same outcome (no change), different reason: inertia, no conscious choice (status quo); ownership adds value (endowment); fear of regret from acting (regret aversion).
- **Regret**: error of commission hurts more than error of omission, so inaction is preferred; regret aversion can cause herding and either excessive conservatism or excessive risk depending on the trend.
- **Loss aversion implies risk-seeking in the loss domain and risk-averse in the gain domain** (S-shaped, asymmetric value function around a reference point). House money effect is the exception: after gains some take more risk.
- **Mitigation answers**: cognitive = education, information, process, records; emotional = adapt, neutral questions, plan and defaults. Exception: endowment with goals at risk means moderate.
- **Self-attribution** is a component of overconfidence, with self-enhancing and self-protecting subtypes.
- Detection tests only gauge presence/absence, not magnitude.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify every observed behavior as cognitive (moderate) or emotional (adapt) before choosing a remedy.
2. For cognitive errors, add information, structure and written records (decision logs, base-rate questions).
3. For emotional biases, change the environment: written IPS, budgets, default enrolment, small staged purchases, and non-threatening questions.
4. Show clients the periodic table of returns and total-portfolio view to defeat return chasing and mental accounting.
5. Measure risk tolerance in several frames and judge managers over full cycles against fitting benchmarks.
6. Lengthen the evaluation horizon for loss-averse clients; frequent looking lowers risk-taking and returns.
7. Review at least two years of your own trades, losers included, to calibrate overconfidence.
8. Diversify away from employer stock and inherited concentrations with a cash-equivalent test.

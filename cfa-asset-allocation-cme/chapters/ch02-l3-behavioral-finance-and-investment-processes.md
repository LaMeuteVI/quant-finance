# Chapter 2: Behavioral Finance and Investment Processes
> **Source**: CFA Program Curriculum 2023, Level III, Reading 2 · Portfolio Management | Michael M. Pompian, CFA; Colin McLean; Alistair Byrne

## Core Idea
Behavioral finance is applied at four levels: the adviser-client relationship (investor types, IPS, risk questionnaires), portfolio construction (inertia, 1/n, employer stock, home bias, layered portfolios), the research and decision process (analyst forecasts, management framing, committees), and the market (momentum, bubbles, value/growth). The practical aim is a portfolio closer to the efficient portfolio of traditional finance that the client can actually stick to, and a research process hardened against overconfidence and groupthink.

## Learning Outcomes Covered
- explain uses and limitations of classifying investors into personality types
- discuss how behavioral factors affect adviser-client interactions
- discuss how behavioral factors influence portfolio construction; apply behavioral finance to portfolio construction
- discuss how behavioral factors affect analyst forecasts; recommend remedial actions
- discuss how behavioral factors affect investment committee decisions; recommend mitigation techniques
- describe how investor biases lead to market characteristics not explained by traditional finance

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Barnewall two-way model**: passive investors (became wealthy passively, need for security exceeds risk tolerance; executives, professionals, inheritors, small-resource investors) vs active investors (risked own capital, risk tolerance exceeds need for security, want control; their risk tolerance drops fast when they lose control).
  - When to use: quick biographic read of a client's career and wealth origin.
- **Bailard, Biehl and Kaiser (BB&K) five-way model**: two axes, confident-anxious and careful-impetuous, giving Adventurer (confident, impetuous, undiversified, resists advice), Celebrity (impetuous, anxious-ish, wants attention, takes advice), Individualist (confident, careful, decides after analysis, easy to advise), Guardian (anxious, careful, protects assets, seeks expert advice, often near retirement), Straight Arrow (center, takes balanced risk).
  - Failure mode: clients are not equally confident across life domains; judge the investing approach specifically.
- **Behavioral Investor Types (BITs) and the Behavioral Alpha (BA) top-down process (Pompian)**: diagnose the dominant bias group first, instead of testing every bias (the "bottom-up" method of Pompian and Longo, which is slow).
  - Step 1: interview; decide active vs passive (did the client risk own capital to build wealth?) using the 9-question test (mostly "a" = active/higher risk tolerance, mostly "b" = passive/lower). A traditional questionnaire misses the active/passive trait.
  - Step 2: plot on the passive-to-active and low-to-high risk scale (Exhibit 3). If active/passive and risk disagree, defer to risk tolerance when choosing which biases to test.
  - Step 3: test for the biases tied to the candidate BIT (Exhibit 4).
  - Step 4: classify into a BIT (judgment needed; neighbors share traits), then tailor advice.
  - Why it works: biases cluster at the extremes (emotional) and the middle (cognitive), so the cheap prior is strong.
- **Behavioral portfolio theory (Shefrin and Statman)**: investors build layered pyramids, one layer per goal (downside-protection layer: T-bills, CDs, money market; upside-potential layer: foreign stocks, aggressive growth, IPOs, "lottery tickets"), ignoring covariance between layers. Contrast: mean-variance builds one portfolio, only total variance matters.
  - How to use: map the client's mental accounts and the risk attitude of each in the IPS; investors hold several risk attitudes at once.
- **Analyst remedies list**: prompt accurate feedback, explicit unambiguous (numeric) forecasts, written rationale and data, systematic review, accountability to supervisors or clients, colleague appraisal, a mandatory counterargument, comparable data, base rates, Bayesian sequential updating, structured search for contrary facts.
- **Bayes' formula**: `P(A|B) = [P(B|A) / P(B)] · P(A)` (see Formulas). Use it to combine base rate and analyst hit rate.
- **Committee design rules (Wood 2006)**: build diversity of background, keep members independent, have the chair collect views in advance and invite dissent, enforce the agenda and a clear decision, track past decisions to get feedback, protect contrarians' self-esteem.

## Key Concepts
- **Psychographic classification**: classifying investors by personality, values, attitudes, not demographics; it guides likely biases, not exact diagnosis.
- **Passive Preserver (PP)**: passive, low risk tolerance, conservative; mainly emotional biases (endowment, loss aversion, status quo, regret aversion) plus anchoring and mental accounting; wants "big picture" goals advice.
- **Friendly Follower (FF)**: passive, low to medium risk; follows friends and advisers into popular ideas; overestimates risk tolerance; cognitive biases (availability, hindsight, framing) plus regret-driven herding; needs education and data.
- **Independent Individualist (II)**: active, medium to high risk; strong-willed, contrarian, trusts gut; conservatism, availability, confirmation, representativeness; some overconfidence and self-attribution; advise with regular educational discussion, respect their intelligence.
- **Active Accumulator (AA)**: active, high risk, first-generation wealth, entrepreneurial; overconfidence, self-control, illusion of control; high turnover; adviser must take control of the engagement.
- **Inertia and default**: DC participants rarely change allocations and keep default funds (often cash/money market); target date funds are an autopilot but one-size-fits-all.
- **Naive (1/n) diversification**: splitting contributions equally across the funds on offer; mix of options (framing) drives equity share. Conditional 1/n: equal split over a chosen subset (Huberman and Jiang).
- **Company stock / familiarity**: employer stock overweight from familiarity, overconfidence, extrapolation of past returns, framing of the match, loyalty, sometimes real financial incentives.
- **Home bias**: often 80 percent or more in domestic securities; behavioral roots: availability, confirmation, illusion of control, endowment, status quo, familiarity.
- **Social proof**: following the group's beliefs; amplifies individual biases in research teams and committees.
- **Calibration**: match of stated confidence to actual hit rate; 90 percent intervals are wrong up to 40 percent of the time (Russo and Schoemaker).
- **Conjunction fallacy**: judging a joint event as more likely than a component; for independent events `P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)`, never above either.
- **Gambler's fallacy vs hot hand fallacy**: expecting reversion to the mean vs projecting the recent trend; both ignore statistical independence.
- **Anomaly**: persistent, direction-predictable abnormal return; may be risk compensation, data mining, survivorship, or small-sample artifact (Super Bowl indicator). Fama: if a reasonable change of method removes it, it is an illusion.
- **Momentum / herding / recency**: positive return correlation up to about 2 years, reversal at 2 to 5 years; herding = trading the same side or ignoring private information; recency = availability applied to recent data.
- **Bubble / crash**: price more than 2 standard deviations from trend (can exceed 10 percent of observations vs 5 percent expected under normality); bubbles build slowly, crashes are fast (30 percent plus in months).
- **Halo effect**: extending a favorable view of some traits (growth record) to others (low risk, high expected return).

## Formulas & Reference Tables
`P(A|B) = [P(B|A) / P(B)] · P(A)` where P(A) = prior (base rate), P(B|A) = probability of the evidence given A, P(B) = unconditional probability of the evidence, P(A|B) = updated probability.
Total probability of the signal: `P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) + P(B|¬A)·P(¬A)`.
Joint probability of independent events: `P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B)`.
Anomaly/bubble band: price index beyond `trend ± 2σ`.

| | Passive Preserver (PP) | Friendly Follower (FF) | Independent Individualist (II) | Active Accumulator (AA) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Passive | Passive | Active | Active |
| Risk tolerance | Low | Low to medium | Medium to high | High |
| Style | Conservative | Moderate | Growth | Aggressive |
| Dominant bias | Emotional | Cognitive | Cognitive | Emotional |
| Emotional biases | Endowment, loss aversion, status quo, regret aversion | Regret aversion (herding) | Overconfidence, self-attribution | Overconfidence, self-control |
| Cognitive biases | Mental accounting, anchoring | Availability, hindsight, framing | Conservatism, availability, confirmation, representativeness | Illusion of control |
| How to advise | Big picture, goals, family; build trust first | Education on diversification, data-backed, caution on "hot" ideas | Regular education, respect their intelligence | Take control of the engagement; prove objectivity; watch spending |

### Evidence numbers worth remembering
| Fact | Number |
|---|---|
| Employer stock share of 401(k) assets (Benartzi 2001) | about one-third; more than 90 percent in some plans |
| Equity allocation: plan with 5 stock + 1 bond fund vs 1 stock + 4 bond funds | 75 percent vs 34 percent |
| Own contributions to employer stock: match free choice vs match in stock | 18 percent vs 29 percent |
| Past 10-yr best vs worst quintile employer-stock allocation | 40 percent vs 10 percent |
| Winners vs losers sold in a month (Barber and Odean) | winners 1.5 times more likely |
| Stocks sold minus stocks bought, next 12 months | sold stocks do about 3.5 percent better |
| Momentum (UK 52 years to 2007) | winners 18.3 percent, losers 6.8 percent, market 13.5 percent a year |
| Value beats growth (Fama-French 1975-1995) | 12 of 13 markets; small beats large in 11 of 16 |

## Mental Models
- **Diagnose cheap, then confirm**: start with active/passive and risk tolerance; those predict which bias family to test. Do not run a 40-bias battery first.
- **Match the message to the bias family**: emotional clients get goals and security stories; cognitive clients get numbers and education.
- **Treat questionnaires as ranges, not answers**: wording changes results, they are one-time snapshots, and clients mean "max loss I can stand" literally less than advisers do; set the portfolio so the stated maximum tolerable loss is not reachable.
- **Default is destiny**: design the choice architecture (auto-enroll, glide path, sensible default fund) because inertia dominates active choice.
- **A strong base rate needs strong discrimination to overrule it**: before acting against the prior, ask how good the signal source really is.
- **Crowd is not committee**: diversity plus independent voting gives wisdom; similar people debating under a powerful chair gives conformity.
- **Anomaly triage**: before trading an anomaly check risk compensation, benchmark choice, data mining, survivorship, costs, out-of-sample persistence, and whether publication has arbitraged it away.

## Worked Example
**Analyst switch recommendation with Bayes (reading's Example 3, recomputed).** Two integrated oil stocks, 3-year correlation 88 percent. The CIO puts the base rate that switching costs (4 percent after tax) exceed the return advantage at 80 percent, so `P(A) = P(switch worthwhile) = 0.20`. The analyst is right 60 percent of the time: `P(B|A) = 0.60` (hit) and false-alarm rate 40 percent.
Natural frequencies per 1,000 cases: worthwhile = 200, not worthwhile = 800. Recommend switch when worthwhile: 0.6 × 200 = 120 (true positives). Recommend switch when not worthwhile: 0.4 × 800 = 320 (false positives). `P(B) = 440/1000 = 0.44`.
`P(A|B) = (0.60/0.44) × 0.20 = 0.2727`, about 27 percent (checked: 120/440 = 0.2727).
Interpretation: only about 1 in 4 "switch" calls is worth making, so decline unless the analyst can show a clearly better discrimination ability or a magnitude estimate that beats the 4 percent cost.

**Conjunction fallacy in market timing (reading's Example 4).** A manager tells a charity to wait because a September fall (probability 0.55) is followed by a 12-month rise (probability 0.7). If independent, the combined scenario has probability 0.55 × 0.70 = 0.385, lower than either piece. The vivid story (reversal after six hot months = gambler's fallacy; compelling narrative = representativeness; extra detail seen as support = confirmation) adds confidence while lowering probability. Better advice: phase the investment over weeks or months.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming a client fits one box**: people show several BIT traits, change with age (risk tolerance usually falls), and act differently under stress.
- **Reading a risk questionnaire literally**: a 20 percent "tolerable loss" does not mean a portfolio that can lose 20 percent.
- **Mean-variance output without bias screening**: the client later forces allocation changes after short-term swings; moving in and out costs more than the optimization gained.
- **Gathering more data to feel certain**: irrelevant data raises confidence, not accuracy (illusion of knowledge and control).
- **Stories as forecasts**: vivid scenario chains signal representativeness and availability; real research includes abstract unknowns and a "however".
- **Complex over-fit models**: fit the sample, fail out of sample; spurious precision hides weak assumptions.
- **Accepting management's framing**: opening summaries, recalculated (non-GAAP) earnings and optimistic language anchor the analyst (the RBS 2007 report counted 78 "strong" and 0 "bad" shortly before rescue).
- **Committee by consensus with a dominant chair**: members "chime in"; no tracking of past decisions means no learning.
- **Trading published anomalies without cost, risk and out-of-sample checks**.
- **Documenting reasons too elaborately for intuition-based judgment**: written explanations can crowd out intuition; record enough to evaluate later, no more.

## Exam Focus & Traps
- **BIT mapping**: PP = passive, low risk, emotional; FF = passive, low-medium, cognitive; II = active, medium-high, cognitive; AA = active, high, emotional. Biases are emotional at the extremes, cognitive in the middle.
- **Which advice fits which type**: PP "big picture" without Sharpe ratios; FF education and diversification; II educate regularly; AA the adviser takes control. A question asking what Wang is "least likely" to do usually targets quantitative detail for emotional types or limiting an AA's role (taking control is right).
- **Risk questionnaires** work better for institutions (cognitive risk analysis) than individuals (emotional); they fail emotionally biased individuals.
- **Top-down (behavioral alpha) vs bottom-up**: top-down is the shortcut; bottom-up tests all biases.
- **Home bias, inertia and default, naive diversification, employer stock, excessive trading** are separate DC-plan findings; know the cause of each (status quo, framing/1/n heuristic, familiarity and extrapolation, overconfidence and disposition effect).
- **DC inertia vs brokerage over-trading** contrast comes from self-selection and restricted choice.
- **Remedies**: for overconfidence use prompt feedback plus accountability and a counterargument; for hindsight use written, numeric, unambiguous forecasts; for confirmation use structured search for contrary evidence.
- **Social proof** is the committee/group bias; "crowds vs committees".
- **Anomalies**: Fama's test (changes with method means illusion); Super Bowl indicator as data-mining; momentum has short-term underreaction and long-term overreaction; value effect is risk (Fama-French three-factor) or mispricing (halo, representativeness, overconfidence) depending on view; popular stocks earn low returns (affect: investors treat risk and return as negatively correlated).
- Bubble definition: more than 2 standard deviations off trend; crash: 30 percent or more drop in months; bubbles slow, crashes quick.
- Self-attribution can be fake: misdirected incentives produce the same skew without the psychology.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify clients with the behavioral alpha top-down sequence: active/passive, risk tolerance, bias test, BIT.
2. Tune communication: stories and goals for emotional types, data and education for cognitive types.
3. Put behavioral factors into the IPS and revisit it at least annually; questionnaires give ranges only.
4. Use defaults and glide paths against inertia, and check naive 1/n, employer-stock and home bias in every client portfolio.
5. Map the client's mental-account layers and their separate risk attitudes, then show the combined portfolio.
6. Force analysts to give numeric, dated forecasts with a counterargument, base rates, and timely feedback.
7. Treat management framing, recalculated earnings and glowing language as anchors to discount.
8. Build committees for diversity and independence, collect views before discussion, and record decisions to learn from them.
9. Test an anomaly for risk, benchmark, data mining, costs and persistence before building a strategy on it.
